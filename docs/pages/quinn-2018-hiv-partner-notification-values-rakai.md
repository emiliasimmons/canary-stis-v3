> Note: summary derived from the abstract only, not the full source.

Qualitative study of HIV partner-notification preferences in Rakai, Uganda, run through 6 focus group discussions and 63 in-depth interviews across high-prevalence fishing communities and low-prevalence mainland communities. Participants were fishermen and sex workers in the fishing communities, male and female mainland community members, and healthcare providers. The interviews probed three notification approaches: passive referral (the index patient tells the partner), provider referral (a health worker notifies), and contract referral (the patient agrees to notify within a set period, after which a provider steps in). Analysis used a team-based matrix method.

## Relevance to project

Partner notification is outside the modelled POC and SOC arms, which switch etiological testing for syndromic management at the same care-seeking door and add no tracing. This source is therefore a design reference rather than a model input: it says which notification modes people in the modelled setting would accept, should a partner-notification arm ever be added.

The operative distinction is by partnership type. Passive referral was preferred for primary partners across all groups. Provider referral was acceptable in fishing communities specifically for reaching multiple casual partners, the partnerships that matter most for onward transmission in a risk-structured network. Providers themselves flagged limited time, resources, and training as the binding constraint on any provider-assisted approach, which bounds the coverage such an arm could realistically assume.

No prevalence, incidence, notification-cascade, or coverage number is available to use as a target or prior.

## Key points

Passive referral was judged most suitable for primary partners.

Provider referral was acceptable in fishing communities for notifying multiple, casual partners, but not framed as the default for primary partners.

Healthcare providers raised limited time, resources, and training as concerns about provider-assisted approaches.

The authors position optional notification modes as a way to overcome barriers to serostatus disclosure and to reach key populations.

## Limitations

Abstract only: methods detail, sampling frame beyond the group labels, and any quantitative coding results were not read. Stated preferences for hypothetical notification approaches, which overstate what people would do in practice, and no linkage or coverage outcome is measured.
