A four-page editorial comment in *AIDS* (2001) written around the Carletonville youth survey published in the same issue. It gathers community-based prevalence data from South Africa, Kenya, Zimbabwe, Zambia and Tanzania to argue that the sex gap in HIV among 15-24-year-olds is real rather than a survey artefact, and that its main driver is young women partnering with older, already-exposed men.

## Relevance to project

Context and a structural justification for age-disparate mixing in the risk network, not a calibration target. The population is east and southern African community surveys around 1997-2000, not Rakai, and the numbers are quoted second-hand from other papers, so they are not defensible as fitting targets. What the editorial supports is a modelling choice: an age-mixing kernel where women aged 15-19 draw partners from substantially older male ages, with a large enough tail that roughly one in five 15-19-year-old women has a partner 10+ years older (17% in Kisesa, Tanzania; >20% in rural Zimbabwe). It also motivates a female-biased per-act HIV transmission probability, with male-to-female 2-3 times female-to-male in the absence of cofactors, and an extra increment at young ages.

For the STI cofactor pathway the editorial supplies the qualitative claim the project rests on, that untreated asymptomatic gonorrhoea and chlamydia are more common in young women than young men and add to HIV susceptibility, but no effect size the model could adopt. The one adjusted cofactor number it carries is for HSV-2, which the model does not include.

## Key points

Carletonville, South Africa, population-based survey of 14-24-year-olds: HIV prevalence 9% in men, 34% in women; 67% among women aged 24.

Across the community studies compiled in Fig. 1, two to eight times more women than men were HIV positive in both the 15-19 and 20-24 bands, urban and rural alike, at every level of overall prevalence. Male prevalence catches up with female in the early to mid-30s. Kisumu, Kenya, 1997: 30% of women aged 15-49 infected versus 20% of men.

Sexual activity does not explain the gap. WHO Global Programme on AIDS surveys across the region found boys starting sex at the same age or slightly later than girls and reporting more pre-marital partners, yet without the corresponding rise in prevalence.

HSV-2 seropositivity was the strongest independent risk factor for HIV in Carletonville, odds ratio 9.8 (95% CI 5.8-16.6) in women and 5.3 (2.7-10.3) in men.

The authors reject the "small core of highly active infected men" explanation, noting that studies with detailed behavioural data (Buvé et al.) did not confirm it, and favour age-disparate partnering instead.

## Limitations

An editorial, so no new data and no methods of its own; every figure is quoted from another study, mostly with the qualifier of its own sampling frame left behind. The prevalence comparisons are cross-sectional and cannot separate incidence from differential survival. Non-response is acknowledged as higher in men and only argued away, not adjusted for. The claim that reproductive tract maturation reduces susceptibility with age is stated with the authors' own note that quantitative evidence is lacking. Nothing here is Uganda-specific except a single citation on delayed sexual debut and falling prevalence among young urban pregnant women.
