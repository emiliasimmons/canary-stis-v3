A PRISMA-ScR scoping review of preferences for long-acting and on-demand PrEP among potential
users in Eastern and Southern Africa (ESA), covering studies published 2014-2024. Of 349
unique citations, 49 met eligibility (original research, peer-reviewed or grey, conducted in
ESA, comparing at least one long-acting or on-demand product against daily oral PrEP).

Products covered: long-acting injectable (LAI, cabotegravir-like bimonthly), the intravaginal
dapivirine ring (DVR), a six-month implant still in development, and oral on-demand (event
driven) dosing. Almost all figures are stated preferences over hypothetical products, since
cabotegravir was the only LAI available and half the LAI studies predate its approval.

## Relevance to project

Context for the prevention-targeting pathway, not a parameter source. The project's POC arm
identifies STI-positive people at the symptomatic care-seeking door and can route them to
prevention; this review says what those people would accept if offered PrEP, and the answer
is that the modality matters as much as the offer. Nothing here is a calibration target: no
study measures uptake, coverage, or persistence, only ranked preference among hypothetical
products.

Usable as a soft bound on any PrEP-uptake assumption stratified by product. Across
subpopulations, LAI is the modal first choice: 62% of AGYW in the TRIO study (South Africa
and Kenya, n=249) versus 15% oral pills, 12% DVR, 10% condoms; 75% of adults in an Eswatini
DCE (n=109) preferred a two-month LAI over daily oral; 48% of urban heterosexual men in South
Africa (n=178) preferred LAI versus 33% oral and 20% condoms alone; 63% of women in a
multipurpose-technology RCT (South Africa and Kenya, n=214) ranked bimonthly injection first
after using placebo forms of all three. A model that assumes uniform PrEP acceptance across
products would overstate coverage for the DVR and understate it for injectables.

Two findings bear on the STI side specifically. Interest in injectable PrEP among AGYW in
Kampala was higher among those with STIs, and among older AGYW. The review reports the full
ordering in that study as HIV vaccine 34%, oral PrEP 26%, injectable 25%, implant 14%, vaginal
ring 1%. The STI association is the direction the project's targeting pathway assumes. Against that, South
African heterosexual men who rated their likelihood of non-HIV STIs as high were more likely
to prefer condoms over LAI, so a PrEP offer that displaces condom use can raise curable-STI
incidence even while lowering HIV incidence. Two FSW studies report explicit risk
compensation: participants said PrEP protection could let them have condomless sex with
clients, or accept condomless sex at a higher price. If the project ever adds a PrEP arm,
that is the mechanism to represent, and this review is the citation for it.

The Ugandan studies (n=13 in the review, alongside 36 South African) are the closest
geographic match to Rakai, but none are RCCS and none report the age-and-sex-stratified
quantities the calibration needs.

## Key points

- Preference ordering across the review: long-acting products over on-demand, on-demand over
  daily oral, and injectables over implants and rings. The DVR was the least preferred
  long-acting product, still ahead of daily oral pills.
- Three attributes drive choice: duration (dosing frequency), efficacy, and discretion.
  Duration was the single most-cited factor across subpopulations; participants would trade
  their preferred formulation for a longer-acting one.
- Discretion cuts both ways. Daily pills read as antiretrovirals and so as HIV-positive
  status, but an injection mark or a palpable implant reads as PrEP use and so as infidelity.
  Women in several studies wanted a product usable without a partner's knowledge; men in one
  South African focus group set disapproved of their partners using PrEP at all.
- Trial-population preferences track the trial's product. In the ASPIRE DVR trial, 94% of
  women named the ring most preferred; in TRIO, where women used all three formulations,
  only 12% did. Preference measured inside a product's own trial is not transferable.
- On-demand dosing was preferred over daily by 60% of male youth and 65% of youth MSM, with
  preference for on-demand rising when sexual exposure was infrequent.
- Anticipated side effects were the most common concern and exceed the observed ones. About
  half of youth in Malawi said they would not use PrEP if side effects occurred; women
  extrapolated injectable-contraceptive effects (menstruation cessation, weight gain) to
  injectable PrEP, which has not shown them.

## Limitations

- Stated preference over hypothetical products throughout. No observed uptake, no
  persistence, no adherence. Eighteen of 36 LAI studies predate cabotegravir approval and all
  implant studies concern a product still in development.
- Study population is skewed: 36 of 49 studies in South Africa, 44 including women, 24
  focused on women only. Two studies included transgender women, despite HIV incidence up to
  13 times the general population. Western and Central Africa are essentially absent.
- Sixteen studies drew participants from PrEP trials, cohorts, or implementation projects, so
  people who struggle with oral PrEP because of stigma, refill visits, or pill burden, the
  population long-acting products target, are underrepresented.
- A scoping review by design: no pooled estimate, no quality-weighted synthesis, and the
  quality assessment (generalizability, acceptance rate, PrEP experience) was done by a
  single reviewer.
