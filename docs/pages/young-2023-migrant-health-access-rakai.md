Source: [young-2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/heapol/czad019)

A qualitative secondary analysis of 60 semi-structured interviews collected in Rakai and
surrounding south-central Uganda across three rounds (November 2017, February 2019, August-
October 2021): 35 migrants and 25 key informants (15 community health workers, 5 clinic
workers, 3 CHW supervisors, 2 community leaders). Migrants were recruited from the Rakai
Community Cohort Study, restricted to people who had moved into a study community within
the previous two years, and purposively sampled on age, sex, HIV status, and residence. Of
the 31 migrants whose status was recorded, 11 were living with HIV and 10 of those 11 self-
reported being on ART. 21 of 35 were women.

The analysis is thematic, organized around three constructed composite personas ("Mary",
"Peter", "Andrew") that vary on HIV status and job security. There are no rates, no
denominators beyond the interview counts, and no quantitative comparison against long-term
residents.

## Relevance to project

Context for the symptomatic care-seeking door, not a target and not a prior. The model's SOC
and POC arms both hang on a person deciding to present with symptoms, and this source
describes what sits between that decision and an actual consultation in the calibration
setting: transport cost and availability (a facility may not exist in the community at all,
and night transport is worse), out-of-pocket payment at private facilities after public
stockouts, day-long waits driven by staff shortages, and anticipated stigma pushing people
to clinics further from home. It supplies no number to parametrize any of those.

Two things bear directly on how a care-seeking probability should be read. First, the
barriers are largely shared with long-term residents and are amplified rather than created
by migration, so a Rakai-wide care-seeking estimate is not obviously wrong for migrants,
just optimistic. Second, the fraction of the population this qualifier applies to is not
small: nationally, 48% of Ugandan households include a migrant and 42% hosted an internal
migrant (FAO 2017, quoted here). Recent in-migrants are also the group with elevated HIV
acquisition risk in this cohort, which the paper cites rather than measures.

Where it could matter more: if the project ever adds a mobility or in-migration state to the
network, this is the source describing what that state should do to treatment continuity.
The ART transfer-form mechanism is the concrete one. Moving care between facilities requires
a printed transfer form naming the destination clinic, which migrants often cannot obtain in
advance because they do not know where or how long they will stay; without it, health
workers reinitiate them on ART from scratch. The paper cites a Zambian estimate that 17% of
traced individuals made undocumented "silent transfers".

Not usable as a comparator for any intervention arm. It reports no effect estimate, and the
intervention it was collected around (a CHW linkage trial, NCT03915899) is only mentioned.

## Key points

Barriers stratify by wealth and by existing health need rather than by migration alone.
Migrants with job security and money chose private facilities, bought speed, and travelled
far enough that no one locally learned their HIV status. Migrants without either traded
time spent seeking care against time spent seeking work, and deprioritized care.

HIV-negative migrants were unaware of the access problems they would face; HIV-positive
migrants anticipated them. Fear of disclosure was directed at both community members and
health workers, whom interviewees did not trust to keep status confidential, and it
displaced care to more distant clinics.

Informal ART sharing, between friends or through health workers, appeared as a stopgap for
people who could not complete a transfer. Social ties were the dominant facilitator across
all three journeys, and during the 2021 COVID-19 lockdown, when curfews blocked movement
between communities, CHWs delivering ART locally were in some cases the only route to
treatment.

Local stereotyping of migrants as thieves or as people who "move to our community to infect
us with diseases" was reported by key informants as a barrier to integration.

## Limitations

Interviews were in Luganda or English, excluding other-language and international migrants,
and coding was done in English. Data saturation was not reached for several themes, stigma
in particular, because the interviews were not originally collected to answer this question.
The personas are illustrative composites, so nothing in them is a frequency. Key informants
were not given a definition of "migrant", so their accounts describe whoever they took that
to mean. Highly mobile migrants were excluded by the two-year residence sampling frame,
which is the group whose care interruption would be worst.

Period effects are large and unmodelled: 2017 and 2021 both coincided with the Ugandan
fishing restrictions on Lake Victoria, and the 2021 round ran entirely under COVID-19
lockdown with distanced, masked interviewing. Barriers reported in those rounds are likely
worse than steady state.
