Review of the evidence for treating STIs to prevent HIV transmission, covering observational
data, all nine RCTs conducted through 2010, and explanations for why trial results diverged.

## Relevance to project

The central framing paper for what this project tests. Hayes et al. lay out the
evidence base that STI cofactors enhance HIV acquisition and transmission, then
show that only one of nine trials (Mwanza) found a significant effect on HIV
incidence. Their explanation for the divergence (epidemic phase, STI prevalence,
intervention potency, control-arm contamination) is the starting point for the
canary-stis model design: the model asks whether a more potent intervention (POC
etiological diagnosis replacing syndromic management) changes the calculus in a
setting where the earlier trials saw no effect.

The paper also names point-of-care STI diagnostics as one of the key unresolved
research priorities, making it a direct ancestor of the question this project
answers.

Not a source of calibration targets. The quantitative estimates it reports
(cofactor relative risks, trial effect sizes) are meta-analytic summaries drawn
from the underlying studies, which should be cited individually when used as
priors or bounds.

## Key points

Observational studies consistently show STIs increase HIV risk. A meta-analysis
of 31 longitudinal studies found a 4-fold increased risk of HIV with any
laboratory-documented STI (aOR 3.9, 95% CI 2.8-5.3). Genital ulcer disease
carried higher risk (aOR 2.8) than non-ulcerative STIs (aOR 1.7). These
per-follow-up-period estimates understate the per-contact cofactor effect.

Of nine RCTs, only the Mwanza trial (improved syndromic management at health
units in Tanzania) found a significant reduction in HIV incidence: 42% (RR 0.58,
95% CI 0.42-0.79). The other eight showed no significant effect.

Four factors explain the divergence across the four community-randomised trials:

1. **Epidemic phase.** Mwanza had an expanding epidemic (prevalence:incidence
   ratio 4.6). The other three sites (Rakai, Masaka, Manicaland) had mature
   epidemics (ratios 10-15). In mature epidemics, much transmission occurs in
   stable partnerships where curable STIs are less common and cumulative HIV
   transmission probability is already high.
2. **Baseline STI prevalence.** High-titre active syphilis at baseline was
   5-6% in Mwanza versus 1-2% in Rakai and under 1% in Masaka. Risk behaviour
   was also higher in Mwanza.
3. **Control-arm services.** Mwanza's control arm had poor-quality existing
   services. The other trials provided syndromic management, condom promotion,
   or periodic STI treatment in both arms.
4. **Intervention strategy.** Mathematical modelling showed the differences in
   intervention approach (syndromic management vs. mass treatment) could not
   explain the contrasting results on their own.

The two individually-randomised trials among sex workers (Abidjan, Nairobi) were
underpowered for HIV incidence. The Nairobi trial reduced STI incidence but not
HIV, with only 35 seroconversions observed against a power calculation assuming
15% incidence (actual 3-4%).

Three herpes suppressive therapy trials (acyclovir 400 mg twice daily) showed no
effect on HIV acquisition or transmission despite reducing genital ulcers. The
explanation: HSV-2 reactivation recruits HIV target cells (CCR5+ CD4 T cells,
dendritic cells) that persist for weeks after ulcer healing and are not cleared
by standard-dose acyclovir.

The paper concludes that the basic concept (STI treatment reduces HIV
transmission) remains sound. The negative trials tested insufficiently potent
interventions, enrolled populations with low STI prevalence, or had design
limitations. Future priorities include elucidating STI-HIV biological mechanisms,
developing point-of-care STI diagnostics, and evaluating alternative partner
notification approaches.

## Limitations

This is a narrative review, not a systematic review with defined inclusion
criteria or quality assessment. The explanatory framework for trial divergence is
post-hoc and qualitative, though supported by the mathematical modelling of
White et al. (2004, 2008) and Korenromp et al. (2005). The per-contact cofactor
magnitudes that would matter most for an agent-based model are acknowledged as
unknown; the review reports only per-follow-up-period estimates.

## Methodology

Summarises evidence across three study designs: observational cohorts and
meta-analyses of cofactor effects, four community-randomised trials of
population-level STI treatment, and five individually-randomised trials
(two among sex workers for curable STIs, three for HSV-2 suppression). The
synthesis across trial designs is the paper's contribution; it does not generate
new data.
