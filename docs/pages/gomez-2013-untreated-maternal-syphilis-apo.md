Source: [gomez-2013](https://doi.org/10.2471/BLT.12.107623)

Systematic review and meta-analysis, commissioned in support of the WHO congenital syphilis elimination initiative, pooling adverse pregnancy outcomes in women seroreactive for *Treponema pallidum* and untreated against women who were not seroreactive. Of 3258 citations screened, six studies met inclusion, spanning the United Kingdom (1917), the United States (1940s), Zambia (1990), Malawi (1987-90) and Tanzania (1998-2000).

## Key points

- Any adverse pregnancy outcome occurred in 66.5% (95% CI 58.0-74.1) of 1715 pregnancies among untreated seroreactive women, against 14.3% (95% CI 11.8-17.2) of 22515 pregnancies among non-seroreactive women. Study-level estimates ranged 53.4-81.8% and 10.2-20.8% respectively.
- Pooled outcome-specific rates in untreated women with syphilis: stillbirth or fetal loss 25.6% (18.5-34.2), neonatal death to 28 days 12.3% (9.3-16.2), clinical evidence of congenital syphilis 15.5% (7.5-29.0), prematurity or low birth weight 12.1% (3.9-31.8).
- Stated as absolute differences against women without syphilis, the figures most often quoted downstream: 21% for fetal loss or stillbirth, 9.3% for neonatal death, 5.8% for prematurity or low birth weight.
- Heterogeneity was severe almost everywhere. I-squared was 91.8% among women with syphilis and 95.9% among women without for any adverse outcome, and 98.3% for clinical congenital syphilis. It fell to acceptable levels only within the group-3 studies, where women were recruited at delivery (22.5%, 95% CI 18.1-27.5 for stillbirth and fetal loss, I-squared 0.0%).
- In the one study reporting it (McDermott, Malawi), infant death between 29 and 365 days was 21.3% (16/75) versus 10.1% (256/2530), an absolute difference of 11.2%.
- Syphilis seroprevalence among mothers in the contributing studies ran 1.5-8.0%.

## Relevance to project

A bound on the non-HIV benefit of point-of-care syphilis testing, and the standard source for it. If the POC arm is ever asked to account for outcomes beyond HIV incidence, these are the per-pregnancy rates that convert a detected-and-treated maternal syphilis case into averted stillbirths, neonatal deaths and congenital syphilis. The comparator is untreated infection, which is what the SOC arm leaves behind when a woman is asymptomatic and never screened.

It does not bear on the pathway triad. There is no HIV in this analysis, and nothing here informs STI-HIV cofactor effects, prevention targeting, or network identification.

Use the rates as an order of magnitude, not as parameters. The confidence intervals on the outcome-specific pooled estimates are wide enough (prematurity or low birth weight, 3.9-31.8%) that treating any of them as a point value overstates what six heterogeneous case-control studies support.

## Limitations

- Six studies, all observational case-control, four of them from before 1950 and three predating penicillin. Only three are from sub-Saharan Africa.
- Heterogeneity was unacceptable for most pooled estimates, so the summary statistics describe a spread rather than a common effect.
- Seroreactivity was determined by tests ranging from the Wassermann and Kahn tests, whose sensitivity and specificity are unknown, to RPR with treponemal confirmation. Stage of syphilis was generally not distinguished, and timing of infection relative to pregnancy was not recorded.
- Outcome definitions were taken from the original papers, which predate consensus definitions of stillbirth and prematurity.
- Group-2 studies enrolled only asymptomatic women, so they underestimate outcomes in untreated primary and secondary syphilis, where fetal fatality approaches 100%.
- Ascertainment of congenital syphilis is subject to reporting bias: infants of mothers known to be seroreactive were more likely to be examined for it.
