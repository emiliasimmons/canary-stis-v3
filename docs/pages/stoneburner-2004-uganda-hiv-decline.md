A Science policy-forum analysis of Uganda's HIV decline through the 1990s, arguing that the
decline was driven by population-wide reduction in casual sexual partnerships rather than by
condoms, testing, or STI treatment. It compares Ugandan antenatal surveillance and
behavioural survey data against Kenya, Malawi, and Zambia. Extracted text is at
`raw/10.1126/science.1093166/content.md`; the extraction covers a full Science page run, so
the article starts partway down the file (the two neighbouring articles, on hollow
nanocrystals and on anvil cloud nuclei, are not part of this source).

## Relevance to project

Context and a comparator for the counterfactual, not a target or a prior. The project asks how
much HIV incidence reduction a POC STI diagnostic buys on top of syndromic management; this
paper is the strongest published claim that in this region a behavioural shift already
delivered a reduction far larger than any plausible biomedical arm, and that it did so before
STI treatment scaled up. Two concrete uses:

Timing. The authors place the incidence decline in 1990 or earlier and state that condom
social marketing, voluntary testing and counselling, and improved STI treatment all scaled up
*after* the initial prevalence and incidence declines. Any Uganda-calibrated model that fits
the 1990s prevalence fall by turning up STI treatment is fitting the wrong mechanism.

Magnitude. Their Kampala simulation reproduces observed antenatal prevalence by cutting
incidence 80% over two years in 15-24 year olds starting 1992-93, versus a baseline holding
incidence flat after 1990. That is a size-of-effect benchmark for a background behavioural
driver, useful for judging whether a modelled STI-diagnostic effect is being asked to carry
work that behaviour change actually did.

What it cannot do: it supplies no STI prevalence, no partnership-duration or concurrency
parameters, and no Rakai-specific numbers. The behavioural quantities are self-reported
survey indicators, not network measures, so they do not parameterise the risk-structured
network directly.

## Key points

National antenatal HIV prevalence in pregnant women peaked at 21.1% in 1991, fell to 9.7% by
1998 (a 54% decline, in both rural and urban sites) and to 6% by 2000. In Kampala and other
urban sites with age-specific data the decline was 75% in 15-19 year olds and 60% in 20-24
year olds. The abstract's headline "70% decline since the early 1990s" is the later,
fuller-period figure.

Reported casual (non-regular) partnerships in the past year fell 60% between the 1989 and 1995
population-based surveys, in both urban and rural populations. Never-married males aged 15-24
reporting premarital sex fell from 60% to 23%; never-married females from 53% to 16%.

Condom use in casual partnerships rose in Uganda but by 1995 was not substantially different
from Kenya, Malawi, or Zambia. Casual-partnership prevalence in those comparison countries
(Malawi and Zambia 1996, Kenya 1998) resembled Uganda's 1989 level, and none showed a
statistically significant HIV decline overall or in 15-24 year olds. Epidemic maturity does
not explain the difference: urban antenatal curves in Malawi and Zambia lag Uganda's by at
most a couple of years.

In the 1995 Uganda DHS, asked how they had changed behaviour, 53% of sexually experienced
women and 55% of men said they "stuck to one partner", 7% and 10% "stopped all sex", 2% and
11% "started using condoms".

Supporting non-antenatal trends: HIV prevalence in male military conscripts aged 19-22 fell
from 18.6% (95% CI 14.1-22.9) in 1991 to 8.0% (95% CI 6.3-9.7) in 1996 and 4% by 2002.

The proposed mechanism is communication through personal social networks. 82% of Ugandan women
had heard of AIDS through personal channels versus 40-65% elsewhere; by 1995, 91.5% of men and
86.4% of women personally knew someone with AIDS, against 68-71% in Zambia, Kenya, and Malawi
and under 50% in Zimbabwe and South Africa by 2002.

## Limitations

The core comparison is ecological: country-level behavioural indicators against country-level
prevalence trends, with no individual linkage between the behaviour change and the averted
infections. Causal attribution to the communication mechanism rests on cross-country contrasts
in survey responses.

The behavioural data are self-reported, and the paper is itself a rebuttal in a live dispute
over how to read them. A later UNAIDS analysis put the decline in casual sex at 9% in men with
increases in women; the authors state that reanalysis showed this to be inaccurate. The
project should treat the 60% figure as contested rather than settled.

Antenatal prevalence is a poor proxy for population incidence: HIV reduces fertility, which
biases the sampling frame, and prevalence responds to mortality as well as to incidence. The
authors address this but do not remove it.

The 80% incidence reduction is a model input chosen so the simulation matches observed
prevalence, not an independently estimated quantity. Fitting a single unknown to a single
trend leaves the mechanism unidentified; a different combination of incidence decline and
mortality would fit similarly.

The comparison countries differ from Uganda in more than communication strategy, and the
paper does not adjust for the political and economic differences (including Uganda's earlier
epidemic and its distinctive state response) that could produce the same contrast.
