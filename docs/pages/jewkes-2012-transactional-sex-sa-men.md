Cross-sectional household survey of a two-stage stratified random sample of men aged 18-49 in three districts of the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, measuring self-reported engagement with women in prostitution and with transactional sexual relationships. Analysis covers 1645 sexually active men who answered both sets of questions. Extracted text and tables sit in `raw/10.1186/1471-2458-12-325`.

## Relevance to project

A male-side prevalence bound for the two network features the model needs sized: the client population behind a sex-work compartment, and concurrency.

Client prevalence: 18.3% (95% CI 16.2-20.7, n=296/1615) of men 18-49 ever had sex with a woman in prostitution, lifetime, in a general-population sample. That is a ceiling on the fraction of men who could ever occupy a client role, not a point-in-time client rate, and the model needs the latter. Used as an upper bound rather than a target.

Concurrency: 41.7% (95% CI 38.8-44.7) of men reported a transactional relationship with a *khwapheni*, an ongoing secondary concurrent partner alongside a main partner. This is a lifetime measure of one flavour of concurrency (the transactional kind), so it understates total concurrency and overstates the point-prevalence.

Gradient by income for the client role is the sharpest structure here and bears on how the risk-structured network should correlate wealth with client status: 13.0% among men with no monthly income against 27.9% at R500-2000, 31.3% at R2001-5000, 29.2% above R5000. Adjusted, relative to no income: OR 2.09 (1.46-3.01) at R500-2000 and 2.20 (1.30-3.73) at R2001-5000. Transactional relationships show no such gradient (61.1% at no income, 76.3% at R500-2000, 61.7% above R5000), so the two behaviours load differently onto a risk axis.

Age: the client role has no age gradient once adjusted (OR 1.17 for 25-34, 1.03 for 35-49, both non-significant against 18-24), which the authors read as men either starting young or never. Transactional relationships do rise with age (OR 1.90, 1.42-2.56, for 25-34).

What it cannot do here: it is South Africa, not Rakai or southern Uganda, and the model's first calibration site is the RCCS. Nothing here is an HIV or STI target. There is no partner-count, no act-frequency, no duration, and no time-anchored measure, so none of it feeds a network parameter directly. It supplies the shape and the rough magnitude, not a number to fit to.

## Key points

Only 29.9% of men reported neither transactional sex nor sex with a woman in prostitution. Transactional relationships were most often with a main partner (58.0%), rather than casual ones, so instruments that ask only about casual exchange miss the largest category.

Men separate the two practices. Among the 314 men who had once-off transactional sex and gave cash, only 34.4% said they had had sex with a "prostitute". Roughly a quarter of men reporting sex with a woman in prostitution (3% of the whole sample) reported no transactional sex at all.

Goods given barely varied by partner type. Cash to pay bills: 49.2% for main partners, 48.5% for *khwapheni*, 45.0% for once-off. The exception is lodging, given to 72.6% of once-off partners against 43.1% of main partners.

Men's reported transactional-sex prevalence (66%) far exceeds women's from a comparable Soweto survey (21% with non-primary partners), which the authors attribute partly to differing instruments and partly to men asserting a provider role.

## Limitations

The transactional-sex measure asks men to speculate on a partner's motives, so it records perceived provider expectation rather than a negotiated exchange. The authors argue the construct, developed on women, does not transfer to men and that what they captured is a conservative male provider norm.

All measures are lifetime and self-reported. Men who had never had a partner of a given type were coded as not having done that type of sex. Under- and over-reporting cannot be quantified, though audio-enhanced PDA self-completion (anonymous, no interviewer) should reduce social-desirability pressure on the stigmatised items. Household weighting ignored the number of eligible men per household.

Sample skews young (52.1% aged 18-24) and poor (48.7% no monthly income), and 85.2% Black African, with only 26 White men, so the race-stratified estimates outside the African category rest on small cells.
