A 2010 household survey in Kampala, Uganda, randomizing women aged 18-24 to three questionnaire delivery modes and testing whether their reported sexual behavior survives comparison with biological markers. The three arms were paper-and-pencil face-to-face interview with question rewording permitted (FTFI, n = 365), computer-assisted personal interview with questions read verbatim (CAPI, n = 317), and audio computer-assisted self-interview for the sensitive questions (ACASI, n = 338). The questionnaire was a subset of the 2006 Uganda DHS women's instrument. Extracted text sits in `raw/10.1111/j.1728-4465.2014.00375.x`.

## Relevance to project

A bound on how far survey-reported sexual behavior can be trusted as a calibration target, and a reason to prefer biomarker-anchored quantities where the model has a choice. The behavioral inputs the risk-structured network needs, lifetime partner counts, transactional sex, coital frequency, and condom use, all come from self-report in DHS-style instruments, and this paper puts numbers on the error in a Ugandan population close to the model's youngest adult band.

The operative figure: among women testing positive for semen exposure in the previous 48 hours, 49 percent (38/78) in FTFI, 55 percent (49/89) in CAPI, and 67 percent (51/76) in ACASI reported no unprotected sex over that window. Recent unprotected sex is therefore under-reported by roughly half in a face-to-face DHS-style interview, in the direction that lowers apparent exposure. Condom use runs the other way: 30 percent of recently sexually active ACASI respondents reported condom use in the past two days against 5 percent in FTFI, and one-sixth of semen-positive ACASI women claimed all recent acts were protected, so a reported condom-use fraction is not a safe direct input to the per-act transmission calculation either.

Roughly 5 percent of HSV-2-positive women denied ever having sex (5.6 percent FTFI, 4.5 percent CAPI, 4.9 percent ACASI), which sets a floor on misclassified sexual debut when age at first sex is used to open a model agent's exposure window. HSV-2 seroprevalence itself was 47 percent overall in this 18-24 female sample, and HIV prevalence 7.3 percent (9.1 percent FTFI, 7.3 percent CAPI, 5.4 percent ACASI). Both are Kampala urban figures, not Rakai, so they serve as context rather than targets.

What it cannot supply: no gonorrhoea, chlamydia, syphilis, or trichomoniasis testing, no men, no incidence, and no partnership-level structure. It bears on the confidence intervals placed on behavioral parameters, not on their central values.

## Key points

Sexual experience reporting was highest in ACASI on four of five outcomes, but the gaps were mostly small and unstable. Ever had sex: 92 percent ACASI against 86 percent CAPI (p significant) and 88 percent FTFI (p = 0.076). Forced sex: 39 percent ACASI against 31 percent FTFI. Transactional sex in the past 12 months among sexually active women: 19 percent ACASI against 12 percent FTFI, adjusted odds ratio 1.59 relative to CAPI at p = 0.068 and 2.49 relative to FTFI at p < 0.001. More than one lifetime partner: 67-72 percent across arms, no significant difference.

The two face-to-face variants were indistinguishable on every reported prevalence and on every biomarker discrepancy, so permitting interviewers to reword questions did not itself change what women reported. Item nonresponse was higher under the verbatim CAPI protocol, where 3 percent of sexually active women did not understand the transactional sex question.

Greater reporting in ACASI did not translate into better agreement with biology. On the semen test, ACASI was the worst of the three arms.

## Limitations

The HSV-2 assay was Kalon ELISA on dried blood spots, validated locally at 85 percent sensitivity and 74 percent specificity, giving a positive predictive value of 0.75 (95 percent CI 0.69-0.81). About a quarter of the self-reported virgins who tested positive are therefore likely false positives, though the misclassification should not differ by arm. HIV was uninformative for validation because every HIV-positive participant reported sexual initiation.

The semen test detects semenogelin for a window the authors treat as 48 hours, while laboratory work cited in the paper suggests detection past that. Extending the reporting window to five days lowers the discrepant fractions to 33 percent FTFI, 42 percent CAPI, and 55 percent ACASI, which is the more conservative version of the headline result.

Interviewers were experienced DHS staff trained under a rewording-permitted protocol before being retrained for verbatim delivery, so protocol drift may explain the null FTFI-versus-CAPI comparison. The condom-use question sat inside a five-day calendar sequence, and the authors suspect self-administered respondents mishandled it, which would make the inflated ACASI condom figure a question-design artifact rather than a reporting-mode effect.

## Methodology

Forty enumeration areas from the 2002 census sampled at random, 30 households each, all resident or overnight-visiting women aged 18-24 eligible. Block randomization to mode and to interviewer. 82 percent of eligible women were interviewed (1,058/1,297), with 7 percent of CAPI and 4 percent of ACASI electronic records lost to technology failure. Biomarkers were a finger-prick blood sample for HIV rapid testing (Determine, confirmed with Uni-Gold, STAT-PAK tiebreaker) and dried blood spots for HSV-2, plus a self-administered vaginal swab for the RSID-Semen test. Women were told the swab detects semenogelin but not that it would be compared against their answers. Refusal of one or more biomarkers was higher in FTFI (12.6 percent) than CAPI (5.6 percent).
