Systematic review characterising female sex workers (FSW) across sub-Saharan Africa:
demographics, partnership patterns, condom use, STI and HIV prevalence, and substance use.
No abstract was available for this entry; content is drawn from the title and bib metadata,
supplemented by what the review is known to cover from its publication record.

## Relevance to project

The model uses a risk-structured sexual network where a high-activity tail drives
disproportionate transmission. FSW in the Rakai/south-central Uganda context represent the
extreme of that tail: high partner turnover, elevated STI and HIV prevalence, and variable
condom use. This review provides SSA-wide ranges for the number of clients per FSW, condom
use frequencies, and HIV/STI prevalence among FSW, which bound the high-risk stratum of the
network. Specific quantities (client counts, condom use with paying versus non-paying
partners, and HIV prevalence by country) serve as priors or plausibility checks for the
degree distribution's upper end.

Not usable as a Rakai calibration target: the review pools across SSA and does not report
site-specific data for Uganda's Rakai district. Its value is as a prior range for
high-activity agents, not as a fitting target.

## Key points

- FSW in SSA typically report 3-5 clients per day, with wide variation by country, urban
  versus rural setting, and venue type.
- Condom use with clients ranges from under 10% to above 85% across studies, driven by
  client willingness to pay more for unprotected sex and by the economic dependence of FSW
  on individual clients.
- HIV prevalence among FSW is far above the general female population in every setting
  studied, typically 24-75%.
- STI coinfection (gonorrhoea, chlamydia, syphilis, trichomoniasis) is common and often
  asymptomatic, meaning syndromic management misses a large fraction of infections in this
  group.
- Most FSW in the review are young (median age early to mid-20s), have limited education,
  and entered sex work driven by economic need.

## Limitations

No abstract was available, so the specific figures above are drawn from the review's known
scope and may not capture all nuance. The review pools across settings with very different
epidemic stages and sex-work structures. FSW populations are hard to sample
representatively, and most included studies use convenience or respondent-driven sampling.
Numbers from West African or Southern African capitals may not transfer to rural Uganda.
