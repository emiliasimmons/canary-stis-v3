Source: [burke-2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0183280)

Qualitative study run through the Rakai Health Sciences Program: 33 in-depth interviews and 6 focus groups totalling 55 participants, conducted in Luganda or English, across high-prevalence Lake Victoria fishing communities and lower-prevalence mainland communities in Rakai District. The interview sample is 12 health care providers (9 mainland, 3 fishing), 6 fishermen and boat owners, 8 female sex workers, and 7 mainland community members. Participants were recruited through RHSP community health workers and RCCS participants who had agreed to re-contact, with snowball sampling from there. Analysis used a team-based matrix method adapted from Framework Analysis.

## Relevance to project

Nothing here is a calibration target, a prior, or a bound. The study reports no prevalence, no incidence, no test sensitivity, and no uptake rate. Almost no participant had encountered a self-test before the interview, so the responses are about a hypothetical device.

What it supplies is a design reference for the acceptability side of a point-of-care diagnostic in exactly the setting the project models. The barriers named are the ones that decide whether a test that works in the laboratory sense reaches the person it is for: fear of attending a clinic at all, cost and time of transport, stigma at the facility, the absence of a counsellor to interpret a result, worry about safe disposal of a blood-draw kit, and doubt that a positive result would be followed by an actual clinic visit. Every one of those applies to an etiological STI panel offered at the same symptomatic care-seeking door, and none of them is captured by a sensitivity and specificity pair.

Two findings bear on how the POC arm should be represented. Participants were willing to pay 1,000 to 100,000 Ugandan shillings for a kit, with most fishing-community participants naming 3,000 to 5,000 (roughly US$0.88-1.47 at the time) and most mainland participants a maximum of 2,000 (US$0.59), while several providers said uptake would require the kit to be free. That is a rough ceiling on out-of-pocket willingness to pay for a self-administered diagnostic in this population, though it is stated preference for a device nobody had used. Second, most participants preferred to collect kits at a health facility precisely so a provider could counsel them, with young men and fishermen the exception in wanting bars and lodges. A test-and-treat panel sited at the clinic, which is what the POC arm assumes, is aligned with the stated preference; a distributed screening programme would not be.

The one behavioural mechanism worth carrying forward is testing a partner before sex. Sex workers described using a self-test on clients who claim to be negative and refuse condoms. That is a route by which a diagnostic changes the act rather than the infection, and it cuts both ways: clients who test negative may be less likely to use protection.

The source cannot support any quantitative claim about self-testing uptake, linkage after a positive self-test, or the effect of self-testing on incidence.

## Key points

Fishing communities along Lake Victoria in Rakai carry a median HIV prevalence of 42% (range 38-43), against a Ugandan national figure of 7.1%. That contextual figure is cited from other work, not measured here.

The most common perceived benefit was privacy, both from partners and from community gossip attached to being seen at a health centre. Fishing-community participants and providers raised fear of the clinic itself far more often than mainland participants did, and framed self-testing as a way to get a preliminary result and then gather the resolve to attend. Avoided transport time and cost was the other frequently named benefit.

The dominant concern, voiced across every group, was the absence of a health professional during testing. Specific worries were misreading the instructions, false positives, not understanding the window period during which a recent infection reads negative, and suicide after an unsupported positive result. Providers also raised improper disposal of a lancet-based kit, both as an infection risk to children and as evidence a partner might find.

Several participants doubted that a positive self-test would be followed up. A few sex workers argued self-testing would only delay treatment, since a facility diagnosis gives immediate access to ART, and one saw no value in it for that reason. Others expected a self-test result, unlike a clinic slip, to be deniable to a partner.

Nearly every participant proposed the same fix: education and counselling before the kit is handed over, with instructions in Luganda and a phone number to call.

Almost all participants said they would use a kit if provided and adequately taught, and would recommend it. Two women dissented, judging it too risky, as did a couple of providers who held that facility-based testing remained necessary. Groups named as likely beneficiaries were married couples, youth, sex workers, bar workers, truckers, fishermen and boda boda motorcycle taxi drivers, which is the same risk set the occupational epidemiology in Rakai identifies.

Participants were not asked about oral swab against finger-prick specimens, but the disposal and weapon concerns raised about sharps would not apply to an oral test.

## Limitations

Hypothetical throughout. Most participants had never heard of HIV self-testing before the interview, so responses are anticipated rather than experienced behaviour, and stated willingness to use and to pay is known to overstate both.

Sampling was purposive with snowball recruitment seeded through RHSP community health workers and RCCS participants, so the sample skews toward people already connected to the research and health system. Occupation was not recorded for mainland participants, so the mainland and fishing comparisons are between locations rather than between matched occupational groups.

Interviews conducted in Luganda were translated to English before analysis, with the authors noting possible loss of meaning, mitigated by the collection team also doing the translation.

The study predates the Ugandan self-testing pilots it anticipates, so none of the preferences here have been checked against observed uptake.
