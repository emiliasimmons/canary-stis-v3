A narrative review in AIDS Research and Therapy covering why HIV incidence in sub-Saharan
Africa falls disproportionately on women, and what has been tried against it. It reports no
new data: every figure is cited from a trial, cohort, or UNAIDS report, and the review's
value is the assembled list of risk mechanisms rather than any single number. Extracted
text sits in `raw/10.1186/1742-6405-10-30`.

## Relevance to project

Context, not a target or a prior. Nothing here is at the resolution the model calibrates
against, and the geography is wrong for Rakai: the quantitative spine is South African
(Durban, KwaZulu-Natal) with scattered Ugandan citations.

Two things it does supply. First, a justification for the SOC arm's weakness that does not
depend on a modelling assumption: STIs in women are frequently asymptomatic, so a
syndromic-management door misses them, which is the mechanism the POC arm is meant to
open. The review asserts this from the literature without quantifying the asymptomatic
fraction, so a page that gives an actual proportion should carry that number instead.

Second, a checklist of risk-structure features the sexual network is expected to reproduce:
concurrency, age-disparate partnering and early sexual debut, female sex work, and
migration. The review gives associations rather than transmission parameters, so these
bound plausibility rather than set values. The strongest single figure for network
structure is that female sex workers are 13.5 times as likely to be living with HIV as
other women (UNAIDS 2013 global estimate, prevalence ratio, not incidence), and that
roughly 15% of HIV in the general adult female population is attributable to unsafe female
sex work globally.

The prevention efficacy figures are comparator material for the POC arm's effect size, all
from randomised trials the review cites: oral tenofovir 67% (95% CI 44-81) and
tenofovir/emtricitabine 75% (55-87) reduction in HIV acquisition among the negative partner
in serodiscordant couples (Partners PrEP); ART for the positive partner in a serodiscordant
couple 96% (HPTN 052); 1% tenofovir vaginal gel 39% (6-60) in South African women
(CAPRISA 004), not replicated in VOICE. Each is an as-randomised effect in a specific
population, and each was adherence-limited. They set the scale a diagnostics-driven
intervention is competing on.

## Key points

- Sub-Saharan Africa held 69% of the world's 23.5 million people with HIV at the time of
  writing (UNAIDS 2012). Adult prevalence: Swaziland 26.5%, South Africa 17.9%, Namibia
  13.3%, Mozambique 11.1%.
- HIV prevalence among non-pregnant women in prevention-trial screening ranged from 14.5%
  (95% CI 11.2-18.4) in east Africa to 38.7% (34.2-43.3) in Lusaka and 39.5% (35.1-44.0) in
  Durban. Prevalence that looks flat can sit over high incidence.
- Risk-factor analyses across those cohorts identify age under 25, a prior STI, and being
  unmarried as predictors of seroconversion in women.
- Sexual debut at 15 or younger versus 21 or older: hazard ratio 1.97 (95% CI 1.07-3.63,
  p = 0.029) for HIV positivity, Durban cohort.
- 12% of new HIV infections in women in one South African study were attributed to intimate
  partner violence; a Ugandan study put the increase in HIV risk from gender-based violence
  at 55%.
- Gonorrhoea, chlamydia, syphilis and trichomoniasis prevalence differed sharply across four
  trial sites: women at the South African sites were about 3 times as likely to acquire an
  STI as those at the Tanzanian and Zambian sites.
- Stigma and fear of disclosure are named as the reason women delay HIV testing, which is
  the same friction that governs symptomatic care-seeking.
- Injectable progestin contraception (DMPA) and HIV acquisition: the evidence is split, and
  the review does not resolve it. WHO's response was a counselling recommendation, not a
  restriction.

## Limitations

A review without a stated search strategy, inclusion criteria, or quality appraisal, so it
carries no more weight than the primary sources it cites, and less than reading them
directly. Several assertions about cultural practice (dry sex, cleansing rituals, virginity
testing) rest on a single working paper and are explicitly unconfirmed as HIV risk factors
by the review's own text. Written in 2013, so it predates universal test-and-treat, the
dapivirine ring result, and current PrEP delivery. For any figure quoted above, the primary
trial is the citable source.
