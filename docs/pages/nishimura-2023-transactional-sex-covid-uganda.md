Source: [nishimura-2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/13691058.2023.2238796)

Semi-structured in-depth interviews conducted by phone between November 2021 and February
2022 with 26 sexually active men aged 18-49 in Rakai District, recruited from prior Rakai
Community Cohort Study (RCCS) participants who had agreed to be recontacted. The question is
how the two Ugandan COVID-19 lockdowns, from March 2020 and from June 2021, changed men's
non-marital sexual partnerships.

Transactional sex is defined as in the RCCS survey item, a relationship dependent on the
implicit exchange of money, favours or goods for sex, spanning one-night encounters through
multi-year partnerships, explicitly excluding marriage and excluding commercial sex work
where the price is negotiated in advance.

## Relevance to project

A structural input on what holds a transactional partnership together, usable as a
qualitative constraint on how the network module treats partnership duration and dissolution
rather than as any kind of number. The finding that matters is directional and consistent
across the sample: when a man's ability to provide materially stopped, the transactional
partnership ended, while non-transactional partnerships in the same economic conditions did
not. Men with several partners describe pruning down to the one they could still support.
Several men whose partners lived far away formed new local transactional partnerships
instead of maintaining the distant one, which is partnership turnover at constant degree
rather than a drop in degree.

That gives a mechanism worth representing if the model is ever asked to reproduce a shock
period, and a caution for the base case: transactional partnership duration in Rakai is
contingent on the male partner's income, so a fixed duration distribution calibrated on
one economic period will not transport across another.

The study supplies no rates. It is a purposive sample of 26 men, 21 of them selected
precisely because they had reported transactional sex in the 2016-2018 RCCS rounds, so
nothing in it is a prevalence, an incidence, a partnership-duration estimate, or a target of
any kind. It says nothing about STIs, symptoms, testing or care seeking.

## Key points

Participants were 9 from agrarian, 9 from fishing and 8 from trading communities; 13
currently married, 8 unmarried, 5 previously married; the commonest occupation was fishing
(8 of 26). Twenty had reported transactional sex in the RCCS and 21 described a recent
transactional relationship.

The economic shock was concentrated in the informal sector. Men in military or government
posts described stable income and correspondingly stable partnerships. Fishermen described
demand for fish collapsing and their provision to partners falling with it. Interviews took
place about eighteen months after the first lockdown and several men reported income still
below pre-pandemic levels.

Two distinct mechanisms operate on the partnerships. Reduced provision drove conflict and
dissolution, and mobility restriction, principally the 7:00pm to 6:30am curfew and the
suspension of transport, cut the frequency of in-person meetings. Men described substituting
mobile money for cash handed over in person, and one described deliberately sending less
because he was not meeting his partner for sex, which locates the exchange at the encounter
rather than at the relationship.

Fear of COVID-19 transmission itself was not reported as a reason for restricting contact
with partners beyond the first weeks of lockdown.

Two men in trading and agrarian communities reported noticing more women seeking
transactional relationships during lockdown, which they could not take up because of their
own finances. The supply side and demand side of the exchange moved in opposite directions.

For context on the setting the paper cites HIV prevalence of 13.7% in agrarian and trading
communities against 40.1% in fishing communities, taken from a separate population-based
Rakai study, and 13% of men's sexual relationships classifiable as transactional before the
pandemic, from the first author's dissertation.

## Limitations

The authors' own limitation is sampling: only 5 of 26 men had no transactional-sex history,
so the comparison group is too small to have reached thematic saturation, and the claim that
non-transactional relationships were more robust to reduced provision rests on those five.

Everything is retrospective self-report about a period ending up to two years earlier,
collected by phone by staff of the Rakai Health Sciences Program, which participants know
well. Men are reporting on their own provision and on partners' behaviour they did not
observe, and several attributions of dissolution to a partner's choice are inference on the
respondent's part.

Men only. Whether the women in these partnerships describe the same dissolutions the same
way is not addressed, and the direction of the exchange means their account would be the
more informative one for network structure.

## Methodology

Phone rather than in-person interviewing was chosen for infection control, with interviewers
stopping and rescheduling when a participant was not in a private location. Fishing-community
men, harder to reach by their own phones, were interviewed on a phone provided by RCCS staff
at a location the staff arranged, which puts those interviews in a different privacy setting
from the rest.

Analysis was Braun and Clarke thematic analysis with a codebook built deductively from the
interview guide plus emergent codes, all transcripts coded by the first author alone, with
themes refined in team discussion. Single-coder application means the themes carry no
inter-coder agreement, so the appropriate use is the mechanism the quotations demonstrate,
not the relative weight assigned to any theme.
