Source: [alam-2010](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-10-19)

Systematic review of partner notification (PN) for the four curable bacterial STIs
(gonorrhoea, chlamydia, syphilis, trichomoniasis) in low, lower-middle and upper-middle
income countries. Medline, Embase and Google Scholar, January 1995 to December 2007,
updated to March 2008: 609 records screened, 474 unique, 39 included (28 Africa, 6 Asia,
5 Latin America/Caribbean). Only 3 were randomised trials. By the authors' own grading,
32 studies were moderate quality, 7 low, and none high. No meta-analysis was attempted.

## Relevance to project

canary-stis implements no partner-notification pathway, and this review is the source that
bounds what such a pathway could deliver if one were ever added. It gives an empirical
ceiling on the cascade, in a setting close to the Rakai calibration site: stated
willingness to self-notify runs 58-93% across seven studies, but the median proportion of
partners actually notified is 54% (range 0-94%), and the proportion who reach a clinic and
get treated is much lower still (20% of female partners referred in China, 30% in Haiti,
34% treated under patient referral in Zambia, 13.3% treated in one Chinese cohort). Two
Ugandan trials are the closest comparators: patient-delivered partner medication treated
74% of partners against 34% under patient referral alone (RR 2.44, 95% CI 1.95-3.07), and
a community trial in rural Uganda reached 25% of partners with treatment. A PN arm
parameterised at anything above roughly 0.3-0.5 partners treated per index case, absent
patient-delivered medication, would be optimistic against this evidence.

The review also supplies a structural argument for the project's POC arm that is
independent of test sensitivity. Reviewed studies name the absence of valid, affordable,
user-friendly diagnostics as a barrier to PN in its own right: syndromic management
generates false positives, and notifying a partner on a false positive carries social cost
(partner violence, separation, blame), which is a reason providers and patients withhold
notification. An etiological point-of-care test removes that objection. This is a
qualitative claim in the review, not a quantified effect, so it belongs in the framing
rather than in a parameter.

What the source cannot do here: it supplies no incidence or prevalence impact of PN, no
cost-effectiveness estimate for a developing-country setting, and nothing that could serve
as a calibration target. Outcomes are almost all self-reported and definitions of
"notified" vary study to study, so the 54% median is not a quantity a model can be fit to.

## Key points

- Notification is strongly partner-type dependent. One Kenyan study of women with syphilis
  reported 94% of spousal partners notified; the extreme low of 0% in the same review is
  casual partners in another study. A Peruvian study: 65% of primary partners informed
  against 10% of secondary partners. Any network-structured PN model has to split by
  partnership type, not apply one rate.
- Intention does not predict action. 86% willing to notify against 30% of partners notified
  in Haiti; 58% willing against 45% notified elsewhere.
- Client-oriented counselling improves referral. The Zimbabwe trial: 92% notified in the
  counselling arm against 67% in control (AOR 4.1, 95% CI 1.3-13.2). The Zambia trial: 1.8
  partners treated per infected man against 1.2 in control (p < 0.001), with no significant
  effect among infected women. Video-based education in South Africa moved returned contact
  cards from 0.20 to 0.27 per index patient, an increase the authors attribute to chance.
- Patient-delivered partner medication was preferred when offered: 85% chose it over 13%
  choosing patient-based referral in a South African study.
- Barriers are gendered. Women cite fear of violence, blame as the source of infection, and
  divorce; women with non-spousal partners cite financial dependence on those partners. Men
  cite embarrassment, loss of respect, and being seen as unfaithful; casual partners are
  not notified because men have no further plan to see them.

## Limitations

Search ends in 2007, so nothing here reflects mobile-phone or internet notification (the
authors flag both as untested future directions), and nothing reflects modern NAAT-based
point-of-care testing. No study reached the authors' "high quality" grade. Outcome
ascertainment is heterogeneous, mixing self-report against clinic-record verification, and
the authors explicitly rule out meta-analysis; the 54% median is a median across
incommensurable denominators. The review covers curable STIs only and excludes HIV by
design. Numbers span Africa, Asia and Latin America with health systems that differ enough
that pooling is a stretch, though the authors argue the consistency of qualitative themes
across continents is itself evidence of robustness.
