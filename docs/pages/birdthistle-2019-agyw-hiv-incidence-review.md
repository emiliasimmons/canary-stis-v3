Systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of directly observed (serologically
measured) HIV incidence among adolescent girls and young women aged 15-24 in the ten
countries selected for DREAMS investment. 51 studies from nine countries met inclusion:
serological data collected 2005-2015, English-language, modelled incidence estimates
excluded. 27 studies drew on general population samples, 24 on higher-risk or venue-based
subpopulations (sex workers, bar and guesthouse workers, fishing communities, clinic
attendees).

## Relevance to project

The Rakai rows of table 1 are the most direct use: an age-, sex-, and period-specific set
of HIV incidence targets for the calibration site, taken from unpublished Grabowski et al
disaggregations of the RCCS. Per 100 person-years, females: 1.03 (0.64-1.57) at 15-19 and
1.47 (1.12-1.90) at 20-24 over 2005-11, falling to 0.59 (0.31-1.01) and 1.53 (1.13-2.01)
over 2011-16. Males over the same two periods: 0.30 (0.12-0.60) then 0.16 (0.05-0.38) at
15-19, and 0.90 (0.61-1.28) then 0.40 (0.21-0.68) at 20-24. Person-years are given per cell
(roughly 1800-3700), so a binomial or Poisson likelihood can be built directly rather than
from a rate and a CI. Santelli et al add two earlier sexually-experienced-only Rakai
estimates, 1.49 (1.06-2.04) for females 15-19 in 1998-2008 and 0.63 (0.20-1.47) in 2006-09,
on a different denominator (sexually experienced participants, not all participants).

Second use is the sex- and age-gradient the model's risk-structured network has to
reproduce. Pooled East African Community rates per 100 person-years are, for females, 0.68
(0.45-1.04) at 15-19, 1.23 (1.01-1.49) at 20-24, 1.07 (0.84-1.36) at 25-29; for males, 0.21
(0.12-0.37), 0.72 (0.52-1.02), 1.32 (0.93-1.86) across the same bands. The female excess is
concentrated at 15-19 (pooled relative risk 3.22, 95% CI 1.51-6.87 in eastern Africa; 5.94,
3.39-10.44 in southern Africa) and is not significantly different from unity by 25-29,
where the male rate has overtaken. A network that reproduces total incidence but not this
crossover will misplace where a point-of-care STI intervention can reach transmission.

Third, it bounds how much of the Rakai decline is already accounted for by ART. Female
15-19 incidence in Rakai roughly halved between 2005-11 and 2011-16 while 20-24 was flat,
against a backdrop of ART and voluntary medical male circumcision scale-up and no targeted
prevention investment before 2016. Any STI-attributable effect the model attributes over
this window competes with that trend.

Not usable as a comparator for the SOC/POC arms: no study here measures an STI
intervention, and STIs are mentioned only as a recruitment venue for a few trials. The
southern African pooled rates (female 4.22, 4.36, 4.48 per 100 person-years at 15-19,
20-24, 25-29) are an order of magnitude above Rakai and should not be borrowed as priors.

## Key points

Absolute levels span more than an order of magnitude across settings. The lowest general
population estimate was 0.38 per 100 person-years among females aged 13-19 in Kumi
district, rural Uganda, 2006-08; the highest was 7.79 at 15-19 and 8.63 at 20-24 among
sexually active young women in uMkhanyakude, KwaZulu-Natal, 2003-12. Higher-risk samples
went further: 12.40 per 100 person-years among females 15-19 in Ugandan fishing
communities by 2015, 13.20 among female sex workers under 25 in South Africa, 10.80 in
Zimbabwe.

Among higher-risk groups, incidence often peaks earlier than in general populations: rates
were commonly higher at 15-19 than at 20-24 in samples of sex workers, bar workers, and
pregnant women, the reverse of the general population pattern. Ugandan fishing communities
show 12.40 at 15-19 against 4.70 at 20-24.

Declines since 2005 appear in Rakai, Manicaland (Zimbabwe), nationally in South Africa
(6.5 per 100 person-years among females 15-24 in 2005 falling to 2.54 in 2012), and among
Mombasa sex workers. No decline was detectable by 2015 in uMkhanyakude or in the Ugandan
fishing communities.

## Methodology

Incidence rates were converted to cases per 100 person-years, and CIs derived by exact
Poisson methods when a study did not report them. Pooling used random effects on log
incidence with corresponding standard errors, restricted to prospective designs (19 of the
51 studies contributed); cross-sectional assay-based estimates were excluded from pooling.
Studies with zero events had 0.5 added to events and person-years. No test for
heterogeneity is reported, on the grounds that incidence is expected to vary by setting.
Eight of the 51 studies contributed previously unpublished age- and sex-disaggregated data
obtained by contacting authors.

## Limitations

Cohort and cross-sectional assay estimates are mixed within the review, and the
seroconversion date in cohorts is usually imputed at the midpoint or a random point
between the last negative and first positive test, which biases the rate when the
inter-test interval is long. The southern African pool is dominated by repeat estimates
from one surveillance site (uMkhanyakude contributes 11 of 21 South African studies), so
the SADC pooled rates are close to a single-site estimate. Estimates from the same cohort
across overlapping calendar windows are treated as separate estimates within the pool, so
the pooled CIs understate correlation. The authors also flag possible upward publication
bias: eight authors approached for unpublished disaggregations returned nothing, and those
that did return data reported low seroconversion counts with wide CIs. Rakai person-years
in the 15-19 female cells are small enough (about 1840 and 1857) that the two-period
comparison rests on 19 versus 11 events.
