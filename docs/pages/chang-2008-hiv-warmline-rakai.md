Source: [chang-2008](https://doi.org/10.1177/1545109708318525)

A three-page service evaluation of a toll-free telephone warmline, meaning a line staffed
during working hours rather than around the clock, set up at the Rakai Health Sciences
Program central clinic in April 2006 to give clinical advice to the field staff, peer health
workers and patients of its antiretroviral therapy programme. It logs who called, why, and
what happened by the end of the call, over the 17 months to August 2007.

## Relevance to project

Close to none for the diagnostics question, and the entry should be read as a placement of
record rather than a source the model draws on. The warmline is an ART adherence and clinical
support service. It measures no STI, no symptom, no test, and no treatment episode of the
kind the point-of-care arm intervenes on, and its 2006-2007 window predates the intervention
period of interest.

The one thing it does supply is a description of how HIV care was physically delivered in
Rakai at the time, which is context for what "the care-seeking door" means in this district.
Rakai covers close to 5,000 square kilometres with a population then around 495,000, and the
programme reached it by sending a mobile clinic out from one central clinic to visit each
community every two weeks. The ART cohort grew from roughly 450 patients at the warmline's
launch to nearly 1,200 by August 2007. Landlines were rare, about two thirds of communities
had reliable mobile coverage, and most had a public village phone. A patient with a problem
between mobile-clinic visits had a two-week wait or a phone call, and the warmline existed
because that gap was operationally intolerable.

That geometry is worth holding when reasoning about symptomatic presentation in the model,
since a service door that opens fortnightly at a travelling clinic is not the same object as
a standing facility. It is a qualitative observation about the delivery system in one
programme fifteen-plus years before the modelled intervention, not a care-seeking rate, and
it should not be turned into one.

## Key points

Volume was 1,303 logged calls over 17 months, 3.5 per weekday, median 75 per month
(interquartile range 53 to 78, full range 35 to 182). Of these, 726 (56%) were inbound and
577 (44%) were placed outward by warmline staff, mostly following up an inbound call.

All the descriptive breakdown is on the 726 inbound calls, not on the full 1,303, and the
outbound half is uncharacterised. Callers were field staff 332 (46%), peer health workers
169 (23%), patients themselves 86 (12%), patient companions 36 (5%), relatives 28 (4%),
friends 17 (2%), other 58 (8%). Peer health workers are community health workers who are
themselves living with HIV and on ART through the programme, each covering roughly 15 to 20
fellow patients; companions are individuals a patient nominates to support their adherence.
So three quarters of inbound calls came from the programme's own workforce rather than from
patients or their households.

Reasons given were general illness concerns 345 (48%), care follow-up 93 (13%), urgent care
needs 93 (13%), medication-specific concerns 91 (13%), medication refills 78 (11%) and
laboratory results 53 (7%). Outcomes were consultation only, meaning advice, reassurance or
scheduling, in 602 (83%), immediate triage to a clinic or hospital in 25 (3%), medication
change in 3 (under 1%) and unknown in 96 (13%).

Of the 93 urgent-care calls, 25 (27%) produced immediate triage and the rest ended in
reassurance, an appointment at the next scheduled visit, or referral to a local facility.
Urgent calls came disproportionately from peer health workers, companions, relatives and
friends rather than from field staff (chi-squared p < 0.01 for each comparison).

Costs were about USD 100 to start, excluding staff compensation, and about USD 277 per
month, which the authors express as USD 0.28 per patient per month against the ART cohort
denominator and USD 3.64 per call. They flag the per-call figure as the concerning one, a
consequence of voice-call pricing in Uganda at the time, and suggest text messaging.

A Likert evaluation of all 39 clinical staff (100% response) found 38% strongly agreeing and
54% agreeing that the warmline improved patients' overall health, and 41% strongly agreeing
and 38% agreeing that it made their own jobs easier.

## Limitations

The authors name three. Call logging depended on warmline staff recording each call
consistently, so both undercounting and variable categorisation are likely. The data capture
only what had happened by the end of the call, with no follow-up on whether triaged patients
arrived or whether advice was acted on. Categories are broad and single-valued, so a call
touching several issues is filed under one.

Beyond those, this is an uncontrolled before-nothing service description. The staff
evaluation asks 39 people employed by the programme whether the programme's own new service
helped, with no comparison, so the 92% combined agreement on improved patient health carries
no weight as an outcome. No patient outcome is measured at all.

## Methodology

Descriptive analysis of a service log with univariate comparisons on two questions the
authors judged operationally important, who called and which calls concerned urgent needs.
There is no sampling frame beyond every call the staff recorded, and no denominator of
patients or episodes against which call volume could be turned into a rate of anything. Call
counts per unit of population or per patient-month of ART are not recoverable from what is
reported, since the cohort roughly tripled across the observation window and the monthly
call counts are not aligned to it.
