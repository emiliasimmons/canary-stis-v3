The Rakai STD Control for AIDS Prevention Study, the community-randomised trial that tested
whether population-level STI control lowers HIV-1 incidence, reported in the Lancet in 1999.
Ten clusters of four to seven contiguous villages in Rakai District, Uganda, were randomised
within three blocks to home-based mass antibiotic treatment (azithromycin 1 g, ciprofloxacin
250 mg, metronidazole 2 g, plus benzathine penicillin for TRUST-positives) or to
anthelmintic, iron-folate, and vitamins. Residents aged 15-59 were visited every 10 months,
interviewed, sampled, and given directly observed treatment irrespective of symptoms or test
results. Results cover baseline plus two follow-up rounds (20 months), the point at which the
Data Safety and Monitoring Board judged the primary endpoint met. Extracted text at
`raw/10.1016/s0140-6736(98)06439-3`.

## Relevance to project

This is the negative control on the project's central mechanism, and the strongest single
bound on how much HIV incidence reduction STI treatment alone can deliver in a mature
generalised epidemic. It sits in the same population the model calibrates to, so its numbers
are directly usable rather than transferred.

Bound on the direct-treatment pathway. With mass treatment reaching over 80% of eligible
residents every 10 months, HIV-1 incidence was 1.5 per 100 person-years in both arms
(101 seroconversions in 6611 person-years intervention, 89 in 6056 control; adjusted rate
ratio 0.97, 95% CI 0.81-1.16). Any simulated POC arm that produces a large HIV incidence
drop from STI clearance alone, in an epidemic of this maturity, is inconsistent with this
result. The authors' own population attributable fraction gives the ceiling: complete control
of syphilis, trichomoniasis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, and chancroid would cut HIV acquisition
by about 17%, with a further ~20% attributable to bacterial vaginosis and HSV-2 ulcers, which
neither arm of this project's model treats.

Calibration targets for Rakai, mid-1990s, adults 15-49/59. Baseline HIV-1 prevalence 15.9%
(16.1% intervention, 15.6% control; 18.6% women, 13.3% men). HIV-1 incidence 1.5 per 100
person-years overall, 1.3 in rounds 1-2 and 1.8 in rounds 2-3, 1.6 female and 1.4 male, 0.8 at
ages 15-19, 1.9-2.0 at 20-29, 1.4-1.6 at 30-59. In HIV-discordant married couples incidence
was 10.7 (intervention) and 8.2 (control) per 100 person-years. Baseline STI prevalence in the
control arm: syphilis 9.4% (TRUST confirmed by TPHA), trichomoniasis 24.0% (culture, women),
bacterial vaginosis 51.2%, and in a random subsample aged 15-29 tested by ligase chain
reaction, gonorrhoea 1.1% and chlamydia 2.2%. HSV-2 seroprevalence at ages 15-29 was 60.9% in
women and 31.2% in men.

Symptom-pathway calibration, which is the door the POC arm replaces. Reported STI symptoms
were common but weakly linked to treatable infection: 5-9% reported genital ulcer, discharge,
or dysuria in the previous 10 months, current symptoms under 4%, and of those symptomatic
between rounds only 16-20% obtained treatment at a project or government clinic. Of 207 ulcer
swabs, 43.4% were HSV-2, 3.9% Treponema pallidum, 2.9% Haemophilus ducreyi, and 51.1%
negative for all three. About 10% of HIV acquisition was attributable to reported symptoms.
These are usable priors for care-seeking probability given symptoms and for the fraction of
syndromic presentations that a bacterial etiological panel would actually catch.

What it cannot supply. It is not evidence about point-of-care diagnostics, targeting, or
network-position identification: the intervention was untargeted mass treatment on a 10-month
cycle, so it says nothing about how much of a POC effect would come from treating the right
people sooner. Neither arm had ART or PrEP.

## Key points

Treatment reduced STI burden without touching HIV. At 20 months, syphilis prevalence was
5.6% versus 6.8% (prevalence ratio 0.80, 0.71-0.89) and trichomoniasis 9.3% versus 14.4%
(0.59, 0.38-0.91). Trichomonas incidence was 4.8 versus 9.1 per 100 person-years (0.52,
0.35-0.79). Gonorrhoea and chlamydia prevalence fell in the intervention arm and rose in the
control, but the arms did not differ significantly at follow-up. In the postpartum substudy,
where exposure to treatment was tightest, trichomoniasis was 5.3% versus 17.4% (0.28,
0.14-0.58), gonorrhoea 1.0% versus 2.1% (0.50, 0.27-0.91), chlamydia 1.2% versus 3.5% (0.32,
0.14-0.71), and bacterial vaginosis 39.1% versus 52.8% (0.74, 0.67-0.81); HIV incidence in
these women was still no lower (3.6 versus 2.7 per 100 person-years during pregnancy).

The contrast with Mwanza is the finding. Mwanza's syndromic-management trial reported an
adjusted risk ratio of 0.62 (0.45-0.85) against Rakai's 0.97 (0.81-1.16), a difference the
authors test at p<0.01. Baseline STI prevalences were similar across the two sites. The
difference the authors point to is epidemic maturity: HIV prevalence 15.9% and incidence 1.5
per 100 person-years in Rakai against 4.1% and 0.6-0.9 in Mwanza. Their reading is that in an
established epidemic, most acquisition happens on partnerships where the STI cofactor is not
the binding constraint.

Persistence despite intensive treatment. Trichomoniasis in the intervention arm fell from
24.0% to 9.3% over 20 months and did not approach elimination, with reinfection from
untreated refusers (about 10%), newcomers, and outside partners. Bacterial vaginosis stayed
near 47% despite metronidazole.

## Limitations

Twenty months and two follow-up rounds is a short window for a cofactor effect that acts
through prevalence rather than through a single treatment event, and the 10-month dosing
interval leaves most of each interval untreated. Ethically mandated referral of symptomatic
control participants dilutes the contrast, though the authors argue the low treatment uptake
(under 20% of symptomatic people) limits how much. The trial was single-masked, and about
58% of the minority who guessed their assignment guessed right, close to chance. Gonorrhoea
and chlamydia were measured only in a random subsample aged 15-29 because of assay cost, so
those arm comparisons rest on roughly 1000 tests per arm per round and several cluster-adjusted
CIs could not be estimated. Follow-up was 73-75%, with losses skewed young. Untreatable
contributors, HSV-2 and bacterial vaginosis, were left in place in both arms, and the authors
name that as a candidate explanation for the null.

## Methodology

Randomisation was by cluster within three blocks defined by projected HIV prevalence, with
clusters drawn to enclose sexual networks and limit reintroduction. All inference used the
cluster as the unit: rate ratios as geometric means of cluster-pair ratios with t-based CIs,
and Poisson regression comparing observed to expected seroconversions per cluster, adjusted
for sex, age band, marital status, religion (a proxy for circumcision and alcohol), condom
ever-use, partner number, partner distance, and transactional sex. The STI analysis was
deliberately conservative: it reports prevalence in everyone consenting at each round,
including new migrants and prior refusers who never had the chance to be treated, so it
measures community-level exposure rather than per-protocol cure. Diagnostics were laboratory
grade for the era: ligase chain reaction on first-catch urine for gonorrhoea and chlamydia,
InPouch culture for trichomonas, Nugent-scored gram stain for bacterial vaginosis, TRUST with
TPHA and FTA-ABS confirmation for syphilis, and multiplex PCR on ulcer swabs.
