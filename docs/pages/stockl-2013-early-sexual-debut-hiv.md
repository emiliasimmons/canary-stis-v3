Systematic review (PubMed to January 2012, plus African Index Medicus and Google Scholar) of quantitative studies in sub-Saharan Africa relating age at first sex to biologically confirmed HIV infection in women from the general population. Twenty-five studies from 128 screened full texts, mostly cross-sectional, across South Africa, Zimbabwe, Tanzania, Cameroon, Kenya, Rwanda, Malawi, Nigeria, Ghana, and a four-city study (Cotonou, Yaoundé, Kisumu, Ndola). Extracted text and tables sit in `raw/10.1111/aji.12043`.

## Relevance to project

Structural justification for keeping debut age in the network model as a marker rather than a mechanism, and a bound on how much of women's early HIV risk the model should try to route through behaviour.

Two uses. First, the age at which women enter the sexual network is a real determinant of HIV acquisition, so the model's debut-age distribution is not a cosmetic detail: in the five studies with samples above 3000 the unadjusted association was significant in every case, with odds ratios in the range 1.7 to 2.4 for debut under 15 to 18 against later reference categories. Second, and more usefully as a negative result, the review cannot attribute that risk to any mechanism the model represents. Adjusting for lifetime partner numbers, condom use, and STI presence removed the association in four of eight studies and left it in the other four. Adjusting for duration of sexual activity (two Zimbabwe studies) left it intact. Adjusting for partner risk (two studies) left it intact. So the project should not expect its risk-structured network to reproduce an early-debut effect by construction, and should not tune partner-count or partner-age mixing to manufacture one.

Not a calibration target. The review reports no incidence, no prevalence by debut age on a common scale, and no harmonised effect size: the exposure definitions run from "under 14" to "under 19" with reference categories as far out as 25 or later, so the odds ratios in Table II are not poolable. It gives a direction and a rough magnitude, not a number to fit.

Nothing here bears on STI diagnostics or on the SOC/POC arm contrast. Its bearing is on how the underlying network and its age-at-entry are specified.

## Key points

Of 26 unadjusted results in 23 articles, 13 were significant. Restricting to studies scoring at least medium quality, five of seven were significant. No study in the review scored high quality (12 to 14 on a 14-point GATE-derived scale); seven scored medium, 13 low, five very low.

The four candidate pathways and what the review found. Longer exposure time from an earlier start: not supported, the association survived adjustment for duration of sexual activity in both studies that tried it. Later risky behaviour: split evenly, four of eight. Higher-risk partners: not supported, the association survived in both of the two studies that adjusted for partner age difference or partner HIV risk. Biological susceptibility from reproductive-tract immaturity or genital trauma: untestable here, because no study in the review measured it.

Socio-demographic adjustment alone (three studies) never removed the association.

The authors' reading is that where the effect survives behavioural adjustment, risk is concentrated at or near first sex itself, through trauma or partner serostatus, and that forced first sex may be the common determinant behind both early debut and later high-risk patterns.

## Limitations

Cross-sectional designs dominate, so causal direction is not established; the few cohorts had short follow-up or enrolled women already sexually active. Age at first sex is self-reported retrospectively, exposed to recall and social desirability bias, and few studies reported measures against it. English-language, peer-reviewed only, and abstracts alone were screened, which plausibly under-samples null results. Only women who consented to HIV testing appear in any of the samples.

The heterogeneous exposure definitions are the binding limitation for quantitative use. Comparing debut before 15 against debut after 20 or 25, when most women in these populations debuted between 16 and 20, inflates contrasts relative to a study using a 15-year split.
