Source: [wilkinson-2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2016.04.015)

A methods guideline, not an empirical study. The Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation commissioned NICE International to write a "reference case" for the economic evaluations it funds in low- and middle-income countries, after a review found wide variation in quality among them: over a third of BMGF-funded evaluations since 2000 did not report a time horizon and half did not explain their choice of comparator. The result is eleven principles, each with a set of methodological specifications and reporting standards. The eleven principles and their specifications are in the two tables at the end.

## Relevance to project

A design reference for any cost-effectiveness arm of this project, and nothing else. It supplies no parameters, no calibration targets, and no epidemiological numbers.

Where it would bind concretely if the POC diagnostic panel is costed:

- Comparator (principle 2): current practice is the required comparator, with best-supportive non-interventional care as an additional analysis. The SOC arm running syndromic management is exactly the "current practice" comparator this asks for, so the existing two-arm structure already satisfies the minimum.
- Outcome measure (principle 4): DALYs averted or QALYs gained, not a disease-specific measure. HIV infections averted, the natural output of the pathway analysis, would be a technical-efficiency measure only and would not let a decision maker trade the POC panel against investments in other disease areas.
- Time horizon and discount rate (principle 6): lifetime horizon in the first instance, 3% annual discount on both costs and effects, with sensitivity analyses at other rates and, beyond a 30-year horizon, explicitly at lower rates. Relevant because HIV infections averted by STI treatment accrue their benefit decades after the averted transmission.
- Costs (principle 5): unit costs must reflect what rollout would cost, not what the study setting paid, and must include donated inputs and out-of-pocket spending. For a POC panel this means the delivered price per test rather than a trial procurement price.
- Constraints (principle 10): budget impact reported separately from cost-effectiveness, and human capital treated as a real constraint in its own right. A test-and-treat panel replacing syndromic management at the same care-seeking door consumes clinic staff time, which this asks to be quantified as staff needed against staff available.

What it cannot do: it takes no position on a cost-effectiveness threshold value, gives no LMIC unit costs, and does not say whether a static or dynamic model is appropriate, deferring that to the analyst.

## Key points

The reference case is principle-based rather than prescriptive, which is deliberate: it is meant to serve global funders, national decision makers, and researchers at once, and a prescriptive rule set of the NICE kind only works when the decision maker and affected population are fixed. Analysts who cannot follow a principle are asked to document why rather than to comply.

On thresholds, the paper takes a supply-side position: the cost-effectiveness threshold should reflect the rate at which the health system currently converts additional money into health, so that a new intervention with a large budget impact displaces more valuable activity and in effect faces a lower threshold. It contrasts this with demand-side conceptions of the threshold, which ask instead how large the health budget should be.

## Limitations

No empirical content and no worked example. The eleven principles are stated at a level that leaves most of the analytic difficulty to the analyst: principle 9 asks for structural, source, and precision uncertainty to be characterised without saying how, and principle 11 asks for equity implications to be explored while noting only that "various mechanisms" exist. Published 2016, so the threshold literature it cites as in press has since moved on.
