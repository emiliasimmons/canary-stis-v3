Secondary analysis of three home-based counselling and testing (HBCT) rounds nested in the
KEMRI/CDC health and demographic surveillance system in Gem, Siaya County, western Kenya:
28 688 people aged 15-64 in 2011, 17 021 in 2012, 16 772 in 2016. Participants were tracked
across rounds by surveillance ID, so a retrospective open cohort could be assembled from
a service-delivery testing programme. Extracted text sits in
`../raw/10.1016/s2352-3018(18)30025-0/content.md`.

## Relevance to project

A comparator epidemic, not a calibration target. Siaya is a second high-burden East African
setting measured on the same instrument the [Rakai](../tags/rakai.md) cohort uses, an open
population-based cohort with repeat serosurveys, and it gives the scale of HIV incidence
decline attainable from ART and circumcision alone, without any STI-directed component.
Incidence roughly halved over five years, from 11.1 (95% CI 9.1-13.1) to 5.7 (4.6-6.9) per
1000 person-years, while ART coverage nationally went 7% (2006) to 57% (2016) and
circumcision prevalence in men 15-49 in western Kenya went 45% (2008) to 72% (2014). That
sets the bar an STI point-of-care arm would have to move against: the residual 5.7 per 1000
person-years is the incidence a mature ART-plus-circumcision programme leaves behind, and
it is nearly six times the elimination threshold of 1 per 1000 person-years.

Two figures are usable as background rates in a Uganda-adjacent parameterisation. Age and
sex adjusted incidence over the whole period was 8.2 per 1000 person-years in women and
girls and 6.4 in men and boys, both aged 15-64, with the peak in ages 25-44 for both sexes
and an earlier peak in women (crude 8.9 per 1000 person-years at 15-24 in women against 3.2
in men of the same age). Prevalence at cohort enrolment was 14.6% overall (5213/35 733),
17% in women (3554/20 893) and 11% in men (1659/14 840).

The spousal results bear on the model's partnership structure. Having an HIV-positive
spouse not on ART carried an adjusted odds ratio of 8.8 (5.2-15.2) for incident HIV against
an HIV-negative spouse; a spouse on ART carried 9.7 (5.3-17.7), statistically
indistinguishable. Denominators are small (23/170 and 22/126 respectively), and the authors
read the null ART effect as confounding by indication, since ART was allocated to advanced
disease under CD4 thresholds. The model implements no partner-notification pathway, so
these enter as evidence that serodiscordant marriage concentrates risk, not as a treatment
effect.

The paper does not measure any STI other than HIV, and cannot inform STI prevalence, the
STI cofactor on HIV acquisition, or care-seeking for STI symptoms.

## Key points

- Prevalence in 15-64 year olds was flat across rounds: 15% (2011), 12% (2012), 15% (2016).
  Prevalence in 15-34 year olds fell, 11% to 9% crude and 11% to 8% age- and sex-adjusted,
  against 20% in an adjacent area in 2003-04. The authors read the younger band as the
  one tracking recent incidence and the full band as confounded by falling AIDS mortality.
- Awareness of positive status among those infected rose from 44% (1875/4300) in 2011 to
  85% (1956/2312) in 2016.
- Circumcision was protective for prevalent HIV in men (adjusted OR 0.6, 0.5-0.8) but not
  significantly for incident HIV (crude OR 0.7, 0.4-1.1, 18/1211 events). Women reporting a
  circumcised main partner had lower incidence (adjusted OR 0.5, 0.2-1.0).
- Regular condom use with all partners was associated with higher incidence (adjusted OR
  4.2, 1.4-12.6, 10/91), read as condoms being reached for in known-risk partnerships.
- Modelled county-level estimates were well above the survey: Spectrum put Siaya prevalence
  at 25% in ages 15-49 for 2016 and antenatal-clinic data at 17%, against 14%
  (1796/12 772) measured in the HBCT survey.

## Limitations

Participants were not randomly sampled; the surveys were run as a service to link people to
care. Only 12 606 of 30 520 HIV-negative enrollees (41%) returned for a second round, and
retention rose steeply with age (26-27% at 15-24, 58-70% at 55-64), so the young end of the
incidence estimate rests on the thinnest follow-up. Loss to follow-up was not associated
with HIV prevalence at enrolment, which is the authors' argument that it is not
differential on the outcome.

Self-reported positive status counted as infection where no test result existed, and this
choice drives the headline. Excluding self-report drops age-adjusted incidence from 7.4 to
4.2 per 1000 person-years overall, and the 2012-16 estimate from 5.5 to 2.4, because
self-report became more common as awareness rose. The direction of decline holds either
way; the level does not. Seroconversion dates were imputed at the midpoint between the last
negative and first positive test, and the 2012-16 interval spans four years, so the
person-time attribution inside that window is coarse. Viral suppression was not measured,
and risk-factor variables were heavily missing in the 2011 round.
