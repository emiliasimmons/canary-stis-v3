Cross-sectional behavioural survey with linked HIV serology from 12 communities in
Manicaland province, rural Zimbabwe, interviewed July 1998 to January 2000 (forestry
plantations, tea and coffee estates, subsistence farming areas, small towns, roadside
trading centres). 4,331 men aged 17-54 and 5,149 women aged 15-44 enrolled, 77 percent of
those eligible; after excluding inconsistent responses, 4,138 men and 4,948 women were
analysed. Sensitive questions used an informal confidential voting interview in 75 percent
of literate respondents' interviews. Extracted text at
`../raw/10.1111/j.1728-4465.2007.00111.x/content.md`.

## Relevance to project

Two things this supplies for the sexual-network layer, both as priors rather than
calibration targets, since the site is Zimbabwe and not Rakai.

Sexual debut timing: median age at first sex 19 for men, 18 for women, with 25th/75th
percentiles of 17/21 for men and 17/20 for women, over the whole sample. Cohort trend is
directional: men aged 20-24 at survey had a rate ratio of first sex of 1.89 against the
45-54 reference (debut moving earlier over 30 years), while women aged 15-19 had 0.77
against the 40-44 reference (debut moving later recently). This bounds a plausible debut
distribution for a generalised-epidemic African setting and says the debut age should not
be treated as static across birth cohorts.

The structural claim is the more useful one for the model's risk stratification: early
debut is a marker for a durably higher-activity network position, not an independent
biological risk. Among men whose first sex was premarital (93 percent of sexually
experienced men), debut before 18 predicted +4.04 lifetime partners, and +2.32 after
adjusting for years of sexual activity, plus more partners in the past month (+0.20), more
sex acts in the past two weeks (+0.64), and more concurrent relationships (+0.28). Among
men whose first sex was within marriage (7 percent), none of these associations held.
Women show the same split, weaker in level: +1.47 lifetime partners for premarital early
debut, +0.47 for within-marriage. This supports assigning risk-group membership on
sustained partner acquisition rather than on debut age, and supports the marital/premarital
distinction as the thing that carries the signal.

The HIV association follows from that. Women with debut at 12-16 had OR 1.49 for HIV
against debut at 21+ (univariate), 1.60 adjusted for years of sexual activity, and 1.14
(p=0.23, not significant) once lifetime partner count entered the model. Men with early
debut had OR 0.75 (p=0.02) univariate, i.e. lower HIV odds, which the authors attribute to
their higher condom use. Sample HIV prevalence at survey was 19 percent in men 17-54 and 26
percent in women 15-44. A model that already routes HIV risk through partner numbers should
therefore not add a separate debut-age hazard on top; doing so would double-count.

The source does not measure STIs and gives nothing on diagnostics or care-seeking, so it
bears only on the network and HIV-acquisition side of the model.

## Key points

- Median debut 18 (women) and 19 (men) sits mid-range internationally and high for African
  settings, so the epidemic's size in Manicaland is not explained by early debut.
- Determinants retained in the multivariate Cox model differ by sex. Men: traditional-church
  membership (HR 1.39), minority tribe (1.25), student status (0.61), unemployed (0.86).
  Women: student (0.20), professional or skilled (0.50), daily radio listening (0.80),
  completed primary education (0.70, only in ages 15-19), minority tribe (1.50), relocation
  from a rural (1.40) or urban (1.30) area.
- Early-debut individuals of both sexes reported more consistent condom use (men premarital
  +0.43 log-odds, women premarital +0.56), so risk and protective behaviour move together.
- The education effect strengthened across cohorts: univariate rate ratio for completed
  primary education among women was 0.85 in the 40-44 cohort and 0.42 in the 20-24 cohort.

## Limitations

Cross-sectional, so event ordering is unrecoverable: whether early sex precedes leaving
school or follows it cannot be settled here. Socioeconomic status is measured at interview,
not at debut, and 23 percent of men and 19 percent of women had relocated in the interval,
which is why the determinants analysis is restricted to respondents under 25. Debut age is
self-reported and subject to recall and social-desirability bias; the authors flag that the
protective male OR could be an artefact of low-activity men understating their debut age.
The male long-term trend toward earlier debut could also reflect selective AIDS mortality
removing early-debut men from older cohorts. Urban Zimbabwean data (Pettifor et al. 2004)
found the debut-HIV association in young women survives adjustment for subsequent
behaviour, so the "fully mediated by partner count" conclusion may not generalise across
settings, including to Rakai.
