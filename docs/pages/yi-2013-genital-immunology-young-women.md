A six-page invited review (Am J Reprod Immunol 69 Suppl. 1: 74-79) from Kaul's group in
Toronto and Nairobi, arguing that the female and adolescent skew of HIV incidence in
endemic settings is only partly behavioural, and that mucosal biology at the site of
exposure supplies the rest. It reviews other people's studies and adds one previously
unpublished comparison (Fig. 1). Extracted text sits at `raw/10.1111/aji.12035`.

## Relevance to project

Structural justification, not a source of fitted numbers. It supports three modelling
choices the project makes and would otherwise have to defend from first principles:

- A **sex- and age-dependent per-act HIV acquisition probability**, with young women above
  older women and above men. The review gives mechanism (cervical ectopy, pro-inflammatory
  genital cytokines, higher CCR5 density) but no per-act probability by age, so it cannot
  set a prior. The one quantity that could seed a relative bound is the CCR5 comparison:
  median 81.5% of endocervical CD4+ T cells CCR5-positive in 27 HIV-uninfected Kenyan
  women versus 41.7% of foreskin CD4+ T cells in 46 HIV-uninfected Ugandan men
  (Mann-Whitney P < 0.0001). That is a receptor-expression ratio in two separate cohorts,
  not a transmission-risk ratio, and should not be mapped onto one.
- A **biological STI-HIV cofactor** distinct from shared behaviour. HSV-2 seropositivity is
  cited at a threefold increase in HIV acquisition (meta-analysis of longitudinal studies,
  both sexes). None of the four bacterial STIs the model carries gets its own cofactor
  number here, so the paper is a mechanism reference for the cofactor, not a calibration
  target for it.
- **Age-disparate partnering** as an acquisition risk for the younger partner, given HIV is
  lifelong and more prevalent in older people. Qualitative here, but it is the standard
  argument behind an age-mixing term in the network.

Also relevant to the model's treatment of reinfection: the review notes the absence of
protective acquired immunity to *C. trachomatis* in humans as one reason chlamydia burden
concentrates in young women, alongside behaviour.

Nothing here bears on Rakai calibration directly, on diagnostic performance, or on the POC
arm. Two Rakai-adjacent numbers appear only as citations of other work (foreskin surface
area and HIV acquisition; per-coital-act transmission in discordant couples), and should be
taken from those sources rather than from this review.

## Key points

- Women are roughly 60% of HIV infections in endemic countries; women aged 15-24 are cited
  as eight times more likely than same-age men to be infected (Kenya AIDS Indicator Survey
  2007).
- Surface area argument: the cervico-vaginal mucosa is much larger than the penis and
  foreskin, and cohorts finding equal male and female per-act risk were generally in
  uncircumcised populations. Vaginal mucosal surface area exceeds endocervical by more than
  15-fold, which is why the review declines to localise acquisition to the endocervix
  despite macaque data pointing there.
- Cervical ectopy (columnar epithelium extending onto the ectocervix) is normal in
  adolescents and regresses by squamous metaplasia with maturity, so a larger share of the
  exposed surface in young women is the single-cell epithelium that is more susceptible to
  HIV, chlamydia, and gonorrhoea.
- Adolescent genital secretions carry more pro-inflammatory cytokines and more inflammatory
  cells in cervicovaginal lavage; genital inflammation is prospectively associated with HIV
  acquisition.
- A luteal-phase window of vulnerability is argued from macaque SHIV challenge and from
  injectable progestins doubling HIV acquisition in one prospective cohort (Heffron 2012).
- Female rectal exposure during heterosexual anal sex is raised as an under-counted
  contributor, with no quantification.

## Limitations

A narrative review with no stated search strategy, no pooling, and no grading, published in
2012-2013, so both the 33 million global estimate and the hormonal-contraception evidence
are superseded. The mechanistic claims are consistently plausible-mechanism claims, not
effect estimates. The CCR5 figure compares two different anatomical compartments in two
different country cohorts assayed by the same group, with no adjustment and no confidence
interval; it establishes a direction, not a magnitude. Several key statements rest on
macaque challenge experiments whose dose and route do not resemble human exposure.
