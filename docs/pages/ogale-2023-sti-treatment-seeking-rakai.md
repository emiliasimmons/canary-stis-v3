Source: [ogale-2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgph.0001626)

A cross-sectional analysis nested in the STI Prevalence Study (STIPS), itself nested in the
Rakai Community Cohort Study. All consenting adults aged 18-49 in two RCCS communities, one
inland agrarian and one Lake Victoria fishing site, were surveyed May-October 2019 (n =
1,825) and tested for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomoniasis and syphilis alongside the
routine HIV screen. Everyone reporting an STI-related symptom in the previous six months
was asked whether they did anything about it and where they went.

## Relevance to project

This is the empirical basis for the symptomatic care-seeking door, the point where the POC
arm swaps an etiological panel in for syndromic management. Two numbers set that door's
size in the model. Of adults reporting symptoms in the past six months, 57% sought
treatment anywhere and 48% (457/956) reached a government or private clinic; the remaining
43% sought nothing at all. Among clinic-goers, 86% of visits were to a clinic that would
plausibly host a POC panel (58% private, 28% government), with 9% going to a pharmacy or
drug store and 3% to a traditional healer, so a POC rollout confined to clinics reaches
roughly half of symptomatic adults, not all of them.

Symptom prevalence itself is the upstream gate: 53% of all participants (962/1,825)
reported any STI symptom in six months, 34% of males (290/860) and 70% of females
(672/964), with 63% of those symptomatic in the past seven days. The female figure includes
substantial non-STI symptomatology (the authors flag urinary tract infection), so it is a
ceiling on the symptomatic-presentation rate rather than a calibration target for STI
incidence.

The paper also bounds what syndromic management achieves. Among symptomatic adults who
reported previously seeking clinic care, about a third tested positive for a curable STI at
survey (33% of males, 45/136; 31% of females, 98/321), no better than those who had not
sought clinic care (male PRR 1.23, 95% CI 0.86-1.75; female PRR 0.92, 0.74-1.15). Whether
that reflects reinfection or ineffective treatment is unresolved here, but either way it
supports modelling syndromic management as leaving a large residual burden, and it is the
headroom the POC arm is meant to close. Prevalence among symptomatic adults was CT 11%,
NG 10%, TV 13%, active syphilis 7%.

Not usable as a partner-notification input. Participants were asked whether they sought
treatment for a partner, but no result on that is reported.

## Key points

Care-seeking did not differ by sex, either for any treatment (55% of males, 57% of females)
or for clinic treatment (47% and 48%), which the authors call unexpected against the wider
Ugandan literature and the 2016 DHS.

Community type mattered for men only: males in the fishing community reached a clinic less
often than inland males (43% vs 55%, PRR 0.78, 95% CI 0.61-1.00), with no such gap among
females. Fishing communities in Rakai carry higher HIV burden and higher-risk sexual
behaviour, so the group with most to gain from treatment is the one least likely to present.

Symptom count drove clinic attendance more than any sociodemographic factor. Reporting 2-4
symptoms in six months versus one gave PRR 1.72 (1.34-2.21) in males and 1.30 (1.02-1.65)
in females; five or more gave 1.73 (1.36-2.20) in females. Specific symptoms differed
sharply in pull: genital discharge in males more than doubled clinic attendance (PRR 2.16,
1.73-2.69) and painful urination raised it (1.55, 1.22-1.98), while genital ulcer, lower
abdominal pain and pain during intercourse showed no male association. Genital warts pushed
attendance down (0.57, 0.37-0.90 overall).

HIV status was not associated with clinic care-seeking, despite 86% of participants living
with HIV being on ART and therefore already in regular contact with the health system.

## Limitations

Two communities only, both rural, so the estimates sit below national figures (2016 DHS put
clinic-seeking at about 70%) and are not a national parameter. The study was underpowered
for sex-by-community-type contrasts, which is exactly the stratification the fishing-male
result rests on.

Symptom history and care-seeking are self-reported over a six-month recall window, with
recall and social-desirability bias both plausible, and the survey was administered by RHSP
staff, which could inflate reported clinic attendance. Treatment locations were not named
or mapped, so drug shops may have been reported as private clinics, blurring the 58%
private-clinic figure that a POC delivery assumption would lean on.

The analysis groups private and government clinics as adequate care and everything else as
inadequate. The authors note a Ugandan study finding poor STI case management quality in
private clinics and drug shops, so clinic attendance overstates effective treatment, which
in turn means the 48% clinic figure is an upper bound on the syndromic-management coverage
the SOC arm should assume.

Prior care-seeking and current infection are measured at one time point, so the null
association with current STI prevalence carries no temporal ordering and cannot separate
reinfection from treatment failure.
