> Note: summary derived from the abstract only, not the full source.

Randomized controlled trial in Rakai, Uganda, testing whether a patient-selected "care buddy," a household or community support person, improves engagement in pre-antiretroviral HIV care. 1209 pre-ART HIV-infected patients aged 15 and over were randomized to standard of care (n=604) or a patient-selected care buddy (n=605) and followed at 6 and 12 months. Outcomes were adherence to CD4-monitoring clinic visits, HIV disease progression, and self-reported conduct of daily life, assessed by incidence and prevalence rate ratios in intent-to-treat and as-treated analyses.

## Relevance to project

A design reference for the care-seeking and retention side, not a calibration input. The modelled arms act at the symptomatic care-seeking door and do not represent peer or household adherence support, so nothing here parameterizes the current model. The trial is evidence on how much a social-support layer moves pre-ART engagement: in this population the effect on the operative outcomes was small, which argues against expecting large adherence gains from an analogous support mechanism if one were added.

The gendered buddy-selection pattern is the transferable behavioural detail. Men chose spouses or partners as buddies far more often than women did (61% vs 30%), consistent with the asymmetry in disclosure and partnership dynamics the sexual-network and care-seeking sources track for this setting.

No progression rate, retention probability, or CD4 trajectory is usable as a target or prior from the abstract.

## Key points

Adherence to CD4-monitoring visits was comparable between arms (adjusted prevalence risk ratio 0.98, 95% CI 0.93 to 1.04).

ART eligibility over follow-up was comparable between arms (adjPRR 1.00, 95% CI 0.77 to 1.31).

Good conduct of daily life was higher in the care-buddy arm (adjPRR 1.08, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.13, P=0.001), the one outcome that moved.

Men selected spouses or partners as buddies more than women did (61% vs 30%, P<0.0001).

22% of care-buddy participants discontinued use of the buddy.

The authors conclude buddies improved conduct of daily life but had no effect on disease progression and only limited effect on clinic-visit adherence.

## Limitations

Abstract only: randomization detail, the definition and measurement of "conduct of daily life," and the as-treated results were not read. The pre-ART framing predates universal treatment, so the clinic-engagement outcomes are specific to a monitoring-only care stage that no longer matches current guidelines.
