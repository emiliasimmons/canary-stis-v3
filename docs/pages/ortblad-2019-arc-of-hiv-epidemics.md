A narrative review in *Current Opinion in HIV and AIDS* summarizing the five universal
test-and-treat (UTT) cluster-randomized trials run in sub-Saharan Africa between 2012 and
2018, then arguing from their results that HIV epidemics in the region are moving from a
hyperendemic phase into a declining/endemic phase in which incidence re-concentrates in
marginalized core groups the trials' delivery models did not reach. Extracted text and
both trial tables are at `../raw/10.1097/coh.0000000000000569/`.

## Relevance to project

Context and a comparator for the counterfactual the model has to beat, not a source of
parameters.

The concrete use is as a bound on what ART-centred prevention delivered, and therefore on
how much residual incidence a POC STI arm is competing for. In BCPP/YaTsie's intervention
communities, 98% ART coverage and 96% undetectable viral load among HIV-positive
participants still left annualized HIV incidence at 0.59% (5.9 per 1,000 person-years),
against 0.92% in comparison communities. The epidemic-control threshold the review uses is
1 case per 1,000 person-years. So the best-performing UTT trial ended roughly six times
above control with treatment coverage past the 95-95-95 targets. Any claim this project
makes about a POC diagnostic's HIV-prevention impact is a claim about that residual.

The review's mechanistic argument is the reason the project's third pathway (identifying
high-transmission positions in the sexual network) is worth separating from direct STI
treatment at all: the authors attribute the residual to a mismatch between the social
networks that carry health information and the sexual networks that carry transmission,
so coverage gains land on people who are not the high-frequency transmitters. That is a
structural justification for a risk-structured network with heterogeneous partner-change
rates rather than a homogeneous population, and for evaluating an intervention by who it
reaches rather than by aggregate coverage.

For Rakai specifically, the review reports RCCS results from four Lake Victoria fishing
communities where home-based testing and combination prevention scaled up from 2013:
between 2001 and 2016 ART coverage and viral suppression more than doubled and HIV
incidence halved, yet incidence stayed above the control threshold and persistent viremia
was documented in almost 15% of community members attending at least two clinic visits.
Incidence there was associated with low education, previous marriage, two or more sexual
partners, and self-reported genital ulcer disease. The genital-ulcer association is the
only direct STI-HIV link the review carries, and it is a self-reported covariate in a
conference abstract, so it supports the coinfection premise qualitatively and cannot serve
as a cofactor estimate.

Not usable as a calibration target. The RCCS figures are secondhand, partly cited as
unpublished data and a CROI abstract, and the fishing communities are a high-prevalence
subset rather than the 30-community RCCS frame the project plans to calibrate to.

## Key points

Four of the five trials measured HIV incidence and only two found a significant reduction.
ANRS TasP (South Africa, 22 clusters, 30% HIV prevalence) saw 2.11 vs 2.27 per 100
person-years, p=0.89, with no significant change in ART coverage (53% vs 53%). SEARCH
(Uganda and Kenya, 32 clusters) raised ART coverage among baseline HIV-infected persons
not on ART from 40% to 80% and undetectable viral load from 68% to 79%, but incidence was
0.73% vs 0.76%, p=0.60. BCPP/YaTsie (Botswana, 30 clusters) is the clean positive: 0.59%
vs 0.92%, p=0.04. PopART (Zambia and South Africa, 21 clusters) gave the inverted result:
the more intensive Arm A reached 72% undetectable viral load with incidence 1.45 per 100
person-years (p=0.51 vs Arm C's 1.55), while the less intensive Arm B showed no
significant viral-load gain (68%) and incidence 1.06 per 100 person-years (p=0.006).
MaxART (eSwatini, clinic-randomized) measured no incidence outcome.

Coverage gains and incidence reductions decoupled. SEARCH and BCPP both improved coverage
significantly; only BCPP moved incidence. The authors propose a non-linear relationship
between ART roll-out and population-level effectiveness: early treatment goes to advanced
disease and antenatal clients who transmit little, the middle of the roll-out captures
sexually active transmitters and yields real benefit, and the tail plateaus because the
remaining untreated are the hard-to-engage high-frequency transmitters.

Both null-incidence trials (ANRS TasP, SEARCH) also ran community or home-based outreach
in their comparison arms, which the authors flag as narrowing the contrast.

## Limitations

Three of the five trials are cited as conference abstracts (SEARCH, BCPP/YaTsie, MaxART at
IAS 2018; PopART at CROI 2019), and the Rakai incidence trend is unpublished data plus a
CROI abstract, so the numbers above are as reported in this review and were not
peer-reviewed at the time.

Trial comparability is weak by the authors' own account: linkage-to-care support,
adherence support, the mix of co-delivered prevention interventions, and the comparison-arm
intervention all differed across trials, and every one of the six countries moved its
national ART initiation threshold mid-trial (WHO went from CD4 350 to 500 in 2013 and to
treat-all in 2015), so control arms converged on immediate ART by the end.

The core-group re-concentration argument is a hypothesis the review advances, supported by
associations from cross-sectional and cohort analyses. No trial measured transmission
sources directly, so the claim that services miss high-frequency transmitters is inferred
from the coverage-incidence discrepancy rather than observed.
