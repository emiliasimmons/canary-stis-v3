Narrative review from CAPRISA in the *Journal of the International AIDS Society*, arguing
that adolescent girls and young women (AGYW, defined as ages 15-24) are a key population
for HIV epidemic control in southern Africa, and cataloguing the behavioural, structural
and biological explanations for their excess risk. It reports no new data of its own,
adapting its figures from the 2012 South African national survey (Shisana et al.) and a
KwaZulu-Natal school survey (Abdool Karim et al. 2014). Extracted content sits in
`../raw/10.7448/ias.18.2.19408/`.

## Relevance to project

Context and a structural justification for the age-mixing and risk-heterogeneity choices
in the sexual network, not a source of calibration targets: every number is South African
or KwaZulu-Natal, and canary-stis calibrates to Rakai, Uganda.

Three uses. First, it motivates an age-disparate partnership channel: the review names
intergenerational partnerships (young women with men 5-plus years older) as the most
convincing single driver of the age-sex incidence gap, with the mechanism being the
higher HIV prevalence of the older male partner plus reduced ability to negotiate condom
use. Second, it supports treating STI cofactor effects as age- and sex-structured rather
than uniform: bacterial and viral STIs are both more prevalent in young women than young
men, and the KwaZulu-Natal school survey it draws on found HSV-2 prevalence more than
three-fold higher in female than male students, with the HSV-2 age curve tracking the HIV
one. Recent HSV-2 acquisition is flagged as conferring the largest increment in HIV
susceptibility. Third, it bounds what the POC arm can plausibly claim in this age group:
per-act HIV acquisition risk in young women is described as too high to be explained by
behaviour alone, so a model attributing the AGYW excess entirely to partner mixing will
understate it.

Not usable as a PrEP efficacy prior. The one efficacy figure it quotes (39% for tenofovir
gel, used in an illustrative calculation that rollout to South African women aged 15-24
would avert more than 44,000 infections in a year) is a headline from a single trial
carried forward without its confidence interval.

## Key points

- AGYW aged 15-24 contribute roughly 30% of new HIV infections in southern Africa; in
  South Africa that is about 113,000 infections per year, over four times the number in
  same-age men.
- Women acquire HIV about five to seven years earlier than men, close to sexual debut. In
  one rural KwaZulu-Natal sub-district, 27% of antenatal attendees under 20 were HIV
  positive between 2009 and 2013 (reported as unpublished).
- Risk factors beyond age-disparate partnerships: transactional sex, early sexual debut,
  few years of schooling, food insecurity, loss of a family member, gender-based violence.
  The review argues most of these act partly through raising the value of financial
  transfers from older partners.
- Biological mechanisms offered for the female and the young-female excess: larger
  cervico-vaginal mucosal surface, semen persisting in the genital tract up to three days,
  higher genital immune activation, greater HIV co-receptor expression on cervical than
  foreskin cells, an immature cervix with more exposed susceptible mucosa, and higher
  baseline genital inflammation.
- DMPA (injectable hormonal contraceptive) is cited as raising HIV acquisition risk in
  women 18-24 while lowering it in women 25 and over, from a single study.
- School-based and community-based education programmes improved knowledge, attitudes and
  testing uptake but showed no effect on HIV, STI or pregnancy incidence in the trials
  that measured biological outcomes. Abstinence-only and peer-led formats were ineffective.
- Youth-friendly service interventions raised service uptake; almost none measured
  biological outcomes.
- Conditional cash transfers are the one behavioural-structural approach with positive
  biological signal: 25% lower probability of HIV acquisition over two years in a Lesotho
  trial, and 64% lower HIV prevalence among Malawian schoolgirls receiving transfers
  (baseline infection unmeasured in the latter).

## Limitations

Narrative, not systematic: no search strategy, no inclusion criteria, no pooling. Claims
are asserted with citations rather than weighed, so effect sizes arrive stripped of
intervals and the biological mechanisms are listed as plausible without ranking. Almost
all quantitative content is South African, and the review predates the DREAMS-era
evidence, dapivirine ring results, and injectable PrEP. The PrEP discussion is advocacy
for rollout under 2015 conditions and dated on its own terms. Several key figures trace to
unpublished data or a conference poster.
