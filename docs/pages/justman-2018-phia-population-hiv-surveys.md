Source: [justman-2018](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMp1801934)

A three-page NEJM Perspective describing the Population-based HIV Impact Assessment (PHIA)
Project, launched in 2014 by ICAP at Columbia with PEPFAR funding and ministries of health,
which runs nationally representative household surveys of adults and children in 14 high-
burden countries. The argument is about measurement rather than biology: facility data only
see people already diagnosed, and model projections inherit both that data and their own
assumptions, so directly measured population estimates of incidence, viral load
suppression, and paediatric prevalence are needed to see where a national response is
failing.

## Relevance to project

Context and a sanity bound, not a calibration target. It gives no Rakai-level or
Uganda-specific number, and no STI content at all, so nothing here enters the model
directly. What it supplies is the population-level ART/suppression backdrop that a
calibrated HIV arm should reproduce for the late-2010s East and southern African setting:
across Zimbabwe, Malawi, and Zambia in 2016, viral load suppression (HIV RNA below 1000
copies/mL) among all adults 15+ living with HIV, regardless of whether they knew their
status or reported ART use, was 62%, against an adult HIV prevalence of 12.2% in the three
countries combined. A simulated 2016 suppression fraction far from that range, on the same
denominator of all infected adults rather than those in care, is a signal the treatment
cascade is misparameterised.

Uganda is one of the seven countries with early PHIA results, which makes the PHIA Uganda
report the natural source to pull if national comparators for the Rakai calibration are
wanted later; this piece points at the survey programme without reporting its numbers.

## Key points

Awareness of HIV-positive status, not ART uptake or suppression once diagnosed, is the
binding constraint across all seven early-reporting countries (Zimbabwe, Malawi, Zambia,
Eswatini, Lesotho, Tanzania, Uganda; roughly 200,000 individuals from more than 85,000
households). Men, adolescents, and young adults are consistently the least likely to know
their status, which is the argument for testing programmes targeted at those groups.

Eswatini ran two national surveys, 2011 and 2016, and over that interval HIV incidence fell
44% while population viral load suppression among HIV-positive people aged 18–49
(irrespective of awareness or ART status) roughly doubled, 35% to 71%. That paired movement
is the clearest population-scale statement in the piece of treatment scale-up translating
into incidence decline.

Both the 62% and the 71% figures are close to the UNAIDS 73% suppression target, and the 62%
exceeds the 2014 United States estimate of 49%.

## Limitations

A commentary, not a data paper. The suppression and incidence figures are cited to
conference abstracts (CROI 2017, IAS 2017) rather than to peer-reviewed survey reports, and
they carry no confidence intervals or age-sex breakdowns here. Denominators differ between
the headline numbers (15+ in the three-country figure, 18–49 in the Eswatini figure), so
they are not directly comparable to each other. Anything used quantitatively should be
re-pulled from the country PHIA reports.

Surveys are cross-sectional household samples; men and mobile populations are the groups
hardest to reach and also the groups the piece identifies as least likely to know their
status, so non-response plausibly runs in the same direction as the finding.

## Methodology

Two-stage cluster random sampling for national representativeness, household-based HIV
counselling and testing with results returned in the field and referral to care, blood
specimens processed at designated laboratories, and electronic questionnaire capture
transferred to central servers for real-time quality monitoring. Incidence is measured with
recency assays rather than inferred from a model, which is what separates PHIA from the
Demographic and Health Surveys and AIDS Indicator Surveys that preceded it.
