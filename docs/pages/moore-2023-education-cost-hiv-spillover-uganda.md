Source: [moore-2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/17441692.2023.2221973)

Team-based ethnographic fieldwork carried out in Rakai District between June and August
2019, part of a larger study of how the transition to adulthood shapes HIV vulnerability.
The argument is that Uganda's Universal Primary Education policy, in force since 1997,
removed tuition at government primary schools but left families carrying uniforms,
textbooks, meals and exam fees, and made attendance a legal obligation, so that the search
for school fees pushes household members into arrangements that raise HIV risk.

## Relevance to project

Two uses, both narrow.

The paper reproduces a table of RCCS descriptive statistics for 2016-2018, restricted to the
six study communities, comparing 1,608 respondents in fishing communities against 1,365 in
agrarian and trading communities pooled. Past-year transactional sex, on the same RCCS item
used elsewhere (money, gifts or favours exchanged for sex, asked of the four most recent
partners), was 48.6% in fishing communities against 38.6% in agrarian and trading, splitting
to 71.7% against 52.3% among women and 26.3% against 17.2% among men. Two or more sexual
partners in the past year was 38.2% against 29.0%, consistent condom use across all partners
7.0% against 11.4%, and ever having had sex among 15-19 year olds 67.2% against 41.9%. HIV
prevalence among women was 44.5% in fishing communities against 18.8% in agrarian and
trading, rising with age from 10.5% against 2.0% at 15-19, to 34.8% against 10.9% at 20-24,
to 51.5% against 26.4% at 25-49; among men 29.8% against 8.1%, from 0.0% in both at 15-19,
to 7.3% against 2.7% at 20-24, to 39.2% against 12.9% at 25-49. Those are usable as a
structural input for setting the size and HIV prevalence of the fishing-community stratum by
sex and age band, with the caveat that the denominators are six purposively chosen
communities and not the whole RCCS.

Second, the ethnography supplies a mechanism for the age-disparate mixing the network module
has to produce. Young men migrate seasonally to Lake Victoria for low-wage fishing work
specifically to raise school fees, and once there they buy sex from older women, who charge
less than younger sex workers, and enter provider relationships with older women who own
bars and restaurants. Women, in turn, describe entering sex work to raise their own
children's fees. Both directions point the same way: economic pressure sorts younger men
toward older, higher-prevalence female partners in the highest-prevalence communities.

Neither use touches point-of-care STI diagnostics. The paper contains no STI measurement, no
symptom reporting, no treatment seeking, and nothing on the syndromic-management door the
POC arm replaces. Its `care-seeking` tag is carried by a single sentence, that families
report choosing between paying for chronic-illness medication and paying fees in the months
they come due, which is a competing-cost argument and not a rate. On the diagnostics
question this source is context only.

## Key points

Of 69 people interviewed, 34 named school fees, meaning tuition plus ancillary costs, as the
largest economic burden their family faced, ahead of housing. Surveying school directors in
the study communities, the team calculated fees at government-funded schools in urban Rakai
reaching 17% of household income per student, and private school fees reaching 66%, both
against an assumed household income of USD 2 per day. That assumed denominator is doing most
of the work in those percentages and is not measured.

Paying children's fees is described as the marker of adult masculinity in Rakai, and failure
to pay shifts the obligation to women and other family members. School fees were not on the
initial interview guide; the topic surfaced unprompted when respondents named lack of income
as the most important health problem facing young people.

Sex workers interviewed linked school fees directly to condomless sex, which commands a
higher price, and to reduced capacity to refuse acts during an encounter once payment has
been made. One respondent connects that reduced refusal capacity explicitly to acquiring
STIs. This is the only place STIs appear in the paper.

Nearly everyone surveyed in the region had attended at least some primary school, 95.1%
pooled across the two columns, which the authors read as evidence that the fee burden is
close to universal rather than confined to a subgroup.

## Limitations

The cost figures are the weakest part. The 17% and 66% shares rest on school directors'
statements about required fees and in-kind goods for one student per term, divided by an
assumed rather than measured household income, and the paper notes that ancillary costs vary
so widely by school that national and district data do not exist. They are illustrative
magnitudes.

The causal chain from fees to HIV acquisition is asserted from what respondents say motivates
them, not measured. Nobody in the study is followed to an infection, and the ethnographic
design cannot separate school fees from the general poverty and youth unemployment the paper
also documents at 47% nationally in 2017.

The RCCS table is imported from another study's data for context, with a duplicated row in
the extracted version (transactional sex appears twice with identical values), and the
sampling frame is the six purposively selected study communities rather than the full RCCS
cohort.

Fieldwork ran three months in 2019, before both the COVID-19 lockdowns and the school
closures from February 2020 to January 2022, so the fee-driven pathways it describes are
pre-pandemic.

## Methodology

Six communities, two each of fishing, agrarian and urbanised trading, chosen from the RCCS
frame as representative pairs so that patterns could be compared across community type. Data
collection was six community mappings, participant observation over six weeks with three to
four visits per community per week, 43 key informant interviews with community leaders, 24
focus groups split by age and sex, and 26 in-depth interviews with young people aged 17-28
and their romantic partners. Data collectors worked in mixed-sex pairs, one interviewing and
one taking fieldnotes.

Analysis was grounded-theory style and iterative, with the codebook revised weekly in team
meetings during collection, which is what let the school-fee theme enter the study after it
had begun. That design choice is the reason the finding exists and also the reason it cannot
be treated as a tested hypothesis: the instrument was reshaped around the theme as it
emerged, and probing intensity for school fees rose over the fieldwork period. The
school-cost survey of directors was added in the same way, after the theme surfaced, so it
has no pre-specified sampling frame beyond "every school in the immediate area", narrowed in
urban communities to the most attended schools as named by community leaders.
