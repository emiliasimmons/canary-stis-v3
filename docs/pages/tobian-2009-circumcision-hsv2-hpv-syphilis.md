Secondary analysis of the two Rakai male-circumcision trials (Rakai-1, NIH-funded, enrolled 2003-2005; Rakai-2, Gates-funded, enrolled 2004-2006), asking whether circumcision prevents HSV-2, HPV, and syphilis in HIV-negative uncircumcised males aged 15-49. Identical protocols, immediate versus 24-month-delayed circumcision, visits at baseline, 6, 12, and 24 months. Extracted text and tables in `raw/10.1056/nejmoa0802556`.

## Relevance to project

Two uses here, both from the Rakai population the model calibrates to.

A syphilis incidence target. Among men HIV-negative and *T. pallidum*-negative at baseline, active syphilis (RPR or TRUST positive, confirmed by TP-PA) was detected in 50 of 2083 (2.4%) over 24 months in the circumcised arm and 45 of 2143 (2.1%) in the control arm, so roughly 1.1 per 100 person-years across both arms in men 15-49 in Rakai over 2003-2006. Both arms are usable together, since the trial found no circumcision effect on syphilis (adjusted hazard ratio 1.10, 95% CI 0.75 to 1.65). The endpoint is serologically active infection, not incident primary infection, so it counts reactivation and treatment failure alongside new acquisition.

Symptom prevalence at the syndromic-management door. Self-reported symptoms at the 24-month visit, among 1370 circumcised and 1395 control men: genital ulcer disease 3.5% versus 5.4%, urethral discharge 2.3% versus 2.8%, dysuria 3.9% versus 4.3%. Discharge and dysuria did not differ by arm at any visit, so pooled they give a discharge-syndrome prevalence near 2.5% and a dysuria prevalence near 4% per six-month recall window in Rakai men, a bound on how many symptomatic presentations the model's care-seeking door can generate. Ulcer disease is the one symptom circumcision moved (P = 0.02 at 24 months, P < 0.001 at 6 and 12 months), and the model carries no ulcerative pathogen beyond syphilis.

Not a source for the four modelled bacterial STIs beyond syphilis: gonorrhoea, chlamydia, and trichomoniasis were not measured. The HSV-2 and HPV results are outside the model's pathogen set and serve as context for the STI-HIV cofactor argument rather than as parameters.

## Key points

HSV-2 seroconversion over 24 months in 3393 men HSV-2-seronegative and HIV-negative at baseline: cumulative probability 7.8% (1684 assigned immediate circumcision) versus 10.3% (1709 delayed), adjusted hazard ratio 0.72 (95% CI 0.56 to 0.92), an efficacy of 28%. Kalon ELISA with an optical-density index cutoff of 1.5, seroconversions confirmed by Western blot.

High-risk HPV prevalence at 24 months in the substudy: 42 of 233 (18.0%) circumcised versus 80 of 287 (27.9%) control, adjusted risk ratio 0.65 (95% CI 0.46 to 0.90). Baseline high-risk prevalence was balanced at 38.1% versus 37.1%. Prevalence, not incidence, so acquisition and clearance are not separable.

No syphilis effect, in either the crude (hazard ratio 1.14) or adjusted analysis.

Men with HSV-2 seroconversion reported genital ulcer disease at 10.3% by 24 months against 2.7% among non-seroconverters (relative risk 3.78, 95% CI 2.87 to 4.98), which quantifies how weak an indicator self-reported ulceration is for underlying HSV-2.

Behaviour differed by arm in ways that cut both directions: condom use was higher in the circumcised arm at 6 months, nonmarital relationships were more common in the circumcised arm at 12 and 24 months, and alcohol with sex was consistently higher in the control arm. Adjusting for these time-varying covariates moved the HSV-2 hazard ratio only from 0.72 to 0.77.

## Limitations

The HPV substudy is a self-selected subgroup observed at both baseline and 24 months (233 and 287 men, from 307 and 302 at baseline), plausibly more compliant and lower-risk than the trial population.

Retention was 82% in both arms at 24 months. Follow-up stops at 24 months, when the control arm is circumcised, so nothing here speaks to durability.

Syphilis serology cannot distinguish new infection from persisting reactivity, and the trial did not report treatment during follow-up, which loosens the incidence reading.

Rakai-2 enrolled a higher-risk group than Rakai-1 (older, more often married, more partners, more alcohol with sex), because it admitted men who declined to learn their HIV result. Pooled numbers mix the two; the trial found no efficacy difference between them (P = 0.52 for interaction), but the risk profile behind a pooled prevalence is not one homogeneous population.

Men only, 15-49, all HIV-negative and uncircumcised at entry.
