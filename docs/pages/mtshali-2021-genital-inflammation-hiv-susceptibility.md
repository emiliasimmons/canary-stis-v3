Narrative review from CAPRISA and the University of KwaZulu-Natal on why women in eastern and southern Africa acquire HIV across the genital mucosa at the rates they do, and on whether treating the infections that inflame that mucosa moves the risk. It is a mechanism review: immunology of the female genital tract, the pro-inflammatory signature left by each pathogen class, and what the evidence says about treatment reversing it. No new data, no pooled estimate. Extracted content in [raw/10.1016/j.jri.2021.103291/](../raw/10.1016/j.jri.2021.103291/).

## Relevance to project

This is the biological justification for the STI cofactor effect the model applies as a multiplier on HIV acquisition, and for treating that multiplier as active in asymptomatic infection.

The one number to carry is the headline: raised genital pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations in the female genital tract are associated with roughly a 3-fold higher risk of HIV acquisition. That is inflammation as the exposure, not any single STI, so it is an upper anchor on what a per-pathogen cofactor could plausibly be rather than a per-pathogen prior. Gonorrhoea, chlamydia and trichomoniasis are each shown here to raise that same cytokine set, which supports giving the four curable STIs a shared cofactor pathway instead of four independent mechanisms.

The second use is structural, and it bears directly on the SOC/POC arm contrast. The review reports similar genital cytokine concentrations, and similar cervicitis frequency, in women with and without STI symptoms. If the cofactor tracks inflammation and inflammation does not track symptoms, then the HIV risk carried by asymptomatic infection is comparable to that carried by symptomatic infection, and everything an etiological panel finds beyond the syndromic door is risk the SOC arm leaves in place. The model should not scale the cofactor down for asymptomatic cases.

Third, it supplies the case against syndromic management on clinical grounds rather than cost grounds: vaginal discharge syndrome is a poor predictor of an STI, so the syndromic pathway both over-treats (women with discharge and no STI) and under-treats (asymptomatic infections it never sees). The recommended regimen for vaginal discharge syndrome, 1 g oral azithromycin plus 2 g oral metronidazole plus 250 mg intramuscular ceftriaxone, covers chlamydia, trichomoniasis and gonorrhoea simultaneously, which is a reasonable description of the SOC arm's treatment when it does fire.

What it cannot supply: no incidence rates, no prevalences, no denominators, no effect size other than the 3-fold figure, and nothing on Rakai or Uganda. It is not a calibration target.

Bacterial vaginosis runs through the review as a parallel driver of the same inflammation, and the model has no BV compartment. That is a deliberate omission this page does not argue against, but it means the cofactor as modeled absorbs a share of risk this source attributes to dysbiosis.

## Key points

Elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNFα, CXCL10, CCL20, CCL4, RANTES) recruit CD4+CCR5+ T cells, dendritic cells and macrophages to the cervicovaginal surface and disrupt the epithelial barrier, giving HIV both a portal and a target-cell supply.

Each curable STI has its own route into that state: gonorrhoea expands susceptible dendritic and CD4+ T cell populations via TLR2, chlamydia activates NF-κB with tissue damage and leukocyte influx, and trichomonas produces punctate haemorrhages plus lipophosphoglycan-driven IL-8 and MIP-3α.

Treating the infection reduces the inflammation. Women treated for chlamydia with azithromycin had lower cervical IL-8, IL-6, IL-1β, IP-10, TNFα, IFNγ and IL-1α than untreated women; gonococcal cervicitis treatment restored plasma CD4 and CD8 counts. Whether that reduction is enough to lower HIV risk is stated as unresolved.

The viral STIs behave differently and matter for what curative treatment can reach. HSV-2 establishes latency with subclinical mucosal reactivation that still draws in CD4+ T cells, and suppressive anti-herpes therapy failed to reduce HIV acquisition. HPV is associated with HIV susceptibility in meta-analyses, and both infection and clearance raise genital cytokines. Neither is curable, so neither responds to an etiological panel for bacterial STIs.

Point-of-care options named are the NAATs (Xpert CT/NG/TV on GeneXpert, binx health io CT/NG, Truelab micro PCR, Randox STI Array) and antigen-based non-NAATs (OSOM Trichomonas, BioRapid Chlamydia Ag, One Step Gonorrhea RapiCard). No sensitivities or prices are given. None detect antimicrobial resistance.

Resistance context: gonorrhoea has lost nearly all antibiotic classes except third-generation cephalosporins; trichomoniasis depends on 5-nitroimidazoles with a shared mechanism and rising metronidazole resistance; chlamydia resistance to macrolides and tetracyclines is rare.

## Limitations

Narrative, not systematic. No search strategy, no inclusion criteria, no quality appraisal, and the cited effect sizes are lifted from primary studies without being pooled or re-checked, so the 3-fold figure inherits whatever confounding its source carried.

The review itself flags that BV-to-HIV associations are confounded by age, partner number, partner microbiome and unprotected intercourse, and that causal direction for BV is unsettled.

Populations are South African (largely CAPRISA cohorts) and the framing is women only, so nothing here speaks to the male half of the network the model runs on.

The central question, whether STI treatment reduces HIV risk rather than reducing inflammation, is left open. The review argues the mechanism is plausible and the intermediate marker responds. That is weaker than an intervention effect, which matters for how much of the model's POC-arm HIV benefit should be attributed to the cofactor pathway rather than to transmission blocked by clearing the STI.
