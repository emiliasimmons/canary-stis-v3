Extrapolation of cost-effectiveness thresholds to a wide range of countries from one
empirical anchor, the estimated marginal productivity of the English NHS, rescaled by
national income using the income elasticity of the value of a statistical life. Extracted
text at `raw/10.1016/j.jval.2016.02.017/content.md`.

## Relevance to project

Sets the bar any cost-effectiveness claim about a point-of-care STI panel would have to
clear, and sets it much lower than the convention. Uganda is not one of the eight
tabulated countries, so the usable figure is the low-income band: at the World Bank
low-to-middle income cutoff of $1,045 gross national income per capita (2013 PPP), the
threshold range is $16 to $537 per QALY, which is 1% to 51% of GDP per capita. The
percentage range is the transferable quantity, since it can be applied to whatever
Ugandan GDP per capita figure the analysis year calls for.

For this project the consequence is arithmetic. A panel priced against 1 to 3 times GDP
per capita has roughly two to six times more room than a panel priced against the
opportunity-cost threshold, so a diagnostic that looks cost-effective under the WHO rule
can still displace more health than it produces. If a cost-effectiveness arm is ever
built on top of the POC/SOC comparison, this is the source for the threshold and the
reason to report results across a threshold range instead of against a single number.
It supplies no costs, no utilities, and nothing specific to STI diagnostics or to Uganda.

## Key points

- The anchor is Claxton et al.'s estimate for the English NHS: a central value of
  GBP 12,936 per QALY, derived from GBP 25,241 per life-year after quality-of-life
  adjustment, estimated from local programme expenditure and mortality while accounting
  for endogeneity.
- Thresholds elsewhere are obtained by scaling with income elasticities of 1.0, 1.5, 2.0
  and 2.5 below $10,725 (2005 PPP) and 0.7 above it, which is what produces the wide
  reported ranges.
- Malawi, the lowest-income country in the world at the time, comes out at $3 to $116 per
  QALY (1-51% of GDP per capita, actual 2013 US dollars); Indonesia $472 to $1,786
  (14-51%); Kazakhstan $4,485 to $8,018 (33-59%); the United States $24,283 to $40,112
  (46-75%) against the $50,000 figure conventionally used there.
- The distinction the paper turns on: a supply-side threshold measures health displaced
  when the budget is spent elsewhere, while the WHO 1-3x GDP rule and the willingness-to-pay
  literature it derives from measure the consumption value of health, which is a different
  quantity and does not answer whether adopting an intervention raises population health.
- An independent Malawi estimate of no more than $150 per QALY, obtained by examining
  which interventions are funded, falls inside the $3 to $116 range the authors derive.

## Limitations

Every non-UK threshold rests on a single English estimate transported by an assumed
income elasticity, so the ranges reflect the choice of elasticity as much as any country
feature. The authors are explicit that these are indicative and rest on strong, uncertain
assumptions. Health system structure, disease burden, and the actual margin at which a
given ministry allocates are all outside the calculation, so a country-specific analysis
would displace these numbers. Figures are 2013 US dollars and need inflating. The paper
also stresses that a threshold is an input to a decision rather than a decision rule.
