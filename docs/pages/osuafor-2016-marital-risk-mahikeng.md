Source: [osuafor-2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12119-016-9360-3)

Mixed-methods cross-sectional study in Mahikeng Local Municipality, North West Province, South Africa, 2012. A two-stage probability sample of enumeration areas from the 2011 census yielded 568 women aged 18-49, currently married or cohabiting, of whom 78.3% were rural; 33 of them also gave in-depth interviews. The outcome is self-reported "ever had a risky sexual act" while in the current union, a binary the paper never defines operationally.

## Relevance to project

Context, not a target or a prior. The setting is South Africa, not Rakai, and the outcome is a self-reported composite with no STI or HIV testing, so no number here can calibrate anything in the model.

Its use is structural: it argues that stable cohabiting partnerships in a generalized-HIV setting are not low-risk, and it names the mechanism the model's risk-structured network needs if it is to reproduce transmission into unions. Women whose partners worked in mining were 2.76 times (95% CI 1.03-7.42, reference: partner unemployed) and in agriculture 6.04 times (1.68-21.69) more likely to report risky sex, attributed in the discussion to labour migration and partner contact outside the union. That is the bridge-population argument for why a married woman with no reported concurrency still carries acquisition risk, and it supports the model retaining an assortative-by-risk edge into otherwise low-activity partnerships rather than treating unions as closed.

Two behavioural quantities are usable only as soft plausibility bounds on the SOC arm's care-seeking. 49.0% of the women (265/541 answering) judged themselves unlikely to contract an STI and a further 28.8% saw no risk at all, and those judging themselves unlikely had 2.85 times the odds of risky sex (1.74-4.67). The qualitative material gives the same finding a mechanism directly relevant to the POC/SOC contrast: women describe fear of violence, blame, and being read as unfaithful if they present at a clinic for STI treatment while an asymptomatic partner does not. Since the POC arm changes only what happens at the symptomatic care-seeking door, this source is a reason to expect that door to be narrower for partnered women than a symptom-prevalence calculation would suggest. It is a qualitative reason, not a rate.

The paper offers nothing on partner notification uptake, consistent with the model implementing no partner-notification pathway.

## Key points

41.7% of the 568 women reported ever having a risky sexual act in the union. The gradient with education is steep and monotone: 66.4% among the 112 women with no education against 24.4% among the 121 with tertiary education, with adjusted odds of 0.14 (0.05-0.34) for tertiary against no education as reference.

Union duration under 5 years carried elevated odds (1.71, 1.04-2.83, reference: 10 years or more), which the authors read as young women in new unions lacking sexual negotiating standing.

Spousal communication about sex separated the sample sharply in the bivariate table: 30.7% reporting risky sex among the 352 who communicated against 61.0% among the 200 who did not. The variable does not appear in the final parsimonious model.

Childbearing was given as the main reason for sex by 71.3% of the 393 women answering, and the desire for motherhood is offered as a direct route to condomless sex regardless of known partner STI status.

## Limitations

The dependent variable is never operationally defined. "Ever had a risky sexual behaviour" is left to the respondent, so the 41.7% headline has no fixed denominator of acts, no time window, and no comparability with any other study's risk measure. This alone rules the figure out as a calibration target.

No biological measurement. The authors list the unknown STI status of women and partners as their own second limitation, so every claim connecting the behaviour to infection is inferential.

Self-report on a sensitive outcome, collected face to face, with social desirability acknowledged; the direction of that bias would deflate the 41.7%.

Cross-sectional, so the odds ratios are associations. Perceived low STI risk and reported risky sex, in particular, plausibly run in both directions.

Table 3 is internally inconsistent in two places. The condom row reports OR 1.55 with CI 0.36-0.56, a CI that excludes its own point estimate. The force-to-obtain-sex row marks "Yes" as the reference category and gives "No" an OR of 1.90, which reverses the direction stated in the text and abstract. Treat both rows as unreliable as printed and use the bivariate percentages instead: 54.1% of the 185 women with condom-unwilling partners reported risky sex against 35.4% of 367, and 61.1% of the 131 endorsing the force belief against 35.6% of 421.

## Methodology

Two-stage sample: enumeration areas selected with probability proportional to rural and urban household counts, then all eligible women in the 600 sampled households, 568 of whom completed. Chi-square for bivariate association at p < 0.05, then a parsimonious binary logistic regression reporting odds ratios. No survey weights, no design effect, and no clustering adjustment for the enumeration-area sampling are reported, so the confidence intervals are narrower than the design warrants. Qualitative interviews were conducted in Setswana, translated, and analysed thematically under four fixed themes.
