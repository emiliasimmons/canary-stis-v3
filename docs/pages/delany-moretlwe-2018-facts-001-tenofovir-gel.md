A phase 3, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of tenofovir 1% vaginal gel used pericoitally (one dose within 12 h before sex, one within 12 h after, the BAT-24 regimen) at nine community sites in South Africa. 2029 sexually active HIV-negative women aged 18-30 were followed for up to 27 months on product between October 2011 and August 2014, contributing 3036 woman-years. Extracted text and tables sit in `raw/10.1016/s1473-3099(18)30428-6`.

## Relevance to project

A comparator and a bound, not a calibration target. The site is South Africa and the population is young women recruited into a prevention trial, so its incidence does not stand in for Rakai.

Three usable numbers. First, background HIV incidence in an at-risk 18-30 year old female population with intensive prevention counselling: 4.0 per 100 woman-years in the placebo arm (95% CI 3.1-5.2), against a design assumption of 3.5% per year. That is a plausible upper bracket for the female incidence a model of a high-burden setting should be able to reach in the trial era.

Second, a bound on topical PrEP. Intention-to-treat efficacy was null (IRR 0.98, 95% CI 0.7-1.4). Efficacy conditional on measured drug was aHR 0.52 (0.27-0.99) for any detectable cervicovaginal tenofovir and aHR 0.45 (0.23-0.89) above the 30 ng/mL protection threshold, adjusted for age, education, marital status, living arrangements and baseline HSV-2, stratified by site. Any coitally dependent topical product represented in this model should carry an effective coverage term at roughly this level: a per-act efficacy near 50-55% delivered on well under half of sex acts, giving a population effect indistinguishable from zero.

Third, an STI-HIV cofactor observation consistent with the project's coinfection structure: baseline HSV-2 seropositivity carried IRR 1.5 (1.0-2.0) for HIV acquisition, and HSV-2 seroprevalence was 43% (tenofovir arm) and 40% (placebo arm) at enrolment, rising from 35% in women under 25 to 56% in women 25 and older. The trial measured HSV-2 only, not the four curable bacterial STIs the model carries, so this informs the shape of the cofactor argument rather than supplying a parameter.

## Key points

Adherence, measured by returned empty applicators against self-reported sex acts, averaged 57% of acts covered in the tenofovir arm and 58% in placebo. Only 19% of tenofovir-arm participants covered more than 80% of acts; 40% covered fewer than half. Gel use in a higher proportion of acts was associated with lower HIV incidence in both arms alike (HR 0.48, 95% CI 0.26-0.89), which reads as adherence marking lower-risk women rather than as product effect.

A prespecified age interaction went in opposite directions: IRR 0.73 (0.5-1.1) under 25 years, IRR 2.23 (1.0-5.1) at 25 and over, p-interaction 0.01. The authors do not claim a mechanism and note the age groups differ in HSV-2 seroprevalence, sexual activity and partner age.

Safety was unremarkable. Grade 2 adverse events were slightly more frequent on tenofovir (IRR 1.09, 95% CI 1.0-1.2, p=0.02), driven partly by intermenstrual bleeding (IRR 1.90, 1.0-3.6). No product-related serious adverse events. None of the 144 seroconverters tested showed Lys65Arg or Lys70Glu tenofovir resistance mutations.

Risk factors for incident HIV, from the trial's own covariate analysis: unmarried (IRR 2.5, 1.0-6.3), living with parents (IRR 2.7, 1.4-5.0), self-perceived high HIV risk (IRR 1.7, 1.1-2.6).

## Limitations

Adherence measurement is the trial's weak point and the authors say so. Applicator returns depend on self-reported act counts, and only 15% of the 585,023 dispensed applicators came back. Cervicovaginal lavage tenofovir detection can persist up to four weeks after a dose, so a detectable sample does not establish that gel was used at the exposing act.

The drug-concentration analyses are post-randomisation and case-cohort, so the aHR near 0.5 is not a randomised efficacy estimate and confounding by whatever makes a woman adherent is exactly what the adherence-versus-incidence gradient in the placebo arm demonstrates. Treat 45-55% as an optimistic ceiling on per-act topical efficacy, not a measured value.

Exposure to HIV at the time of gel use was never observed, a limitation the authors flag as general to prevention trials.
