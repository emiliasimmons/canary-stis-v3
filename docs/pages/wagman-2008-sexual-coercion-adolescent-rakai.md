> Note: summary derived from the abstract only, not the full source.

Qualitative study of sexual coercion among adolescent women aged 15 to 17 in Rakai District, Uganda, drawn from focus group discussions and in-depth interviews with pregnant and never-pregnant sexually active adolescents. It examines the continuum of coercive experiences, the contexts in which they occur, how adolescents perceive and respond to them, and the links between coercion and reproductive-health outcomes including early sexual debut and pregnancy.

## Relevance to project

Context for the risk structure at the youngest end of the modelled network, not a calibration input. The project's sexual-network layer places adolescent girls and young women in a high-acquisition position, and this source describes the mechanism behind part of that exposure: coerced and transactional sex among under-18 women, perceived as a normal feature of relationships. It supports treating early debut and low sexual decision-making authority as drivers of AGYW exposure rather than optional refinements, and it bears on how partnership formation and condom use are represented for this age band.

The transactional-sex finding connects the coercion continuum to the same economic drivers the occupational and key-population sources in Rakai describe. The care-seeking relevance is indirect: coercion perceived as normal within marriage is a barrier to help-seeking and disclosure that any facility-based intervention in this population operates against.

No prevalence of coercion, age-at-debut distribution, or pregnancy rate is available from the abstract to use as a target.

## Key points

Informants described multiple forms of coercion: coerced or forced intercourse, unwanted sexual touching, verbal harassment, and transactional sex.

Sexual coercion was perceived as a normal part of intimate relationships.

A woman's lack of decision-making authority, including over sexual encounters, was felt to be implicit to marriage.

The authors frame the findings as inputs for violence-prevention programs targeting adolescents.

## Limitations

Abstract only: sample sizes, recruitment, and coding results were not read. Qualitative and perception-based, so it supports no rate or distribution. Coercion is self-reported and subject to normalization and recall bias, and the sample is confined to Rakai District adolescents aged 15 to 17.
