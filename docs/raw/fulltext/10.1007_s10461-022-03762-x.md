# A novel “HIV salience and Perception” scale is associated with PrEP dispensing and adherence among adolescent girls and young women in Kampala, Uganda

## Abstract

Low perceived HIV risk is a barrier to effective pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) use among African adolescent girls and young women (AGYW). Single-item risk perception measures are stigmatizing and alienating to AGYW and may not predict PrEP use. There is a need for a tool capturing domains of perceived HIV risk and salience that align with PrEP use among AGYW. This HIV PrEP study was conducted in Kampala, Uganda. We developed and piloted the 9-item “HIV Salience and Perception” (HPS) scale (range: 9-36); higher scores indicate beliefs of higher vulnerability to HIV. We administered the scale to Ugandan AGYW participating in an ongoing cohort study at enrollment, one, three and six months. PrEP dispensing was measured quarterly and adherence was measured daily via Wisepill (high adherence: ≥80% of expected pill bottle openings). We assessed scale performance and used generalized estimating equations to determine associations between scale score and PrEP use. Among 499 AGYW, 54.1% of our sample was ≥20 years (range:16-25). The median HPS score was 18 (range:8-33; α=0.77). Higher score was associated with PrEP dispensing (aRR=1.07 per point increase; 95% CI=1.01-1.13; p-value=0.02) in the overall cohort and among only those ≥20 years (aRR=1.10; 95% CI=1.03-1.19; p-value=0.01). We did not observe an association between scale score and PrEP adherence. AGYW scoring higher on a novel HPS scale were more likely to initiate and obtain PrEP refills through 6 months. This scale may capture drivers of PrEP dispensing and could inform PrEP delivery and counseling for AGYW.


## INTRODUCTION

Daily oral tenofovir-based pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a highly effective HIV prevention method, although effectiveness depends on product uptake and consistent but not perfect adherence during periods of risk (1–6). Findings from research with adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in sub-Saharan Africa show that this population often has difficulty adhering to the daily regimen of contemporary PrEP medications and sustaining PrEP use across periods of oscillating HIV risk (7–9). Perceiving one’s own risk of HIV can contribute to engagement in HIV prevention behaviors, but young women have often reported perceptions of HIV risk that are not aligned with more objective measures of potential HIV exposure (e.g., condomless sex, sexually transmitted infections), HIV acquisition, and PrEP use (10–12).

PrEP studies and delivery programs to date have generally relied on measures of HIV risk perception that are blunt and stigmatizing. They also fail to account for cognitive biases in risk assessment, including “salience” (attention to issues that are most apparent or top-of-mind), “present bias” (prioritization of short-term rewards over longer-term outcomes), and “over-optimism” (assumption that one is invulnerable to disease), and may ask about sexual behaviors over a time horizon that does not reflect current risk (13–15). Most HIV risk perception measures consist of single items, rather than scales, and may not capture multiple dimensions of the construct (16). A recent study with South African AGYW found that single item measures of HIV risk perception (e.g., “Indicate your level of risk of being infected with HIV” with response options from 0-100%; “What is the chance you will get HIV at some stage of your life?” with Likert-style response options) were confusing, alienating in their focus on one’s own “risky behavior” instead of their partner’s, and stigmatizing in their portrayal of young women as “reckless” with their health (17). Such measures could bias assessments of perceived HIV risk among AGYW because of a lack of desire to identify with stigmatizing behaviors (11,16,18). In addition, these items may not capture aspects of HIV risk most important for this population, such as trust in partners and relationships (11,16,18). More nuanced and adolescent-friendly measures may more accurately reflect AGYW’s motivations for taking PrEP for HIV prevention and understanding alignment between PrEP use and dynamic HIV risk among AGYW (19–21). This could reduce reporting biases and mismeasurement of HIV risk perceptions and improve counselors’ understanding of AGYW beliefs about their own HIV risk and partnership concerns. This could in turn result in improved counseling to promote PrEP dispensing and adherence, setting AGYW up to succeed with effective PrEP use during periods of potential HIV exposure. Moreover, improved HIV risk perception measurement has the potential to improve PrEP programs by getting PrEP to those AGYW who need it most (10–12). For example, low HIV risk perceptions are associated with low PrEP uptake and adherence, and misalignment between perceptions and sexual behavior provide an opportunity to probe AGYW about their partnership dynamics and potential PrEP needs (10–12).

While PrEP programs currently consider HIV risk perception, alongside several other criteria, in identifying adolescent girls and young women eligible for PrEP and counseling young women about the importance of PrEP adherence (16,22,23), limited work has been done to use risk assessment tools specific to this population that incorporate multiple dimensions of perceptions and cognitive biases in risk assessment. In addition, few studies have assessed longitudinal associations between changes in dimensions of HIV risk perceptions, PrEP dispensing, and PrEP adherence which would enable PrEP providers to understand how AGYW conceptualize their HIV risk and view their own dynamic PrEP needs. We sought to contribute to these research gaps by developing a novel HIV salience and perception scale to measure complex, multi-dimensional constructs related to PrEP use for AGYW which are not captured by existing HIV risk perception questions. We assessed scale performance and evaluated associations between HIV risk perception, PrEP dispensing, and PrEP adherence among a cohort of HIV-uninfected AGYW participating in a PrEP cohort in Uganda.


## METHODS


### Study design and participants

The “Kampala Women’s Bone Study” is a prospective cohort study of AGYW aiming to assess the relationship between concurrent tenofovir (TFV)-based oral PrEP and depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) use and bone health (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03464266). Enrollment was conducted from May 2018 to March 2020. The study was implemented at a peri-urban research clinic site in Kampala, Uganda. Eligible women were between the ages of 16-25, sexually active, newly initiating DMPA or using male condoms only for contraception, reported no recent PrEP use in the three months prior to study enrollment, and were not planning to become pregnant in the next 24 months.

Participants were screened, enrolled, and offered daily PrEP at enrollment. PrEP initiation at enrollment was not required for participation. All participants attended study visits at one month after enrollment and quarterly thereafter, for up to 24 months. They received HIV testing, PrEP refills (as needed), DMPA injections (as needed), and HIV prevention counseling at each visit. Uganda national guidelines were followed for syndromic management of sexually transmitted infections (24). Participants who accepted PrEP also received PrEP adherence counseling at each visit including messaging about the importance of daily dosing during periods of HIV risk, side effect management, safety during pregnancy, and continued condom and contraceptive use while taking PrEP. All participants were offered PrEP at each visit and those who accepted PrEP could discontinue at any time.


### Data collection

At enrollment, sociodemographic data were collected (e.g., age, marital status, education level, financial status) and an empiric HIV risk scoring tool was applied (25). Information on sexual behavior (e.g., condomless sex acts since the prior study visit), fertility intentions, psychosocial and behavioral factors (e.g., HIV stigma, social support, depressive symptoms, alcohol and drug use), contraceptive use, and HIV salience risk perceptions were collected at baseline and quarterly follow-up visits with questionnaires administered by interviewers in the participant’s preferred language (English or Luganda). PrEP dispensing (via dispensing records), discontinuation (via dispensing records and discontinuation CRFs), and adherence (via self-report and Wisepill electronic monitoring devices) were measured at every follow-up visit.


### Key measures

We developed our “HIV Perception and Salience” scale (HPS) through a series of five iterative steps. First, we reviewed the literature to develop a conceptual understanding of HIV salience and risk perceptions and how they may relate to HIV prevention behaviors, to assess how HIV salience and risk perception constructs have been previously measured, and to explore strengths and limitations of these tools. We identified a number of single-item measures that used Likert scales, risk ladders, and risk narratives to assess HIV risk perception and two scales that were previously developed and validated to assess HIV risk perceptions in the US and Ethiopia (16,18,26–29). The single-item measures included the following: “Do you think you have risk of getting the AIDS virus? (no, small, moderate, or great risk)”; “In general, what do you think is your risk of getting HIV from your partner? (no, low, moderate, high risk)”; “What do you think the chances are that you will ever get HIV/AIDS? (impossible, not likely, somewhat likely, very likely)”; “How likely are you to become infected with HIV in the next four weeks? (no, small, moderate, or high chance)”. Risk ladders generally asked, “How likely do you think you are to get HIV in your lifetime”, with responses from 0-10 or 0-100. The two scales included 10-15 items related to one’s own fears, worries, and perceptions of HIV (26,27). Second, we reviewed themes from recent qualitative studies of PrEP delivery to adolescent girls and young women in sub-Saharan Africa to assess key themes around HIV salience and risk perceptions discussed during in-depth interviews and developed additional potential questionnaire items related to these themes (18,30–32). Third, we reviewed these items with experts in the fields of HIV prevention, behavioral science, and behavioral economics research to develop and refine scale items. Fourth, we conducted cognitive testing of our scale items with five young people in Uganda who were not part of the Kampala Women’s Bone Study. These individuals completed the HPS items with a trained interviewer and were asked to provide feedback on item comprehension, wording of items and question stems, response options, and comfort with answering the items. Fifth, we revised scale items, reviewed the updated scale items with experts, and trained clinic staff on scale administration. These steps comprised an “item generation” phase of questionnaire development (26,27,33,34). We subsequently administered our items to all adolescent girls and young women in the Kampala Women’s Bone Study at enrollment and quarterly follow-up visits as part of “item reduction” and “scale validation” phases of questionnaire development (26,27,33,34). In addition to our HPS scale items, we also asked several single-item HIV risk perception questions that were used in prior studies for comparability between studies (e.g., “Are you generally a person who takes risk?”; “In the past three months, how often have you thought about getting HIV?”).

PrEP dispensing was measured by a series of items asking whether the participant was given PrEP at that clinic visit and the reason for not dispensing PrEP at that visit if applicable. These items were completed by the clinic staff based on discussions with the participant and pharmacy PrEP dispensing records. For our analysis, we defined PrEP dispensing at each visit as participant receipt of at least one bottle of PrEP during enrollment, Month 1, and quarterly follow-up visits. Participants were not required to accept PrEP as a condition of study participation. Those who declined PrEP at a visit still received other study services, including HIV testing and counseling, contraceptives, and laboratory monitoring.

PrEP adherence was measured with Wisepill electronic monitoring devices that record a date-time stamp with every closure and transmit the data to a cloud-based database (Wisepill™, South Africa). Adherence was calculated as the percentage of days with a device opening event out of expected days of PrEP use during the period between study visits. Days of expected PrEP use excluded days for which PrEP was not dispensed due to missed refill visits, protocol-defined PrEP stops, or refill refusals; we also excluded days without a valid Wisepill signal. Participants were considered to have high adherence during a given follow-up period if ≥80% of days with expected PrEP use had a Wisepill box opening event since the prior visit. This cutoff corresponds to 5-6 doses per week and plasma tenofovir concentrations >40 ng/mL, which is consistent with steady-state daily dosing and has been associated with high levels of protection against HIV (35,36).


### Statistical analyses

We used descriptive statistics to summarize the sample of study participants. Our final HPS scale included nine items with a Likert scale with 4 responses from strongly agree to strongly disagree (Table 1). Items were scored from 1-4 and sum scores were created. Higher sum scores indicated greater risk perception (items 6 and 9 were reverse-scored to be consistent with this scale scoring). The scale included three items specific to participant’s sexual partner(s) and “not applicable” responses were available for these options and scored as “0” for participants who did not currently have a sexual partner. Based on this scoring, the possible range of scores is from 9-36. We conducted analyses to explore HPS item frequencies and psychometric properties of scale performance (e.g., reliability via a Cronbach’s alpha statistic, correlation between items, whether removal of any single items would increase reliability or scale variance) using enrollment data. We also conducted an exploratory factor analysis with a promax rotation to assess whether items grouped into distinct factors. We assessed convergent validity by examining associations between our scale score and responses on single-item risk perception measures used in prior studies (16,18,28,29) and sexual behaviors (e.g., condomless sex, number of sex partners) using Spearman’s rho.

After assessing scale properties and performance, we assessed the association between score on our HPS scale and PrEP dispensing and adherence through six months of follow-up. We used generalized estimating equations with a log link, Poisson distribution, and robust standard errors to estimate risk ratios. We included HPS scale score as a continuous exposure variable and PrEP dispensing (yes/no) and adherence (high/low) at each study visit as categorical outcome variables in separate longitudinal models. For PrEP dispensing, we explored whether HPS scale score at a given visit (reflecting feelings and perceptions about risk over the next three months) was associated with accepting PrEP dispensation at that same visit. For PrEP adherence, we estimated associations between HIV risk perceptions at a given visit and PrEP adherence during the three-month period following that visit. We assessed several additional baseline covariates for confounding, including age, education, income and financial stability, living with primary partner, marital status, and partner characteristics (primary partner HIV status, circumcision status, financial dependence on primary partner). Of these covariates, any that resulted in a substantial change in the effect estimate (>10%) were included in the multivariable model. Prior PrEP trials have found that younger women (those less than 20 years of age) may have poorer PrEP adherence than older women. Stability of sexual partnerships and actual, as well as perceived, HIV risk also likely differs by age. Due to these factors, we also stratified our models by age (<20 years, ≥20 years) to explore associations between HIV risk perceptions, PrEP dispensing, and PrEP adherence separately for younger and older women in our cohort.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we explored associations between HPS scale factors and individual items and our PrEP-related outcomes of interest. This enabled us to explore whether one factor or a small subset of items were potentially responsible for observed associations. Second, we standardized the scale to include relevant questions for participants with and without main sexual partners at the time of the survey. We calculated a mean HPS scale score from the first six items alone for participants who did not report a main sexual partner. We calculated a mean score from all nine items for participants who reported a main sexual partner. We explored associations between these standardized scores and PrEP dispensing and adherence to determine the extent to which our findings were robust to changes in scale scoring decisions. Third, we reclassified participants who missed study visits as “non-adherent” to assess whether our findings on assocations between HPS score and PrEP adherence were robust to retention issues in this cohort. Lastly, we examined whether baseline HPS score was associated with study retention through the 6-month follow-up period.

All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (Cary, North Carolina, USA).


### Ethical statement

Protocols were approved by ethical review boards at the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology and the University of Washington.


## RESULTS


### Participant characteristics

This study enrolled 501 HIV-uninfected adolescent girls and young women. However, 2 AGYW did not complete enrollment procedures and thus this analysis included 499 AGYW. The median age at enrollment was 20 years (interquartile range [IRQ] 18, 21). The median years of school completed was 11 (IQR 8, 12 years), 86.2% of participants were single with a steady partner, and 91.6% reported that their partner provides financial or material support (Table 2). The median number of vaginal sex acts in the three months prior to enrollment was 20 (IQR 8, 60) and 67.5% reported using condoms never, rarely, sometimes, or often while 32.5% reported always using condoms. We observed 0 incident HIV infections among the 499 participants through six months of follow-up.


### Performance of HIV risk perceptions and salience scale

We observed good distribution of HPS scale scores among 499 participants at enrollment, with a range from 8-33 (scale items shown in Table 1). The median score was 18 (IQR: 15-20) and the mean score was 17.4 (standard deviation=3.7). Items demonstrated good internal reliability with a Cronbach’s α of 0.77. This statistic did not substantially change with the removal of any of the items. For example, item 7 had the weakest correlation with the total score (r=0.19) but its removal resulted in a Cronbach’s α of 0.80.

Our exploratory factor analysis revealed the presence of two factors (Table 1). Factor 1 included items 1-6 which assessed HIV risk perception and salience based on one’s own sexual behavior and feelings of vulnerability around HIV acquisition. Factor 2 included items 7-9 which assessed HIV risk perception and salience based on trust in one’s sexual partner and perceptions of a partner’s sexual behavior. Almost half the participants responded “not applicable” to item 7 (44.5%) because they did not have a new sexual partner. Items 8 and 9 also had “not applicable” responses for participants who did not report a main partner.

The HPS scale score demonstrated strong convergent validity. Specifically, HIV risk perception and salience score at enrollment was significantly correlated with number of sexual partners (Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient [rs]=0.32, indicating a weak to moderate positive correlation; p<0.01), and higher scale score was positively correlated with more sexual partners. Score was also associated with any report of condomless sex in the three months prior to enrollment (rs=0.36, indicating a weak to moderate positive correlation; p<0.01), and higher scale score was correlated with condomless sex. Finally, we compared scale score to single-item measures of HIV risk perceptions from prior studies and found that higher scale score was associated with thinking of HIV more often (rs=0.23, p-value<0.01), having a higher level of worry about HIV (rs=0.26, p-value<0.01), and thinking of oneself as having a moderate or high chance of acquiring HIV in the next 3 months (rs=0.34, p-value<0.01).


### Associations between HIV risk perceptions and salience scale score and PrEP dispensing

Of 499 participants enrolled, 464 (93.0%) completed Month 1, 446 (89.4%) completed Month 3, and 413 (82.8%) completed Month 6. A total of 35 participants missed their Month 1 visit, 53 missed their Month 3 visit, and 85 missed their Month 6 visit. Only 12 participants (2.4%) were lost to follow-up after their enrollment visit (they missed all three follow-up visits). A total of 25 participants (5.0%) missed two follow-up visits and 62 participants (12.4%) missed one follow-up visit. HIV risk perception and salience scale scores remained relatively stable over time and enrollment scores were not associated with missed study visits through the 6-month study period (relative risk [RR]: 0.98; 95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 0.92-1.04; p-value=0.54). The median scale scores at enrollment, Month 1, Month 3, and Month 6 were 18 (IQR: 15-20), 17 (IQR: 15-19), 16 (IQR: 15-19), and 17 (IQR: 15-18), respectively.

PrEP was dispensed at a total of 1516 attended study visits between enrollment and Month 6 (85.1% of 1782 attended study visits; Table 3). PrEP was not dispensed during 247 attended visits (92.9% of 266 visits when PrEP was not dispensed) because participants declined PrEP due to changes in their HIV protection needs, side effects, or other PrEP-related concerns. PrEP was not dispensed during the remaining 19 visits (7.4% of 266 visits) due to investigator-led PrEP terminations. PrEP dispensing was between 80.4-88.7% across all study visits (86.4% at enrollment, 88.7% at Month 1, 87.1% at Month 3, and 80.4% at Month 6). In the overall sample, higher HPS scores at a given visit were associated with greater likelihood of PrEP being dispensed during that same follow-up visit (adjusted relative risk [aRR] of PrEP dispensing was 1.07, per 1 unit increase in HPS score; 95% CI: 1.01-1.13; p-value=0.02). In analyses stratified by age, HPS score was associated with greater likelihood of PrEP uptake among those ≥20 years (aRR: 1.10; 95% CI: 1.03-1.19; p-value=0.01) but was not statistically significantly associated with PrEP uptake among participants <20 years (aRR: 0.99; 95% CI: 0.93-1.08; p-value=0.97; interaction p-value: 0.06).

In our sensitivity analysis, Factor 2 (items 7-9 related to trust in one’s sexual partner and perceptions of a partner’s sexual behavior) was associated with PrEP dispensation (aRR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.15-1.37; p-value<0.001) but Factor 1 was not statistically significantly associated with PrEP dispensation. In addition, items 5 (“My sexual behavior gives me a chance of getting HIV in the next 3 months”; aRR: 1.46; 95% CI: 1.07-2.00; p-value=0.02), 8 (“My main sexual partner’s behavior gives me a chance of getting HIV in the next 3 months”; aRR: 1.43; 95% CI: 1.06-1.99; p-value=0.02), and 9 (“I trust my main sexual partner to keep me free from HIV”; aRR: 1.85; 95% CI: 1.35-2.53; p-value<0.001) were the only items each significantly associated with PrEP dispensing. HPS scale score remained associated with PrEP dispensing in a sensitivity analysis using standardized mean scores to account for whether a participant had a main partner at that visit (aRR: 1.76; 95% CI: 1.09-2.86; p-value=0.02) and the magnitude of the effect was greater in this analysis than in our primary analysis using the score summing across HPS items.


### Associations between HIV risk perceptions and salience scale score and PrEP adherence

PrEP adherence was high at 568 (69.3%) of 820 total study visits through Month 6 (Table 4). High adherence was observed at between 61.0-79.1% of visits (61.0% at Month 1, 74.2% at Month 3, and 79.1% at Month 6). We did not find statistically significant associations between HPS score and PrEP adherence in the overall cohort (aRR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.99-1.10; p-value=0.11) or analyses stratified by age group. In our sensitivity analyses, neither Factor 1 nor Factor 2 were associated with PrEP adherence. When considering individual HPS scale items, only item 5 was statistically significantly associated with PrEP adherence (aRR: 1.45; 95% CI: 1.05-1.99; p-value=0.02). The standardized mean HPS score was also not associated with PrEP adherence in this sample (aRR: 1.43; 95% CI: 0.91-2.25; p-value=0.12). We did not observe an association between HPS score and PrEP adherence when considering participants who missed visits to be non-adherent to PrEP (aRR: 1.03; 95% CI: 0.98-1.08; p-value=0.23).


## DISCUSSION

This manuscript summarizes scale development and performance for a novel HIV perception and salience measure and scale testing in a prospective cohort of AGYW receiving PrEP in Uganda. We found that a 9-item scale incorporating elements related to HIV risk perception and vulnerability, behavioral economics constructs such as salience of HIV infection, and trust in a partner and relationship satisfaction, had high internal reliability and validity. Item clustering revealed two latent factors related to HIV risk perception and salience: 1) beliefs about HIV risk from one’s own sexual behavior and 2) trust in a partner. A higher score on this HPS scale at enrollment was correlated with a greater number of sex partners, less frequent condom use, and endorsement of single-item risk perception measures from other PrEP studies. We also found a statistically significant association between scale score and PrEP uptake and refills through six months of follow-up.

Although other PrEP delivery studies have typically assessed HIV risk perceptions with single-item measures, we hypothesized that a scale incorporating items around cognitive assessments of risk, self-assessments of vulnerability or worry, and saliency of HIV would offer a more comprehensive view of AGYW’s concern about HIV and their perceptions about their risk and their partner’s risk. This work was grounded in prior studies that developed multidimensional HIV risk perception scales for populations at risk of HIV and, consistent with these studies, our scale performance findings (internal reliability, factor loadings) suggest that items about perceived likelihood, worry, vulnerability, and risk-taking may be closely related (26,27). Qualitative and quantitative studies with young women seeking PrEP in the United States and sub-Saharan Africa have also found that partnership characteristics (e.g., feelings of trust or stability in a partnership) are important elements of HIV risk perceptions and may better predict HIV risk behaviors like condomless sex than risk perception measures asking about one’s own feelings of vulnerability, worry, or likelihood of HIV acquisition (16,18,37). Inclusion of items about trust in partners may also improve the overall quality of HIV risk perception data, as indicated by a recent cognitive interviewing study with young women in South Africa who reported that it was difficult to answer questions about likelihood of HIV acquisition given that so much of their HIV risk depends on their trust in their partner (17). Other research on HIV risk perception scale development that included items around sexual risk perceptions pertaining to sexual partners has similarly found that these partner-specific items cluster into their own subscale separate from items on general perceived risk related to one’s own behavior (38). Our scale was interviewer-administered which created unique opportunities for staff to elicit discussions about partnership dynamics and develop a mutual understanding of AGYW HIV prevention needs. However, some AGYW may prefer to reflect on and answer questions about partner-specific items on their own and future work is needed to explore the acceptability of self-administration of our HPS scale.

Consistent with our findings on associations between HIV risk perception score and PrEP outcomes, other studies have reported that perceived HIV risk is associated with interest in and initiation of PrEP but not with PrEP adherence (39–41). Associations between HIV risk perception score and PrEP uptake are encouraging and suggest that while women may be accurately able to assess their HIV risk to determine whether they need to initiate or refill their PrEP medication. This association was statistically significant for the subset of women 20 years of age and older, who may have greater autonomy in their decision to seek PrEP, may be more accurately able to perceive their HIV risk, or may have different factors contributing to their HIV risk and PrEP needs than younger women. However, the difference in the magnitude of effect for the association among women ≥20 years and <20 years old was small and may not be clinically meaningful.

We did not detect a statistically significant association betwee HPS score and PrEP adherence, indicating that women with high HIV perceptions and salience may not be able to consistently adhere to PrEP between clinic visits. It is possible that our risk perception measure was not able to accurately capture important dimensions of HIV risk associated with PrEP adherence, due perhaps to limitations in scale items, comprehension issues responding to items, or social desirability bias (17). We also were limited to measuring PrEP adherence using Wisepill data, which assesses adherence to the device, rather than pharmacologic markers, which can directly assess pill consumption, and future research could explore associations between HPS scores and tenofovir diphosphate drug level data. However, we observed high PrEP adherence in over half the population, and women in this study received comprehensive HIV testing and counseling and PrEP adherence counseling, which may have led to high PrEP adherence regardless of reported HIV risk perceptions. Other factors, including PrEP stigma, concerns over side effetcs, and disclosure-related issues, may also be more important predictors of PrEP adherence than HIV perception and salience in this population. An alternative explanation is that HIV risk perceptions and consistent, high PrEP adherence behavior may be misaligned for young women, highlighting an opportunity for targeted PrEP adherence counseling to improve regular PrEP use in this population (42). At the individual-level, more predictive HIV risk perception measures that are associated with PrEP use could inform tailored counseling on PrEP readiness, uptake, and adherence based on HIV risk perceptions and misalignment between HIV risk perceptions and PrEP use (42). In addition, our findings highlight that longer-acting biomedical HIV prevention products (e.g., cabotegravir injections, dapivirine ring) may be particularly important HIV prevention options for younger women who have difficulties with sustained daily oral PrEP use even during periods with high HIV risk perceptions (20,43).

Strengths of this study include the multi-step approach to develop our HPS scale, with consideration of key findings from the scientific literature, expert opinion, and results from pilot testing. We also assessed scale performance and associations with PrEP use in a large cohort of AGYW receiving PrEP in Uganda and collected repeated measures over follow-up with minimal loss to follow-up. However, we were limited in our scale development efforts and did not conduct pilot testing or cognitive interviewing of our scale items with a large group of respondents in our target population. We engaged a population of young women already interested in PrEP and enrolled in a PrEP delivery study, and our findings may not be generalizable to a general population of young women who have not already thought about their HIV risk and identified a need for PrEP. Future studies and PrEP delivery programs seeking to use the HPS scale may wish to incorporate it at screening rather than enrollment to better capture baseline HIV perceptions and salience prior to PrEP initiation. Our stratified analysis of the associations between HIV perceptions and PrEP outcomes by age groups was exploratory in nature, and the cohort was not designed for a fully powered analysis to answer questions of effect modification by age. However, prior studies have reported differences in HIV risk, behavior, and PrEP adherence for younger and older women, and further research is needed to explore HPS scale performance and associations in these populations. PrEP adherence was measured using electronic data on pill container openings, which may be biased if participants store their pills in a different container or open the container but do not take a pill out. Other PrEP delivery projects have found good correlation between these electronic data and pill count data with pharmacologic measures of PrEP drug levels, although these studies were conducted with adult women in HIV serodiscordant relationships (36,44). Questionnaires were interviewer-administered, rather than self-administered, which could impact participants’ answers, but interviewers were all well-trained in the study, data collection tools, and working with an AGYW population seeking PrEP. While the HPS items capture AGYW’s general feelings of trust in sexual partnerships, items do not explicitly ask about partner’s HIV status and likelihood of HIV transmission due to antiretroviral therapy use and viral load. Other scales including these items have not reliably predicted PrEP use, but they may nevertheless be important considerations for some AGYW. Finally, we only assessed HIV risk perceptions and salience, PrEP uptake, and PrEP adherence through six months of follow-up and future studies could explore associations between these constructs for a longer follow-up time.


## CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this study contributes to an evidence base on HIV risk perceptions and PrEP use for young women by proposing a novel measure incorporating elements of HIV risk perceptions, salience of HIV, and trust in sexual partners to better understand nuances of HIV risk perceptions in this population. Future research is needed to explore scale performance and associations between scale score and PrEP use in other settings. Additional work is also needed to enhance PrEP adherence counseling messaging and roll-out of longer-acting HIV prevention modalities to support alignment between HIV risk perception and PrEP use behavior. This scale could be used to improve PrEP uptake and outreach, by identifying AGYW with high HIV perception and salience or perceptions that seem misaligned with sexual behaviors and providing targeted PrEP readiness assessments and counseling based on the drivers of HIV risk. It could also be used in evaluations, to assess effectiveness of modified PrEP adherence counseling and PrEP decision-making interventions over time (e.g., exploring associations between a counseling intervention and changes to HIV perception and salience or alignment between scale score and sexual behaviors). Finally, the scale has the potential to reduce measurement bias in HIV risk assessments and improve PrEP uptake by asking less stigmatizing questions and including items about partnership dynamics and trust in relationships. Approaches to improve measurement of HIV risk perceptions and alignment between risk perceptions, actual HIV risk, and PrEP use are critical to promote cost-effective PrEP use and reduce the burden of HIV among young women in HIV endemic settings.
