# Impact of community health worker intervention on PrEP knowledge and use in Rakai, Uganda: A mixed methods, implementation science evaluation

## Abstract

Background:Effective models of support for HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) are needed for populations at elevated risk. In a hyperendemic Ugandan fishing community, PrEP counseling was provided through a situated Information, Motivation, and Behavioral Skills (sIMB)-based community health worker (CHW) intervention. We evaluated the intervention using a mixed-methods, implementation science design.Methods:We surveyed all community members aged 15–49 through the Rakai Community Cohort Study. We used multivariable logistic regressions with generalized estimating equations to estimate the intervention’s effect on PrEP knowledge and utilization. To understand intervention experiences and mechanisms, we conducted 74 qualitative interviews with 5 informant types (clients, CHWs, program staff, community leaders, health clinic staff) and analyzed data using an iterative, deductive approach. A mobile phone application provided intervention process implementation data.Results:Individuals self-reporting receipt of the CHW intervention showed significantly higher PrEP knowledge (N = 1848, PRR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.06–1.14, p = <.0001), PrEP ever use (N = 1176, PRR: 1.77, 95% CI: 1.33–2.36, p = <.0001), and PrEP current use (N = 1176, PRR: 1.86, 95% CI: 1.22–2.82, p = 0.0039) compared to those who did not. Qualitative findings attributed positive PrEP outcomes to CHW counseling and effective use of motivational interviewing skills by CHWs. Salient themes across the RE-AIM framework included support for the CHW intervention and PrEP across clients, community, and implementers. Mobile application data demonstrated consistent delivery of the PrEP module throughout implementation.Conclusions:CHWs improved PrEP knowledge and use among clients in an HIV hyperendemic fishing community. Mixed-methods, implementation science evaluations can inform adaptation of similar PrEP implementation strategies.


## Introduction

Nearly 1.4 million people are living with HIV in Uganda (prevalence: 5.4%).1 Fishing villages face a disproportionate burden of HIV compared to the general population; this discrepancy is attributed to high rates of mobility and higher risk for HIV exposure, in part due to dangerous working conditions, multiple sexual partners, alcohol use before sex, and inconsistent condom use.2–4 Oral antiretroviral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is a proven method for prevention of HIV acquisition. Since its introduction in Uganda in 2017,5 an estimated 220,000 people have initiated PrEP.6 In Uganda, the Ministry of Health has previously identified fisherfolk as a priority population for PrEP,7 and numerous studies have highlighted PrEP as an effective, safe, and cost-effective approach for preventing HIV infection across populations.8 Previously described barriers to PrEP use in Uganda include stigma, mobility, and low perceived risk for HIV.9,10

Used across the world, community health worker (CHW) programs are often implemented in low-resource settings where clinic-based services are less accessible and health infrastructure is strained.11 There is mounting evidence CHWs increase uptake of HIV services through counseling, outreach, and linkage to care.12,13

Based on the CHW model, the mLAKE (mHealth Lakefolk Activity Keeping Engaged) trial was conducted in a fishing community in Uganda from 2015 to 2018 by the Rakai Health Sciences Program (RHSP), a non-governmental organization. As previously reported, this trial evaluated the impact of a CHW intervention on HIV care engagement, antiretroviral therapy adherence, male circumcision coverage, and virologic suppression.14,15 Counseling on PrEP, defined as CHW-led exploration and optimization of related knowledge, motivation, and behavioral skills using motivational interviewing strategies, was added to the original intervention design in 2017 and PrEP-related outcomes have not been previously reported. Using the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) implementation science framework and a mixed methods approach, we aimed to now characterize: 1) the impact of CHWs on PrEP knowledge and use in the community; and 2) how and by what mechanisms CHWs may have made an impact on PrEP knowledge and use.


## Methods


### Study setting

The study was a pragmatically-oriented cluster randomized, controlled trial administered by RHSP and conducted in a fishing village located in the south-central region of Uganda (area: ∼2 km2). The community has an estimated adult/adolescent population of 4000–5000; however, the population has fluctuated substantially over time with key economic and ecological events. At an estimated 40%, the HIV prevalence in the village is nearly 5 times the national prevalence.3 Contextual and structural factors related to the fishing industry in the village contribute to higher HIV risk behaviors, including daily physical danger, frequent and seasonal mobility, and substance use.2,4,16 RHSP is the primary supporter of HIV services in the community.17


### Intervention design

PrEP became available to the community in August 2017 with support from the U.S. President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) through the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).5 Fishing communities and trading centers in the south-central districts of Uganda were chosen for initial PrEP roll-out due to the hyperendemic nature of HIV in these settings. As part of the PEPFAR PrEP program, RHSP conducted community sensitization through tailored outreach with selected priority groups including fisherfolk, sex workers, truck drivers, and individuals in HIV-discordant relationships. HIV-negative individuals (≥15 years old) classified as higher-risk were offered PrEP with accompanying counseling and follow-up at a centrally located clinic.

The central components of the CHW intervention were three-fold. The intervention was 1) delivered in a non-clinical setting, 2) based on motivational interviewing-informed counseling, and 3) mHealth supported.17,18 The development of the intervention was grounded in the situated Information, Motivation, and Behavioral Skills (sIMB) conceptual framework. The full study design for the Health Scout intervention has been previously reported;17 in short, ten CHWs—called Health Scouts—were recruited from the community and trained to use relevant information, motivation, and behavioral skills to promote client engagement in HIV services.17,19 Health Scouts were supervised by RHSP staff and were regularly evaluated for competency in assigned tasks.

The Health Scouts used mHealth technology through a mobile phone application to guide counseling sessions with clients. The phone application guided Health Scouts on specific counseling based on an initial screening algorithm followed by modules tailored to the client based on their prior responses. The Health Scout intervention was implemented from September 2015 to December 2018. The PrEP module was added to the intervention in September 2017 in conjunction with the PEPFAR PrEP program. Full descriptions of the modules and algorithms can be found elsewhere.17


### Data collection

We conducted 74 semi-structured in-depth interviews between September and November 2018 across 5 types of informants: Health Scouts (n = 8), clients (n = 51), program staff (n = 5), community leaders (n = 7), and health staff (n = 3). Purposeful sampling was used to select informants.20 The sample pool for clients was restricted to the intervention arm and informants were selected for maximum variation across gender, age, and HIV status.14 Data collection materials were designed in English and translated to Luganda by bilingual staff. Interview guides for each class of informant were designed based on the RE-AIM framework for program evaluation, inclusive of all components of the Health Scout intervention, including PrEP.21 Interviews were conducted in Luganda and audio-recorded for transcription purposes. Informed, written consent was obtained from all informants and a brief demographic survey was administered at the conclusion of each interview. The staff member who conducted each interview transcribed and translated it to English.

Two main sources of quantitative data were gathered. First, self-reported data was collected from the Rakai Community Cohort Study (RCCS), an open, population-based cohort conducted by RHSP since 1994.3 In the study setting, RCCS was conducted at the study mid-point (from 5 December 2016 to 13 February 2017) and at end-of-study (from 3 September to 19 December 2018). Second, the mHealth mobile application collected real-time response data during implementation. Full details on data collection using RCCS and complete prompts/algorithms from the mobile application can be found elsewhere.15


### Data analysis

The RE-AIM conceptual framework21 was used to guide mixed methods data analysis to provide a structured approach to understanding of the experience with the Health Scouts and PrEP in this community.22 For each domain of the framework, we considered how the Health Scouts influenced knowledge and use of PrEP through the intervention design and accompanying counseling strategies. Table 1 outlines the components of RE-AIM and the accompanying outcomes and assessment methods used for analysis. While data were collected concurrently, we utilized sequential (i.e. the qualitative data were used to better understand the quantitative findings) and convergent (i.e. qualitative and quantitative analyses were conducted independently of each other) designs for data analysis (S1). Findings were integrated across RE-AIM variables in the final step of analysis.


#### Qualitative data.

An iterative, phased approach was used for qualitative data analysis. First, we deductively drafted a codebook using the RE-AIM and sIMB conceptual frameworks. After reading the data, the codebook was systematically applied across transcripts. We then conducted a round of inductive coding to ensure all viewpoints were captured and accurately represented. The original codebook was revised to include more specific themes that emerged from both phases of coding. The main themes drawn from the transcripts were discussed by the analysis team and synthesized into key findings. Computer-assisted qualitative data analysis software NVivo was used for data organization and management.


#### Quantitative data.

Our primary exposure variable was self-reported receipt of any Health Scout counseling. We examined three binary (Yes/No) outcome variables measuring PrEP knowledge, PrEP ever use, and PrEP current use. Descriptive statistics and prevalence data were reported for study outcomes. Intention-to-treat multivariable analyses were conducted using log-linear binomial regression with generalized estimating equation to estimate prevalence risk ratios (PRR) in the intervention versus control. Based on evidence from previous analyses that found substantial contamination in the control arm,15 as-treated analyses were performed to measure outcomes using Chi-square tests using the exposure variable. Subsequent analyses adjusted for sex and age.


### Ethics statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine Institutional Review Board, the Research and Ethics Committee of the Uganda Virus Research Institute, and the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology. All participants provided oral and written informed consent and assent as appropriate.


## Results

Individual-level characteristics across study arms of the Health Scout intervention at baseline were largely evenly distributed (S2). When comparing individual-level characteristics across exposure status at end-of-study for the RCCS community survey, more individuals were unexposed than exposed, and those who were exposed were more likely to be in older age groups, female, married or previously married, less educated, and HIV seropositive than those who were unexposed (S3). Among clients who completed a qualitative interview, gender, age, and self-reported HIV status were roughly evenly distributed, while a majority were classified as fisherfolk. Demographic characteristics varied among other types of interview informants (S4).

### Reach

Mobile application data showed 1634 clients in the intervention arm were screened for eligibility for PrEP counseling by the Health Scouts. Of these, 1515 clients (92.7%) were eligible and had the PrEP counseling module activated during their Health Scout counseling session. At the end-of-study cohort survey, 20.1% (60/292) of exposed and 11.5% (102/884) of unexposed individuals reported ever using PrEP (p-value <.0001). In addition, 11.0% (32/ 292) of exposed and 5.9% (52/884) of unexposed individuals reported using PrEP currently (p-value 0.0039).

Qualitatively, we explored reasons why clients reported choosing to use PrEP after a counseling session with their Health Scout. Clients’ reported reasons for starting PrEP included feeling they had increased risk of acquiring HIV because they engaged in sex work, had condomless sex, had multiple partners, or had a partner they knew was living with HIV. Clients credited their Health Scout with providing information to better evaluate their own risk. An overall desire to protect oneself was also identified as a motivation for using PrEP. Respondents shared men rejected PrEP more frequently than women, as men felt they had alternative options for protection. Further, multiple women discussed how their male partner inhibited their own use of PrEP.


### Effectiveness

To assess intervention effectiveness, we assessed exposure to Health Scout counseling and its association with PrEP knowledge, use, and continuation. In the as-treated analysis, RCCS participants who reported receiving a Health Scout counseling session were nearly twice as likely to report current or ever use of PrEP (Table 3). In addition, the as-treated estimates showed significantly higher PrEP knowledge at the end-of-study survey. In the intention-to-treat analysis, both current and ever use of PrEP was higher in the intervention group compared to the control, but was not statistically significant; as noted previously, contamination in the control arm may have introduced a null bias to these estimates (S5).15 Adjusted and unadjusted results showed negligible differences in estimates.

To better understand these effectiveness results, we considered the acceptability of PrEP for clients of the intervention. Some clients accepted the information provided by their Health Scout and acted on this new information by deciding to take PrEP. Changed attitudes about PrEP resulted in health-seeking behavior for some clients. Some misinformation–including the belief that use of PrEP reduced the effectiveness of other HIV antiretrovirals, was used for deliberate infertility, and was a disguise for other HIV medications–was cited as deterring acceptance of PrEP. However, Health Scouts corrected similar misinformation with other clients, which promoted PrEP acceptability and use. Informants also described the daily pill burden and side effects such as nausea, fatigue, and headache as barriers to PrEP use.


### Adoption

For adoption, we sought to understand the acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of the intervention and its strategies from the perspective of Health Scouts, health staff, and other program implementers. Primarily, PrEP was viewed as an effective and appropriate strategy for HIV prevention. Additionally, health staff expressed their view that Health Scouts act as “a bridge to the community” due to their ability to mobilize and engage clients from within the community.


### Implementation

The qualitative findings for implementation addressed the quality of the intervention delivery. According to informants, Health Scouts primarily focused on providing personalized information about PrEP to clients during their counseling sessions while promoting PrEP use across sIMB constructs. Informants described how Health Scouts used motivational interviewing strategies to provide accurate and helpful information to clients (Information), encouraged clients to consider the benefits of PrEP (Motivation), and supported clients in their decision-making process to visit health facilities and adhere to pill regimens (Behavioral Skills). Further details on the relationship between PrEP counseling and the sIMB constructs have been published previously.14 In summary, use of the sIMB constructs during counseling sessions appeared effective at increasing client knowledge and perceptions around PrEP. However, clients’ resistance to PrEP and lack of time inhibited Health Scouts’ ability to deliver PrEP counseling as originally intended in some instances.


### Maintenance

Responses from both categories of PrEP users derived from the mobile application data suggest the PrEP counseling module was delivered consistently by Health Scouts throughout the implementation period with increasing proportions interested in PrEP over time (Figure 1).


## Discussion

We analyzed qualitative and quantitative data to assess the mechanisms and impact of the Health Scout intervention on PrEP utilization in an HIV hyperendemic fishing village in south-central Uganda. We relied on the RE-AIM framework to help guide and frame our understanding of the study population’s interactions with CHWs (Health Scouts) related to PrEP. Overall, Health Scouts appeared to positively influence clients’ PrEP knowledge, adherence, and retention across RE-AIM domains. Our mixed methods approach provided insights into a complex intervention strategy, adding to limited literature on implementation strategies for PrEP in sub-Saharan Africa.

We found knowledge of PrEP increased significantly by the end of the study in the exposed group, a finding that has been documented by other HIV prevention interventions.23 Qualitative evidence supported this finding, as information was frequently cited as the primary focus of the interaction between Health Scouts and clients related to PrEP. A prior qualitative assessment of the RHSP PrEP program cited awareness of high community HIV risk and trust in PrEP effectiveness as facilitators to PrEP use, a result of the community-level sensitization efforts done by the program.9 The current study suggests Health Scouts increased knowledge above the community level, demonstrating the value of CHWs to deliver counseling through a motivational interviewing-informed approach. These findings contribute to existing literature on the effectiveness of CHWs to provide information on HIV transmission and support access to HIV-related services.12,13

Overall, the analysis of the Health Scout intervention suggested there was a positive impact on PrEP use among the exposed group in the study setting. Qualitative data suggested a range of experiences with the Health Scouts intervention facilitated PrEP utilization. Implementer and stakeholder support for both PrEP and the community-based approach of the intervention contributed to community acceptance. This aligns with studies highlighting community mobilization as an avenue for influencing uptake of HIV prevention methods through broader changes in social structures.23,24 In addition, the Health Scouts’ use of motivational interviewing-informed counseling in tandem with components of the sIMB framework provided clients with the information necessary to make the choice to take PrEP, motivated them to seek out and adhere to PrEP, and promoted health behaviors essential to mitigating HIV acquisition.

Despite the knowledge and support clients received, Health Scouts were unable to address complex, multi-faceted barriers including misinformation, pill burden/side effects, and inequitable gender norms that led some men to reject their female partner’s use of PrEP. These findings correspond with other studies in the region highlighting the gap between knowledge and PrEP use, including the difficulty in addressing barriers to use.23,25 A prior evaluation of the RHSP PrEP program found high rates of PrEP uptake but low rates of retention, especially among key groups.5 Challenges to PrEP adherence have been well documented throughout Africa26 and are country- and situation-dependent. Future interventions in Uganda should explore how resistance to taking PrEP can be addressed through a CHW-delivered approach. Given the complexities of changing risk perceptions and PrEP, it is challenging to use traditional measures of retention, both within and outside this study.5,9,27,28 Further qualitative studies are needed to understand how the decision to stop taking PrEP in sub-Saharan Africa is related to multi-level structural barriers and individual risk perception.


### Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its mixed methods design applied across the dimensions of the RE-AIM framework, an approach that allowed for a more in-depth understanding of the impact of the Health Scout intervention in this hyperendemic community. Furthermore, the intervention uses a CHW-based approach in conjunction with mHealth and motivational interview-informed counseling to influence PrEP use, a novel strategy for HIV prevention that has potential applications across other global settings.

This study has several limitations. Since the interview guide was originally developed to assess the Health Scout intervention more broadly than the focus of this study, there were limited qualitative data specifically on the implementation of PrEP counseling. Our assessment of the maintenance dimension of RE-AIM focused on changes in effectiveness over time rather than elements of longer-term sustainability and institutionalization. Additionally, the intervention engaged a community categorized as “high risk,” an approach that has been criticized as perpetuating stigma and negative perceptions of PrEP due to its over-emphasis on certain subpopulations within a region or country.27,29 Although we did not find the Health Scouts visits to be a source of stigma related to PrEP, program designers should consider potential stigmatization when developing future interventions targeting key populations. Additionally, frequent movement among the study population contributed to contamination of the control arm, inhibiting an accurate assessment of PrEP outcomes across randomized study arms. Use of an as-treated analysis comparing exposed to unexposed groups was employed to account for this contamination; however, there may be residual confounding. Exposure to other projects, radio ads, and social media were not measured; however, cluster randomization was done to account for these potential confounders. The inclusion of a single, moderately sized village also raises concerns of generalizability to other populations.


## Conclusion

A CHW-led intervention implemented in an HIV hyperendemic fishing village in south-central Uganda was associated with improved PrEP knowledge and utilization within the study population, supporting the use of CHWs in providing theory-based, motivational interviewing-informed counseling to improve PrEP outcomes. These findings may inform the design and implementation of future interventions targeting PrEP use in sub-Saharan Africa. Further research on factors contributing to PrEP decision-making and adherence, including the role of stigma, can help optimize public health approaches to promote PrEP use for HIV prevention.
