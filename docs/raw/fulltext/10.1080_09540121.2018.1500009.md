# Women’s role in male circumcision promotion in Rakai, Uganda

## Abstract

Medical male circumcision (MMC) has expanded in sub-Saharan Africa, yet uptake remains sub-optimal. We sought to understand women’s perceptions of and influence on MMC in Rakai, Uganda. We conducted in-depth interviews with 27 women in fishing and trading communities, including women married to circumcised and uncircumcised men, single women, and sex workers. Data analysis followed a team-based framework approach. All female participants preferred circumcised men because of perceived reduced HIV and sexually transmitted infection (STI) risk, improved penile hygiene, and increased sexual pleasure. Perceived negative aspects included abstinence during wound healing, potentially increased male sexual risk behaviors, fear of being blamed for HIV acquisition, and economic insecurity due to time off work. Participants felt women could persuade their partners to be circumcised, accompany them to the clinic, refuse sex with uncircumcised men, and participate in community MMC activities. Findings support women’s important role in MMC acceptance.


## Introduction

Medical male circumcision (MMC) has expanded rapidly in sub-Saharan Africa, yet uptake remains sub-optimal in many settings.(Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS, 2016; Maibvise & Mavundla, 2014; Uganda AIDS Commission, 2015) Recently, attention has focused on the role of women in male circumcision programs.(Lilleston, Marcell, Nakyanjo, Leonard, & Wawer, 2017; Mati, Adegoke, & Salihu, 2016; Osaki et al., 2015; Semeere et al., 2016) Evidence suggests that women are not directly protected against HIV when their partners are circumcised, although they receive indirect protection if male HIV prevalence is reduced due to circumcision, and that female partners of HIV-positive men who are circumcised may actually be at greater risk of HIV acquisition if sexual intercourse resumes before complete wound healing.(Wawer et al., 2009) Consultations held in preparation for male circumcision scale-up in 2007–2008 articulated the need for research to better understand the implications of MMC for women and to minimize potential risk.(AIDS Vaccine Advocacy Coalition: Global Advocacy for HIV Prevention, 2008; Kim, 2008; UNAIDS/CAPRISA, 2007) Recent studies have also called for a gender perspective in qualitative research on MMC in sub-Saharan Africa.(Martinez Perez, Trivino Duran, Gasch, & Desmond, 2015)

Women’s knowledge about MMC varies widely. In studies conducted in Botswana, Namibia, Swaziland, Zambia, and South Africa, 59–90% of women knew that MMC provided partial protection against HIV, 23–70% knew to abstain from sex for six weeks post-procedure, and 14–30% believed it was acceptable for a circumcised man to expect condomless sex.(Andersson & Cockcroft, 2012; Haberland, Kelly, Mulenga, Mensch, & Hewett, 2016; Lanham, L’Engle K, Loolpapit, & Oguma, 2012; Mantell et al., 2013; Maraux et al., 2017; Mugwanya et al., 2010; Plank et al., 2010; Wilcken, Miiro-Nakayima, Hizaamu, Keil, & Balaba-Byansi, 2010) In qualitative studies from Kenya, Zambia, and Tanzania, women said they were willing and able to influence men to get circumcised.(Layer et al., 2014a; Riess, Achieng, & Bailey, 2014) In Zimbabwe, 14% of men cited lack of partner support as a major barrier to MMC, especially among older married men who were concerned about fidelity, relationship trust, and the six-week post-surgery abstinence period.(Hatzold et al., 2014) In Zambia, partner support and discussion increased men’s readiness to undergo MMC;(Jones et al., 2014) the Spear and Shield project showed that female partners’ acceptance of MMC increased men’s likelihood to get circumcised.(Cook et al., 2016)

Uganda has scaled up MMC, reaching 50% of its target by 2015.(Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS, 2016) With PEPFAR support, the Rakai Health Sciences Program (RHSP) in rural Rakai district, Uganda, has performed over 100,000 MMCs, and MMC prevalence among men in Rakai increased from 14% to 69% between 2004 and 2014. New demand generation strategies are being developed, using peer influence (e.g. sports-based programs) or financial compensation for transport or opportunity cost.(Djimeu & Brown, 2016; Sgaier et al., 2016; Wilson, Frade, Rech, & Friedman, 2016) A partner-based pilot intervention had pregnant women engage their spouses in MMC discussions.(Semeere et al., 2016) Although qualitative studies have been conducted among men,(Ssekubugu et al., 2013) little qualitative research has explored women’s role in MMC promotion. The goal of this study was to understand women’s perceptions of male circumcision and their role in influencing men’s decisions to get circumcised.


## Methods

We conducted a qualitative research study in Rakai District from August-September 2015 in fishing communities and main-road trading communities, where HIV prevalence is higher than in agricultural communities.(Chang et al., 2016)

In total, we conducted 27 in-depth interviews [Table 1]. Participants were purposively selected from individuals who had previously participated in the Rakai Community Cohort Study (RCCS)(Chang et al., 2016) and had agreed to be re-contacted for future studies. We followed a criterion-based sampling approach, where we purposefully selected women in different types of relationships relevant to the study topic, specifically: married women (further divided into those with circumcised versus uncircumcised husbands), single women, and women working in bars and/or as sex workers. Only women married to non-Muslim partners were included, since Muslim men circumcised in childhood are not MMC candidates. Married women’s partners’ circumcision status was initially identified through the most recent RCCS data, but then verbally re-confirmed immediately prior to the qualitative interview. Bar girls/sex workers were oversampled because they were able to provide information on both clients and their primary partners. While not all bar girls in these communities also earn money through sex work, the majority do because they are dependent on this source of income. During our interviews, we asked women in this group about their interactions with clients; the ensuing discussions indicated that they were working as sex workers. Within each group of women, we aimed for approximately equal distribution between fishing and trading communities. We then used convenience sampling to select individuals who met these criteria and had recently completed the RCCS.

Data were collected by experienced, trained research assistants. Using RCCS-generated lists, research assistants approached potential participants, invited them to participate in the study and obtained written informed consent from each. Interviews were conducted in private and lasted 60–90 minutes. Interviewers took notes, and all interviews were audio-recorded, with participants’ permission. Interviews were conducted in Luganda, the local language, then transcribed and translated into English by the same research assistants, generally within 24 hours.

A semi-structured guide was used to focus discussion on topics of interest, including women’s knowledge about and source of information on MMC and STIs; attitudes and preferences or concerns about MMC; women’s role in influencing men’s decision to get circumcised; and participants’ recommendations on how women can promote MMC.


### Data analysis

Data analysis followed a modified framework approach.(Ritchie & Spencer, 2003) Two members of the study team (NN and DP) first read through the transcripts and each prepared a list of identified key themes and subthemes which were discussed with CK to jointly develop a final list of key themes to create a matrix frame. Two members of the research team (NN and DP) then read each transcript and summarized the themes into the matrix table, including relevant quotations. Finally, matrices for each interview were summarized by theme and compared across groups. These thematic summaries were discussed and developed into the analysis presented here.


### Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Uganda Virus Research Institute (UVRI)’s Research and Ethical Committee (REC), the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (UNCST) and Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health IRB. Participants provided written informed consent.


## Results

We present our findings below by five key themes: (i) women’s preferences about circumcision, (ii) positive aspects of circumcision for women, (iii) negative aspects of circumcision for women, (iv) women’s role, experiences, and recommendations about circumcision, and (v) circumcision and condom use.


### Women’s preferences about circumcision

All 27 participants said they preferred circumcised to uncircumcised men, including women married to uncircumcised men. “A man who is not circumcised is not modern,” said a 33-year-old married woman, a perspective echoed by many participants. Women explained that circumcised men were stylish and modern because the act of getting circumcised implies that a man cares about both his own and his female partner’s health, since circumcision reduces the risk of HIV and STIs. They said female partners are more relaxed during sex with circumcised men because they are less worried about contracting infections. ### Positive aspects of MMC for women


#### Reduced risk of HIV and STIs

All participants said that a positive aspect of MMC for women was the female partner’s reduced risk of HIV and STIs. This was attributed largely to the removal of the foreskin which would otherwise increase the chances of males contracting HIV/STIs, in turn exposing their female partners to infection.


#### Improved penile hygiene

Women also said that the foreskin retains “dirt” and that circumcision made it easier for the man to clean himself. Also, some women believed that ulcers due to HIV and STIs “hide” beneath the foreskin, so when a woman has sex with a circumcised man, she feels secure since she can see her partner has no “dirt” or ulcers.


#### Improved sexual desire and pleasure

Many women felt that circumcision increased sexual desire and pleasure for both partners due to smooth, easy penetration and improved men’s sexual prowess with prolonged and more frequent sexual encounters. However, one 45-year-old woman said she felt no difference in sexual pleasure after her husband’s circumcision.


### Negative aspects of MMC for women


#### Behavioral disinhibition

Some participants, particularly those married to uncircumcised men, were concerned that circumcision would lead to riskier male sexual behaviors. These women stated that circumcised men often experienced increased sexual desire leading them to seek extra-marital relationships which could put them and their partners at risk of HIV/STIs. Additionally, some participants thought circumcision could lead men to engage in extra-marital relationships if they thought they had lower risk of HIV infection.


#### Wound healing period and sexual abstinence

Most participants believed sexual abstinence during wound healing lasted longer than the recommended six weeks. Some women felt this was a burden, especially for married couples. Women said some couples engaged in sex before complete wound healing due to the female partner’s impatience. Participants said that some men who would have abstained until the wound had healed gave in to their wives’ desire for intercourse because they feared that their wives might be tempted to have sex with other men.

A few participants claimed that unlike men, women can wait to resume intercourse without becoming unfaithful. However, they felt that younger women might be more impatient than older women.

Many participants were concerned about the potential for a “blame game” if an HIV-negative man gets circumcised but acquires HIV. They felt that the man would blame the woman for infection. One explained:

Participants felt this could result in domestic violence or separation.


#### Time off work

Some participants, especially those from fishing communities, were concerned about time off work and loss of income. Participants worried that, if a man had to take time off work after MMC, it would adversely affect his family’s finances. Participants also said that many fishermen are employed by boat owners or managers who would not allow them to take time off work.


### Women’s role, experiences, and recommendations about circumcision

All participants acknowledged women’s positive role in promotion of MMC and provided recommendations of how women can promote MMC.


#### Talk and convince

Participants felt that women’s major role was to talk to their partners and convince them to undergo MMC. They said it was important for women to talk nicely and politely to their partners about circumcision’s advantages. Men rarely attend health education and sensitization meetings, whereas women usually attend such sessions and could pass on this information to their partners. They cautioned, however, that women not be coercive, because men might react unfavorably. Participants suggested that the best time for women to introduce the topic was when the couple was in the bedroom “resting”.

However, some participants noted that convincing male partners could be challenging, and some women married to uncircumcised men described unsuccessful attempts to convince their partners to get circumcised. One 25-year-old participant said, “When I told him to go and get circumcised, he told me, ‘I am already infected.’” Another described how she convinced her husband to get circumcised, but his uncircumcised male friends discouraged him, saying that he would get sepsis or experience pain. Many participants, both with circumcised and uncircumcised partners, said that fear of pain was a major reason why men refuse circumcision. One participant reported that her husband felt uncomfortable and shy at the thought of his sons knowing that their father was circumcised.

Some participants described how they had tried to convince male friends, relatives, and in-laws to get circumcised. Some sex workers reported sharing experiences with other sex workers about circumcised versus uncircumcised clients, leading them to influence clients to get circumcised. Another participant described how she had convinced her male neighbor, who wanted to marry her friend, to get circumcised before the marriage. Others noted that women could encourage their female friends with uncircumcised partners to discuss MMC. However, there was general agreement that social norms made it difficult for women to raise the issue of circumcision with men other than their husbands, long-term partners, brothers, or sons.


#### Accompany partners to clinics

Some participants recommended that women accompany their partners to MMC clinics and jointly participate in counselling sessions. One woman noted that couples counselling would enable both partners to understand the importance of abstaining from sex during the healing period. However, one participant felt that the woman’s presence might make her partner uncomfortable if he experienced painful erections after the procedure.


#### Withhold sex

Some participants recommended that female partners threaten to or actually abstain from sex with uncircumcised partners. This was mostly suggested by sex workers, some of whom said they had refused sex with uncircumcised clients, just as they commonly deny sex with clients who refuse to use condoms. More lenient sex workers said they would have sex with uncircumcised men but warn them that the next time they came, they should be circumcised. One sex worker said she accepted sex with uncircumcised clients just for the money, but uncircumcised clients disgusted her. Participants reported that some men end up getting circumcised out of fear of being scorned or ridiculed by female partners, especially sex workers. However, one participant warned that withholding sex might not work since “he can always look for other women to satisfy his need”. There was also some fear of violence if men were denied sex.


#### Participate actively

All participants supported the active involvement of women in MMC promotion. These included women participating in MMC/health education sessions, communication messages, games, music, dance, drama, and open discussions about MMC.


### Circumcision and condom use

All participants emphasized the HIV prevention benefits of condoms, without the interviewers raising this topic. Participants argued that condom use was important after circumcision to prevent STIs like gonorrhea. Many participants, especially sex workers, said they often used condoms. When asked about the difference between a circumcised and uncircumcised man during sex, many sex workers said they felt no difference since they consistently used condoms with clients. Sex workers also said that many circumcised men did not want to use condoms because of the false belief that they were fully protected against HIV.

Participants recommended further community sensitization about condoms use after circumcision. They also suggested that sex workers insist on condom use with male clients regardless of their circumcision status and advise other sex workers to refuse condomless sex.


## Discussion

In Rakai, women perceive circumcision as stylish and modern. They generally feel that the benefits of improved sexual pleasure, improved penile hygiene, and reduced risk of HIV/STI infections outweigh the negative aspects like abstinence during wound healing, loss of financial support during a man’s time off work, potential blame for HIV/STIs, and potential male behavioral disinhibition. Across our diverse sample of participants, we found that all participants thought circumcision was a good thing. Women described trying to convince their partners and other male family members and neighbors to get circumcised, though not always successfully, and they provided multiple suggestions for engaging women in supporting MMC.

Our findings are consistent with other research in sub-Saharan Africa suggesting that many women approve of and/or prefer circumcision,(Ikwegbue, Ross, & Ogbonnaya, 2015; Lanham et al., 2012; Layer et al., 2013; Plotkin et al., 2013; Riess et al., 2014) that approval is related to perceived sexual benefits for women as well as men,(Ikwegbue et al., 2015; Layer et al., 2013; Plotkin et al., 2013; Riess et al., 2014) and that women are currently engaged (and willing to be further engaged) in convincing their partners to be circumcised.(Lanham et al., 2012; Layer et al., 2014b; Osaki et al., 2015; Riess et al., 2014) Similarly, concerns that MMC may increase risk behavior among some men have been documented elsewhere,(Ikwegbue et al., 2015; Layer et al., 2014b; Osaki et al., 2015) though studies of men who have undergone MMC have generally not found deleterious behavioral changes following circumcision.(Auvert et al., 2013; Kagaayi et al., 2016; Kong et al., 2012; Westercamp, Agot, Jaoko, & Bailey, 2014) Concerns about blame-shifting and abuse associated with HIV infection have also been described previously.(Burke et al., 2017; Colombini, James, Ndwiga, & Mayhew, 2016; Mulrenan, Colombini, Howard, Kikuvi, & Mayhew, 2015; Nabaitu, Bachengana, & Seeley, 1994; Ninan, 2003; Quinn et al., 2018)

One strength of this study was our inclusion of sex workers, who have only been assessed in one Zambian study of MMC.(Abbott, Haberland, Mulenga, & Hewett, 2013) In that study, Zambian female sex workers were more skeptical than sex workers in our study about the efficacy of MMC, though participants held similar views about improved penile hygiene and the continued importance of condom use. Our findings do not indicate how often sex workers refuse clients due to lack of circumcision, and our interviews did not go into detail about how such refusals occur in practice. However, sex workers’ general positive perspective on MMC and open attitude toward discussing MMC in the context of their work suggest that they may be an important but overlooked group for MMC promotion.

While women across all groups claimed to prefer circumcised men, women married to uncircumcised men expressed greater concerns about behavioral disinhibition, while sex workers were the main group advocating refusal of sex with uncircumcised men. These findings echo those of a Tanzanian study, which found that refusal of sex was discussed only in relation to casual partnerships, and that married women were most concerned that circumcision might indicate a man’s interest in extra-marital partnerships.(Osaki et al., 2015) In our study, discussions about time off work and loss of income tended to focus on the impact on wives. It is also clear how difficult it is for women to influence or bring up the topic on MMC with men other than partners, clients, sons, and other family members or neighbors. Furthermore, we noted that women in fishing communities cited lost financial support as a particularly salient barrier. These findings suggest MMC messaging could be tailored to different subgroups of women, such as married women, sex workers, or women in fishing communities.

One potential limitation of this study is social desirability bias. Participants knew that interviewers worked with RHSP, which is known to provide circumcision services. However, previous qualitative research on MMC in Rakai documented more negative perceptions,(Ssekubugu et al., 2013) indicating that people are willing to share negative opinions, even to RHSP staff.

Our findings add to the evidence suggesting that women can influence men’s MMC decisions and may help promote MMC. Evaluation will be needed to assess the effectiveness of outreach to women.(Curran, 2014)
