## Association of life-course events with predictors of HIV risk behaviour and HIV acquisition in Manicaland, Zimbabwe: a longitudinal analysis of a population cohort

Michael Pickles, Simon Gregson, Louisa Moorhouse, Rufurwokuda Maswera, Blessing Tsenesa, Freedom Dzamatira, Phyllis Mandizvidza, Sophie Bagnay, Tawanda Dadirai, Brian Kumbirai Moyo, Owen Mugurungi, Constance Nyamukapa, Timothy B Hallett

## Summary

Background An individual's HIV risk, and consequently their HIV prevention needs, change over time. In this study we aimed to quantify these changes, examine which life-course events were associated with them, and investigate the extent to which those life-course events were associated with HIV acquisition.

Methods We used longitudinal data from eight rounds of a general population cohort in Manicaland province, eastern Zimbabwe, on sociodemographic and HIV risk behaviours, as well as HIV serostatus from the first seven rounds. We first  visualised how HIV risk behaviours, comprised of having multiple, concurrent, non-regular, or transactional partners, condom non-use, drug use, and visiting bars, changed for individuals over time using Sankey diagrams. We then examined whether logistic regression models incorporating life-course events-namely, changes in marital or employment  status,  in-migration,  or  birth  of  a  child-were  more  strongly  associated  with  changes  in  HIV  risk behaviour than models using only sociodemographic variables. Finally, we compared how well socio­ demographic, HIV risk behaviour, and life-course events were associated with the person's risk of HIV acquisition as follows: we used logistic regression to identify which states (divided into sociodemographic, HIV risk behaviour, and life-course events) were most strongly associated with risk of HIV acquisition; based on this we use three models (corresponding to the three divisions) to identify the top 20% of individuals predicted to be  at risk of acquiring HIV by each model, and computed what proportion of the actual HIV infection events occurred in that group.

Findings Between 1998 and 2021, 21 213 individuals were interviewed at least twice, contributing a total of 34  212 participant observations. In this setting, individuals had periods of HIV risk lasting less than 3 years; only 12·3% (102 of 831) of those reporting transactional sex had also reported this in the previous round. We found that life-course events such as changes in marital status, employment status, and in-migrant status were associated with these changes in HIV risk behaviour. Using life-course events, particularly ones related to changes in marital status, 23% and 30% more HIV acquisitions were identified than using HIV risk behaviours or sociodemographic information, respectively.

Interpretation HIV risk changes dynamically in this population, and life-course events could be a powerful way to understand changes in HIV risk behaviour and risk of HIV acquisition.

## Introduction

HIV prevention efforts are central to meeting the goal of ending AIDS  as a public health threat. For  HIV prevention to be effective, people need to be connected with appropriate knowledge and technologies to enable protection during periods of their lives when they are at risk of acquiring HIV. 1

In sub-Saharan Africa, most HIV transmission occurs from unprotected sex with a partner who is HIV-positive and who is virally unsuppressed. Transmission is influenced  by  factors that increase susceptibility or transmissibility, including the number and type of sexual partner, and condom non-use, which we refer to here as sexual  risk  behaviour.  Prevention  research,  particularly for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), has examined how sexual risk behaviour changes over time, which Haberer and  colleagues 1 termed  dynamic  HIV  acquisition  risk behaviours.  These  variations  in  an  individual's risk behaviour, occurring over periods of weeks to a few years, have been referred to as seasons of risk. 2

Yet  it  is  also  important  to  understand  what  drives these  changes  in  risk.  Shorter  seasons  of  risk,  lasting weeks, might be due to vacations 2 or holiday festivals, 3 as  well  as  short-term  economic-related  mobility  that might lead to  risks  including  concurrent  partnerships and  engagement  in  transactional  or  commercial  sex. 4 Longer  seasons  of  risk  might  be  driven  by  life-course events. 5 However,  aside  from  first  instance  of  sex, 6,7 there is a scarcity of literature looking quantitatively at how risk behaviour for HIV is patterned with respect to

## Research in context

## Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Embase from database inception to Nov 14, 2024, for papers in English, using the terms ('lifecourse' OR 'life-course' OR 'life course' OR 'divorce' OR 'widow*' OR 'marriage' OR 'employ*' OR 'unemploy*' OR 'child birth' OR '*migration') AND 'HIV' AND 'risk*'. We excluded studies outside sub-Saharan Africa, or those that focused on key populations only. We identified 11 papers that used longitudinal quantitative data to examine how life-course events affected either HIV risk or risk of HIV acquisition in the general population in sub-Saharan Africa.

Migration and mobility were the most commonly examined life-course events. Five studies looked at HIV acquisition and migration. Four studies found that recent migration or mobility were associated with higher risk of HIV acquisition, and one study additionally also showed that the risk of HIV acquisition was the same in non-recent migrants as non-migrants. One study that retrospectively interviewed individuals who had migrated between survey rounds found no difference in HIV incidence or sexual risk behaviour in migrants compared with non-migrants, although the authors noted that their findings could have been due to the small sample of migrants interviewed. Three other studies looked at HIV risk behaviour and migration, finding that mobility or recent migration, or both, were associated with higher sexual risk behaviour. Three studies looked at marital status life-course events. Two studies found a higher risk of HIV acquisition in remarried individuals compared with first-time

life-course  events. 8 Boileau  and  colleagues 9 looked  at HIV status for different sequences of sexual or marital life-course  events  among  women  in  rural  Malawi, whereas Del Fava and colleagues 8 examined the ordering of three specific life-course events (first sex, first marriage or long-term partnership, and first child) for a  cohort  in  Zimbabwe.  Furthermore,  although  selfreported risk behaviour is often used as a proxy for true HIV  risk, these self-reported  behaviours  might  be subject  to  reporting  biases, 10 and  life-course  events could potentially better reflect true HIV risk.

In this paper we used data from a general population cohort  study  in  Manicaland  province,  Zimbabwe,  to investigate  four  types  of  life-course  events:  changes  in marital and employment status, migration, and birth of a  child.  We  investigated  whether  life-course  events  are associated both with the risk of HIV acquisition and with starting a risk behaviour associated with HIV acquisition. We  had  three  research  aims:  (1)  to  quantify  how  an individual's  HIV  risk  behaviour  changed  over  time  in this population; (2) to identify life-course events associated  with  changes  in  HIV  risk  behaviours;  and (3) to examine the extent to which life-course events were associated  with  HIV  acquisition  compared  with  sociodemographic and HIV risk variables.

## Added value of this study

In this study we examined four types of life-course events, and their relationship with both HIV risk behaviour and risk of HIV acquisition. We used a much more detailed set of marital life-course events than has been previously used, and our results highlighted that recent marriage and recent divorce were strongly associated with HIV acquisition, and also examined the effects of birth of a child and changes in employment. We showed that life-course events can identify more instances of HIV acquisition than normally considered sociodemographic and HIV risk factors.

## Implications of all the available evidence

The present study highlights the potential use of examining lifecourse events to predict changes in HIV risk behaviour and HIV acquisition, in that HIV prevention programmes could use this to identify individuals with an elevated need of HIV prevention.

## Methods

## Data sources

We used eight rounds of data collected from a mixture of towns,  roadside  settlements,  and  rural  sites,  including tea  and  forestry  estates  (appendix    p  2)  in  Manicaland province, Zimbabwe. Rounds 1-6 were conducted between  1998  and  2013,  with  a  2-3  year  gap  between rounds, and rounds 7-8 took place in 2018-19 and 2021. In each round, participants aged 15 years and older were interviewed  regarding  sociodemographic  and  HIV  risk behaviour, and in rounds 1-7 dried blood spot samples were  collected  for  HIV  testing.  Further  information  is given in the appendix (pp 1-2).

Follow-up  rates  in  rounds  1-6  ranged  between  48% and  61%,  with  most  loss-to-follow-up  being  due  to migration. 8 However,  due  to  the  difference  in  the  sites used in later rounds (appendix p 2) and the 6-year interval between rounds 6 and 7, follow-up in round 7 was 3%, increasing to 48% in round 8.

Written informed consent was obtained for all participants  before  survey  participation.  For  participants aged younger than 18 years, written informed consent was obtained  from  a  parent  or  guardian,  and  assent  was obtained from the child. Ethical approval for the Manicaland study was obtained from the Imperial College See Online for appendix Research  Ethics  Committee  and  the  Medical  Research Council of Zimbabwe.

## Procedures

We considered four types of life-course event: (1) change in  marital  status;  (2)  change  in  employment  status; (3) in-migration; and (4) birth of a child. We inferred that a marriage or employment status life-course event (such as recent marriage) had occurred when a person reported different statuses in the current round and the previous round  in  which  they  participated.  Because  time  since migration  and  time  since  most  recent  birth  of  a  child were asked in an interview, recent in-migrant (in-migrants refers to individuals who had moved into the village) and recent  birth  of  a  child  were  defined  by  time  since  the event: less than 2 years was defined as recent for both. Individuals  for  whom  the  event  had  happened  longer than 2 years ago were classed as non-recent. In this study, the married category included both formal marriage and long-term cohabiting partnerships. The appendix (pp 2-3) shows all life-course events and definitions.

We considered seven self-reported HIV risk behaviour indicators (appendix p 3): (1) having multiple partners in the past year; (2) having concurrent partners at the time of interview; (3) having a recent (as defined in the appendix p 3) non-regular partner; (4) engaging in transactional sex in  the  past  month;  (5)  not  using  a  condom  at  last  sex; (6) using drugs at present; and (7) visiting a bar in the past month.  These indicators were chosen because they reflected the contemporaneous risk of acquiring HIV and were  consistently  available  across  multiple  rounds.  The last  indicator  (visiting  a  bar  in  the  past  month)  had been  shown  to  be  associated  with  HIV  acquisition  in Manicaland, 11 and  incorporated  risk  coming  from  the sexual network of individuals frequenting bars and beer halls. 12 We  defined  a  change  in  a  risk  behaviour  as reporting not having that behaviour followed by reporting that behaviour, with the change assigned to the round in which the individual first reported the risk behaviour.

We  did  not  include  potential  HIV  risk  behaviour indicators  where  large  changes  between  rounds  were driven  by  national  HIV  policy  or  programmes  rather than individual life-course events (for example, knowledge  of  partner's  HIV  status,  which  would  be modified through increased HIV  testing). Because changes were made in the wording of the questions for some of these indicators (appendix p 3), before further analysis, the prevalence of a response was plotted as a bar chart  by  round  to  examine  if  there  was  any  noticeable change between rounds when the wording changed.

Participant serostatus data were available in rounds 1-7 . Analogous to changes in risk behaviour, we defined HIV acquisition as when a participant had a negative serostatus followed by a positive serostatus. We assigned the event of the HIV acquisition to the round in which the participant's serostatus was first positive. As such, HIV acquisition was only defined between rounds 2 and 7 .

## Statistical analysis

For  all  analyses,  we  use  a  dataset  whereby  each  entry (termed a participant observation) represented an occasion on which a person was interviewed again, and which recorded the current status of the person and the changes in their status since their previous interview .

To  quantify  how  an  individual's  HIV  risk  behaviour changed over time (aim 1), we used descriptive analysis through Sankey diagrams to quantify the extent to which change in behaviour occurred within the cohort.

To identify life-course events associated with changes in HIV  risk  behaviours  (aim  2),  we  created  a  logistic regression  model  for  each  HIV  risk  behaviour,  with a change in risk behaviour as the outcome variable. The independent  variables  for  these  models  were  socio­ demographic variables  (sex,  age  [in  10-year  age  groups: 15-24  years,  25-34  years,  35-44  years,  45-54  years,  and 55  years  and  older],  site  type,  and  having  secondary education; we also included an interaction term for age group  and  sex,  motivated  by  the  known  differences  in HIV incidence by age between men and women 13 ) and the survey round. We referred to this model as the base model for risk behaviour. For each life-course event type, we  then  compared  this  base  model  against  the  model where  that  life-course  event  type  was  added,  using  the Akaike information criterion (AIC), a measure of goodness-of-fit taking into account the simplicity of the model.  We  computed  the  difference  in  AIC  (ΔAIC risk ) between the life-course event model and the base model. A  value  of  ΔAIC risk of  -10  or  less  meant  that  the  base model  had  essentially  no  support  compared  with  the model incorporating the life-course event type, 14 which we took  as  evidence  that  the  life-course  event  model  was substantially  better  at  predicting  the  change  in  a  given HIV risk behaviour.

To examine the extent to which life-course events were associated  with  HIV  acquisition  compared  with  sociodemographic and HIV risk variables (aim 3),  we  used logistic regression with HIV acquisition as the outcome, adjusting  for  survey  round  as  a  proxy  for  the  timevarying HIV prevalence and incidence in this population. We  calculated  the  odds  ratio  of  HIV  acquisition  with respect to each life-course event in separate regression models. We also created separate regression models for the  sociodemographic  statuses  defined  in  the  base model  from  aim  2,  and  for  each  current  HIV  risk behaviour  (not  the  change  in  behaviour).  We  then established how different sets of variables were associated with the person's risk of HIV acquisition. We did  this  by  using  three  models  to  identify  the  20% predicted  to  have  the  highest  risk  of  HIV  acquisition, and computing what proportion of actual HIV infection events occurred in that group. The greater the proportion, the better the method is at predicting risk. The three models we compared were: (1) using sociodemographic information only (ie,  how  many mathematical models and HIV prevention programmes represented  risk);  (2)  using  HIV  risk  behaviours  only; and (3) using life-course event variables only.

In  all  analyses,  we  excluded  participant  observations where  the  sociodemographic  variables  (sex,  age,  has secondary  education,  and  site  type)  were  not  available. Since  the previous  life-course state  was  required  to construct the model (eg, the recent marriage event), this meant that all analyses were subsetted to round 2 onwards. We additionally excluded participant observations where the required life-course events were unavailable. Since the question about birth of a child was only available up to round 6, we did two sets of analysis for aims 2 and 3: first, an  analysis  restricted  to  rounds  2-6  including  this  lifecourse event; and second, a sensitivity analysis using data from rounds 2-8 but excluding this life-course event. The restriction in the primary analysis also avoided the effects of  the  long  gap  between  rounds  6  and  7,  different  site composition, the change in the question regarding drug use  in  rounds  7-8,  and  any  effects  of  the  COVID-19 pandemic on behaviour in round 8. Finally, in aim 3, we did  not  restrict  the  analysis  to  individuals  who  were negative  for  HIV  initially,  because  the  objective  was  to identify HIV acquisition in a population where HIV status might be unknown beforehand. We assessed the role of missing  data  by  using  multiple  imputation  to  generate datasets for which the analyses of aims 2 and 3 were then repeated (appendix pp 10-11, 18-20).

All  analyses  was  done  in  R  version  4.3.0.  Sankey diagrams  were  created  using  the  ggsankey  package. Logistic regression was conducted using the glm.cluster() command from the miceadds package version 3.16-18   to account for clustering from individuals participating in multiple rounds.

Of note, there was a large increase in reported transactional sex in round 3; because this might have corresponded to an  economic  downturn  where  Zimbabwe's  economy contracted  by  18%  compared  with  the  year  before, 15 we excluded transactional sex data from round 3 because this might have been driven by economic factors outside the current life-course scope. 16

Sankey  diagrams  (figure),  comparing  responses  in a  given  round  with  the  previous  round  in  which  the individual participated, showed that an individual's risk behaviour was likely to change between rounds. Of the 831 participant observations where the participant reported  currently  engaging  in  transactional  sex,  only 102 times (12·3%) had they also engaged in transactional sex in the previous round. Of the other 729, the median time between the two interviews was 2·8 years (IQR 2·5-3·1 years).

The appendix (p 9) shows the proportion of participant observations,  by  sex,  where  a  risk  behaviour  that  was reported  in  the  current  round  was  also  reported  in  the previous round. Condom non-use at last sex was generally consistently reported between rounds. However, the other HIV risk behaviours were much less consistently reported. Only 40·8% (1047 of 2564), 36·2% (405 of 1118), and  41·0%  (1359  of  3312)  of  individuals  who  reported multiple, concurrent, or non-regular partners, respectively, in a given round reported the same in the previous round. Although visiting a bar was reported consistently (3397 [66·9%] of 5079), only 30·2% (514 of 1700) of those reporting  drug  use  also  reported  this  in  the  previous round. Thus, 69·8% of those using drugs in a given round reported not having been doing so in the previous round. Aside from condom non-use at last sex and engaging in transactional sex, men who had a HIV behaviour risk in the current round were more likely to report also having it in  the  past  than  women  (appendix  p  9),  suggesting  that turnover of risk is higher in women.

For aim  2,  we  found  that  changes  in  HIV  risk behaviours were associated with life-course events. The fit  of  the  logistic  regression  models  for  each  HIV  risk improved when adding each life-course event (table 2). Using the cutoff of an ΔAIC risk of -10 or less, changes in marital or employment status improved the fit for almost all  HIV  risk  behaviours;  when  using  rounds  1-8,  the change in AIC was larger than when just using rounds 1-6 due to the larger number of observations. Changes in migrant status and birth of a child improved the fit for condom  non-use at last sex only. Using multiple imputation  to  address  missing  data  largely  did  not modify the results, with only changes in migrant status and birth of a child now improving the fit for transactional sex (appendix pp 10-11).

## Discussion

We found that in this largely rural Zimbabwean population, although only a small proportion of individuals  had  HIV  risk  behaviours  at  a  given  time, there was a high turnover in risk behaviours, and a much larger population might have had these risks at one or more times during their life. Moreover, these changes in risk  appear  to  be  tied  to  life-course  events,  such  that a recent change in circumstances for an individual was associated  with  their  risk  of  HIV  acquisition,  more  so than  their  own  self-reported  risk  behaviours.  These findings  suggest  a  new  way  of  screening  for  HIV  risk behaviours and that HIV prevention programmes could use this information to prioritise specific populations.

Less than one in two men and one in three women who reported a risk behaviour had reported the same behaviour in their previous interview a median  of 2·8 years beforehand. We showed that changes in marital status, employment status, and migration status were all important  in  describing  how  individuals  acquired  new HIV risk factors. These changes in HIV risk factors were more  common  in  recently  married  individuals  and individuals  in  a  long-term  marriage  who  had  been previously  married,  compared  with  those  who  were  in a long-term first marriage. Recent in-migrants were also more likely to have these risks than long-term in-migrants. Finally,  we  showed  that  HIV  acquisition  was  more strongly associated with life-course events than HIV risk factors, and we illustrated this finding by showing that, in this setting, the method of identifying individuals through life-course events would find 23% more individuals who seroconverted than the method of using these HIV risk factors.

This  analysis  was  limited  to  some  extent  by  the  selfreported  nature  of  HIV  risks.  HIV  risk  behaviour  and HIV incidence 17 were both known to have changed over time, both due to specific programmes such as voluntary counselling  and  testing 18 as  well  as  AIDS-differential mortality, 13 although the results of this analysis were similar when only considering the first six  rounds  (1999-2012)  compared  with  all  eight  rounds (up to 2019). The median interval between survey rounds was  2·8  years,  which  meant  that  the  data  could  not establish  the  sequence  of  the  life-course  event  and  the HIV acquisition, and we also did not make any adjustment for between-individual differences in the period between the  two  rounds.  The  current  analysis,  using  simple logistic regression, did not fully make  use of the longitudinal nature of the cohort, and prediction modelling could more robustly assess these associations in  future.  We  also  had  little  information  on  the  sexual partners  themselves,  and  there  is the possibility of residual confounding. There was a high rate of attrition in  the  cohort.  Individuals  with  missing  data  were  also excluded; and although the findings were largely unchanged using multiple imputation to address missing data, if these data were not missing at random, this might have affected the conclusions. However, the use of data from  this  long-running  cohort  provided  a  rare  insight into  how  individual-level  behaviour  and  risk  of  HIV acquisition were associated with life-course events.

A  further  potential  limitation  was  that  marital  status incorporated  different  forms  of  union.  Marital  status included  both  formally  married  (either  through  civil  or religious ceremony or customary marriage where a lobola 19 [bride  price]  is  paid),  as  well  as  long-term  cohabiting, which occurs when paying the lobola might take a long time. 20 Although we have shown the importance of marital status,  future  work  could  examine  whether  life-course events  from  specific  types  of  union  are  more  strongly associated with HIV risk behaviours and HIV acquisition.

These results are consistent with existing findings on life-course  events,  particularly  migration,  although  no other studies looked at associations between life-course events and acquisition. A cross-sectional analysis showed an association between short-term mobility and concurrency in Zimbabwe, 21 indicated that migrant men in South Africa were more likely to have casual partners, 22 and indicated that mobility was associated in Kenya and Uganda with higher levels of sexual partner concurrency; 23 and  in  Kenya,  qualitative  work  highlighted  increases in transactional sex among migrant women. 24 In the Zimbabwe  context, qualitative interviews  found returning migrants have reported multiple partners. 25 In a  longitudinal  analysis  in  rural  Kenya  and  Uganda, mobility  was  associated  with  higher  risk  partnerships (casual, commercial, or inheritor or inherited partner). 26 Boileau and colleagues 9 looked at HIV status for different sequences of sexual or marital life-course events among women in rural Malawi, finding that HIV prevalence was lowest  among  women  who  married  their  first  sexual partner,  whereas  women who delayed first sex and did not marry their first partner were, once married, more likely to be HIV-positive than those who did marry their first  partner  after  delayed  first  sex.  Qualitative  work  in Manicaland shows that fidelity changes over the course of  marriage. 27 Del  Fava  and  colleagues 8 showed that, in Manicaland,  both  having  a  child  before  marriage  and delayed  parenthood  were  associated  with  higher  HIV prevalence than having a child shortly after marriage.

Variations in sexual risk behaviour have been observed over  short  periods  in  trials.  In  a  PrEP  trial  among adolescent  girls  and  young  women  in  Zimbabwe  and South Africa, Velloza and colleagues 28 found decreases in self-reported HIV risk behaviour measured at intervals of once  every  3  months  for  1  year,  with  similar  findings coming from another PrEP trial in Kenya, 29 although some changes might be due to the Hawthorne effect (whereby participants  might  modify  their  behaviour  due  to  the knowledge  that  they  are  being  observed).  Mathematical modelling  studies  have  tried  to  account  for  changes  in sexual  risk  behaviour  to  look  at  how  the  effect  of  PrEP changes  when  aligned  with  these  risks, 30,31 although  the broader implications of such changes have not yet been investigated in detail.

Finally,  given  the  potential  for  life-course  events  to  be strongly associated with HIV acquisition in Manicaland, it is important to assess external validity by trying to replicate these findings in other settings, both in Zimbabwe and elsewhere,  and  especially  by  using  questions  on  the timing of marital status events to allow better resolution between changes in marital status and HIV acquisition. If HIV acquisition risk does spike around the time of lifecourse events, prevention programmes could benefit from providing services accordingly, for example by providing services for those registering a marriage, a birth of a child, or a death of a spouse, or those migrating.

HIV  risk  behaviour  is  dynamic  in  Manicaland.  Lifecourse  events,  in  particular  those  related  to  marital status, could potentially give a new and powerful way to understand  changes  in  HIV  risk  behaviour  and  HIV acquisition.
