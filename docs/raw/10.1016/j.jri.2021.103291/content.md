## 1. Introduction

In 2019, approximately 38 million (31.6 - 44.5 million) people were living with HIV globally (UNAIDS, 2020). Although the epidemic varies between countries and regions, Eastern and Southern Africa remain the most affected (UNAIDS, 2020). In sub-Saharan Africa, 58 % of new in­ fections were documented in women, with young women and adolescent girls accounting for 25 % of all infections. Young women of reproductive age remain one of the most vulnerable and affected groups, with prev­ alent infection significantly greater than in males of the same age (de Oliveira et al., 2017).

Given that heterosexual transmission of HIV across the genital mucosa is the most common route of infection in women, an in-depth understanding of the biological mechanisms associated with HIV risk in the female genital tract  (FGT)  is  essential  for  effective  control  of  the  epidemic.  Genital  pro-inflammatory  cytokines  are  welldescribed  biological  co-factors  to  HIV  risk.  Increased  levels  of  pro-inflammatory  cytokines  in  the  FGT  have been  associated  with  a  3-fold  higher-risk  of  acquiring  HIV,  presumably  through  involvement  in  barrier compromise and the recruitment of highly activated HIV target cells to the site of initial viral infection and replication. Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and bacterial vaginosis (BV) are suggested possible contrib­ utors to genital inflammation in the FGT, and this, coupled with the relationship between genital inflammation and HIV risk, underscores the importance of effective treatment of STI and BV in the promotion of women ' s health. In most low- and middle-income countries, STIs are treated syndromically, a practice providing rapid treatment  without  identifying  the  infection  source.  However,  this  approach  has  been  associated  with  overdiagnosis  and  the  overuse  of  drugs.  Further,  because  many  women  with  STIs  are  asymptomatic,  syndromic management also fails to treat a vast proportion of infected women. Although several studies have explored the role  of  STIs  and  the  vaginal  microbiome  on  genital  inflammation  and  HIV  risk,  the  impact  of  STI  and  BV management on genital inflammation remains poorly understood. This review aimed to collate the evidence on how BV and STI management efforts affect genital inflammation and the genital microbiome in women.

Other host-related factors include exogenous and endog­ enous hormones, epithelial barrier integrity, access to high frequencies of  HIV  target  cells,  sexually  transmitted  infections  (STIs),  genital microbiome  composition,  and  the  presence  of  genital  inflammation (Anahtar et al., 2015; Ding et al., 2010; Fichorova, 2009). ## 2. Association between genital inflammation and HIV acquisition risk

Inflammation is considered the natural immune response following injury  or  infection.  Genital  inflammation  is  often  characterised  by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in the FGT (Masson et al., 2015). These cytokines promote the migration and activation  of  several  immune  cells,  such  as  T  cells  (CD4 + ,  CCR5 + , CXCR4 + ) (Nkwanyana et al., 2009), dendritic cells (DCs) (Haase, 2010) and macrophages in the cervicovaginal mucosal surface, presumably enhancing viral access (Haase, 2010). Inflammation usually results in epithelial  barrier  disruption,  which  provides  a  portal  for  viral  entry (Appay  and  Sauce,  2008).  Therefore,  inflammation  in  the  FGT  may promote an environment conducive to HIV infection, replication, and pathogenesis by providing the virus access to a steady supply of sus­ ceptible  target  cells  (Zhang  et  al.,  1999).  The  relationship  between inflammation, immune cell activation and HIV risk has been shown in non-human  primate  models,  where  simian  immunodeficiency  virus (SIV)  infection  after  a  mucosal  challenge  has  been  associated  with significantly higher pre-infection levels of susceptible target cells and/or pro-inflammatory and chemotactic cytokine concentrations (Carnathan et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 1999). In macaques, the topical anti-inflammatory  glycerol  monolaurate  downregulated  chemokine concentrations and decreased the frequencies of susceptible target cells in the genital tract (Li et al., 2009). Similarly in humans, oral adminis­ tration  of  the  anti-inflammatory  acetylsalicylic  acid  in  women  was associated with a significant decrease in the proportions of HIV target cells (Th17 and CD4 + CCR5 + cells) in the genital tract (Lajoie et al., 2018). Further, in vitro studies of lactic acid-treated vaginal and cervical epithelial  cells  describe  associations  decreased  concentrations  of  in­ flammatory cytokines, IL-6 and IL-8 and increased anti-inflammatory IL-1RA  (Hearps  et  al.,  2017).  Taken  together,  these  findings  suggest that  efforts  to  reduce  genital  inflammation  may  impact  HIV  risk  in humans.

Understanding the various causes of genital inflammation and their relative consequences to HIV risk is, therefore, critical to the design of effective  biomedical  interventions  to  prevent  HIV  infection.  Genital cytokine  concentrations  are  impacted  by  several  factors,  including seminal  fluid  exposure  (Sharkey  et  al.,  2012),  genital  microbiome composition (Anahtar et al., 2015) and genital infections such as STIs (Masson et al., 2014). Here we discuss viral, bacterial and protozoal contributors to inflammation in the FGT.

## 2.1. Bacterial and protozoal sexually transmitted infections

Curable STIs such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis, and Trichomonas vaginalis have been associated with elevated levels of genital  tract  pro-inflammatory  cytokines  (e.g.  IL-1 β ,  IL-6,  TNFα , CXCL10, CCL20, CCL4, and RANTES) (Fichorova et al., 2006; Masson et  al.,  2014;  Mlisana  et  al.,  2012).  While  genital  inflammation  is essential for infection clearance, it may also be associated with disrup­ tions of the epithelial barrier, enabling HIV and STI-linked bacteria and parasites  to  enter  and  establish  infection.  Several  studies  have  docu­ mented similar genital inflammatory cytokine concentrations (Mlisana et al., 2012; Masson et al., 2019; Jespers et al., 2017) or similar fre­ quencies of cervicitis in women with or without STI symptoms (Bogaerts et al., 1999), suggesting that the inflammatory impact of STIs can occur independently of clinical manifestation of the infection. Furthermore, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) can occur in the absence of clinical symptoms of STIs (Wiesenfeld et al., 2002). Taken together, these ob­ servations suggest that asymptomatic and symptomatic HIV infections are associated with similarly increased HIV risk. Improvement on the standard of care identifying asymptomatic infections is therefore war­ ranted,  considering  most  infected  individuals  do  not  clear  infections spontaneously.

Neisseria gonorrhoeae , a sexually transmitted Gram-negative bacterium, has been shown to elicit a pro-inflammatory response and expand populations of susceptible primary dendritic cells and CD4 + T cells, promoting the establishment of a productive HIV infection (Ding et  al.,  2010). Chlamydia  trachomatis activates  the  NFκ B  signaling pathway  known  to  increase the production of  chemokines  and pro-inflammatory cytokines (Andersen et al., 2006), and is associated with  tissue  damage  (Hvid  et  al.,  2007)  and  leukocyte  influx  at  the epithelium (Wong et al., 2019). Trichomonas vaginalis is an anaerobic protozoan  that  stimulates  an  inflammatory  response  coupled  with punctate  haemorrhages  that  compromise  the  integrity  of  the  genital epithelial  barrier  (Fichorova  et  al.,  2013).  The Trichomonas  vaginalis lipophosphoglycan is associated with the production of cytokines in the cervicovaginal epithelium in vitro (Fichorova et al., 2006), including IL-8 that stimulates the chemotaxis of neutrophils in the endothelium, and MIP-3 α that facilitates immune cell transmigration and dendritic cell maturation (Fichorova et al., 2006). Since these STIs induce inflamma­ tion and leukocyte migration to sites of infection, they may facilitate HIV infection if left untreated (Andersen et al., 2006; Fichorova et al., 2006, 2013; Ding et al., 2010).

## 2.2. Viral sexually transmitted infections

Highly prevalent viral STIs such as herpes simplex virus types 1 and 2 (HSV-1 and HSV-2) and human papillomaviruses (HPV) have also been shown to increase HIV acquisition risk (Liebenberg et al., 2019; Shannon et al., 2017; Looker et al., 2020; Stefanidou et al., 2013). Herpesviruses, particularly HSV-2, are thought to increase HIV acquisition risk through multiple mechanisms, including mucosal barrier disruption, and increasing the influx of CCR5-expressing cells (Shannon et al., 2017; Horbul et al., 2011; Fakioglu et al., 2008). Once the epithelial cells are infected, HSV-2 establishes latency with periodic reactivation (Horbul et al., 2011). HSV-2 infection is associated with the release of chemo­ kines and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFNγ and 1L-15, which indirectly  facilitate  HIV  replication  (Parr  and  Parr,  2000).  Mucosal reactivation may not manifest clinically (Posavad et al., 2017), but is nonetheless associated with an influx of neutrophils, natural killer cells, macrophages, and CD4 + T lymphocytes (Krzyzowska et al., 2013; Lund et al., 2006; Koelle et al., 2000). The prolonged subclinical persistence and enrichment of HIV target cells in the genital tract is likely associated with the inability of anti-herpes therapy to minimise HIV acquisition among infected individuals (Mujugira et al., 2013). Effective preventive measures are required to limit inflammation caused by HSV-2 and its impact on HIV susceptibility risk in the genital tract, especially in high HIV endemic settings.

Human  papillomaviruses  are  the  most  common  viral  STIs,  with multiple genotypes associated with cervical cancers and genital warts, respectively (zur Hausen, 2009). Prevalent HPV infection, irrespective of the sub-type has been  associated with HIV  susceptibility in meta-analysis  studies  (Houlihan  et  al.,  2012;  Lissouba  et  al.,  2013). Although  HPV  is  often  capable  of  effectively  evading  the  immune response (McMurray et al., 2001), cell-mediated immune responses are essential for wart regression (van Seters et al., 2008) and in controlling HPV-associated  neoplasia  (Westrich  et  al.,  2017).  Furthermore,  both HPV infection and clearance have been associated with increased levels of chemokines and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNFα , TNFβ , MIP-1 α , GM-CSF, IL-8, and MIP-1 β ), and with adaptive immune response-related cytokines in the female genital tract (IL-2, IL-4, SCD40L) (Liebenberg et al., 2019; Mhatre et al., 2012). Since HSV and HPV infections prime the HIV associated immune response in the genital tract, and, consid­ ering  that  these  viruses  cannot  be  currently  cured,  preventive  in­ terventions are of utmost importance.

## 2.3. The genital microbiome

The genital microbiome can modulate genital immunity and, there­ fore,  influence  the  degree  of  defence  against  invading  pathogens

(Aldunate et al., 2015). There is growing evidence of infant microbiota being populated with maternal bacteria at birth from the vagina or skin, in  cases  of  natural  birth  or  cesarean  section,  respectively  (Domi­ nguez-Bello et al., 2010; Mueller et al., 2015). Furthermore, bacterial communities  observed  at  birth  across  the  infant ' s  skin,  oral  cavity, nasopharynx, and gut are largely undifferentiated, which is more likely that  the  vaginal  niche  of  the  female  infant  has  similar  communities (Dominguez-Bello et al., 2010). Changes in the normal flora have long been associated with reproductive health sequelae such as pelvic in­ flammatory disease, miscarriage and pre-term birth (Brown et al., 2019; Freitas  et  al.,  2018).  A  genital  tract  not  dominated  by Lactobacillus species may facilitate the transmission of HIV and other STIs, and in­ crease the risk of adverse reproductive outcomes in women (Anahtar et  al.,  2015;  Brown  et  al.,  2019). Lactobacillus species,  particularly L.  iners,  L.  crispatus,  L.  gasseri,  and  L.  jensenii are  well  established  as optimal vaginal commensal organisms. By producing lactic acid, these lactobacilli play a critical role in inhibiting the colonisation and survival of reproductive tract pathogens, such as HSV-2, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and uropathogenic Escherichia coli (Buv ´ e et al., 2014; Van De Wijgert et  al.,  2008).  However,  due  to  the  absence  of  lactic  acid-producing lactobacilli,  the  prevalence of non-optimal vaginal flora (or bacterial vaginosis;  BV)  has  been  associated  with  an  increased  risk  of  HIV acquisition and transmission in observational studies (Buv ´ e et al., 2014). Many of these associations are, however, confounded by factors such as age,  the  number of sexual partners, partner microbiome, and unpro­ tected sexual intercourse. It is also unclear whether exogenous patho­ gens or opportunistic endogenous microbiota cause BV, or whether it results from microbial communities that have lost their functional status due to disturbances in the vaginal environment (Aldunate et al., 2015). Additional, comprehensive studies are needed to fully understand the relationship between the female genital microbiome and HIV risk.

Evidence exists to support a relationship between diverse anaerobes and increased levels of the genital cytokines (IL-1 α , IL-1 β , TNFα , IFNγ , IL-10, IL-8, IL-12p70, IL-4, MIP-1 α and MIP-1 β ) and the cellular profiles (CD38 + HLA-DR + ; CCR5 + CD4 + T cells) associated with HIV infection risk (Anahtar et al., 2015; Gosmann et al., 2017a). BV is associated with elevated levels of IL-1 β , IL-8, and RANTES and with low levels of IP-10 and IL-1RA (Kyongo et al., 2015; Shannon et al., 2017; Mitchell and Marrazzo, 2014; Rebbapragada et al., 2008). BV is also associated with increased frequencies of activated HIV target cells in the cervix (Gos­ mann  et  al.,  2017b;  Rebbapragada  et  al.,  2008).  Conversely,  lactic acid-producing L. crispatus and L. jensenii are associated with reduced concentrations  of  pro-inflammatory  cytokines  at  the  FGT  (Gosmann et al., 2017a; Anahtar et al., 2015), and with reduced frequencies of activated  T  cells  (CCR5 + CD4 + T  cells)  in  the  cervix  (Mitchell  and Marrazzo, 2014). The effects of BV may facilitate local HIV replication and result in increased genital HIV shedding from HIV-infected women, potentially placing their male partners at higher risk of infection (Cohen et al., 2019). Thus, maintaining an optimal cervicovaginal microbiome is  of  important  public  health  benefit,  and  a  better  understanding  of long-term consequences of cervicovaginal dysbiosis in women at high risk for HIV infection is critical to developing more effective HIV pre­ vention and treatment strategies.

## 3. Implications of treatment on genital inflammation

## 3.1. Management of sexually transmitted infections

In most low- and middle-income countries, STIs are diagnosed and treated  syndromically.  International  guidelines  recommend  vaginal discharge syndrome in symptomatic women being treated with 1 g oral Azithromycin,  2  g  of  oral  Metronidazole,  and  250  mg  Ceftriaxone intramuscular injection (IMI) (CDC, 2015). The combination of these antimicrobial  agents  targets Chlamydia trachomatis, Trichomonas vagi­ nalis and Neisseria gonorrhoea , respectively. Although this approach is convenient in treating mixed infections, clear drawbacks to syndromic management of STIs exist (Garrett et al., 2017), including social stigma, the inability to curb asymptomatic infections, the ineffective treatment of other causes of vaginal discharge, and in some regions, the failure to notify partners (Morris et al., 2014; Mlisana et al., 2012; Masson et al., 2014).  Enhanced  partner  notification  enables  linkage  to  care  of  the possible infected partner (Cavalcante et al., 2016). Usually, the index patient  is  issued  with  a  partner  notification  card,  allowing  partner identification  upon  healthcare  centre  visit  (Cavalcante  et  al.,  2016). Although this method is effective, associated challenges include factors such  as  stigma,  fears  of  personal  safety  or  reputation,  and  potential inability to notify casual partners (Alam et al., 2010; Wood et al., 2018). The  proper  implementation  of  partner  notification  referral  may  be beneficial  and,  therefore,  essential  to  protect  patients  from  adverse consequences and reach partners who would not otherwise be informed, thereby improving disease intervention.

Vaginal discharge syndrome cannot be accurately identified, because an individual can present with one or a combination of either discharge from the vagina/cervix, dysuria, vulvovaginal itch or lower abdominal pain, and it is a poor predictor of an STI (Mlisana et al., 2012). This often leads to inaccurate treatment, and over and/or under treatment. While the syndromic approach may be better than no STI care, it is far from ideal. The decision to adopt it should be setting-specific, considering the variations in sexually transmitted diseases epidemiology and cost im­ plications (van der Eem et al., 2016). Considering the high epidemiology and complications associated with STIs in our setting (Masson et al., 2014), an urgent need for alternative STI management strategies such as point-of-care tests and other recent technologies are required to tackle the STIs infections.

## 3.1.1. Point-of-care tests for STIs

Recent advances in point-of-care testing have resulted in robust tools that enhance rapid diagnosis and immediate treatment of curable STIs (Huntington et al., 2018; Pai et al., 2012). Rapid diagnosis and treat­ ment  may  reduce  the  burden  of  overtreatment  posed  by  syndromic management  and  loss  to  follow-up  (Tucker  et  al.,  2013).  Modelling suggests the potential benefits of point-of-care tests in high STI endemic settings  (Hui  et  al.,  2013).  Currently,  there  are  several  available point-of-care tests for diagnosing common STIs such as Chlamydia tra­ chomatis , Neisseria gonorrhoeae , and Trichomonas vaginalis . These include nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) and non-NAATs. The widely used NAATs are Xpert CT/NG, TV testing on the GeneXpert platform (Cepheid), binx health io CT/NG Assay (binx health, Inc), the Truelab Real-Time micro PCR system (Molbio Diagnostics Pvt Ltd), and the STI Array  (Randox  Biosciences)  (Herbst  de  Cortina  et  al.,  2016;  Har­ ding-Esch et al., 2018; Adamson et al., 2020). Non-NAATs are generally antigen detection-based tests such as OSOM Trichomonas Rapid Test (Sekisui Diagnostics), BioRapid Chlamydia Ag Test (Biokit SA), and One Step  Gonorrhea  RapiCard  InstaTest  (Cortez  Diagnostics  (Kelly  et  al., 2017; Samarawickrama et al., 2014; Gaydos et al., 2017). Even though these  tests  are  efficient  STI  diagnostic  tools,  they  cannot  detect antimicrobial-resistant infections (Low and Unemo, 2016). Therefore, further  development  of  the  advanced,  ideal  detection  tests  is  still required, as these cannot detect drug resistance in STIs.

## 3.1.2. Antimicrobial resistance in STIs

Antimicrobial resistance in STIs is increasing globally, and it often occurs when microorganisms render regimens ineffective (WHO, 2017). Several factors, such as misuse of medicines, poor infection prevention, and development of mutations, are accelerating antimicrobial resistance (WHO,  2017).  STIs  use  several  antimicrobial  survival  mechanisms, including inactivation of the antibiotic, alteration of the drug binding sites, reduction of membrane permeability, and increased drug efflux (Rasoloson et al., 2002; Rambaran et al., 2019; Lyu et al., 2020). The increasing  frequency  of  drug  resistance  in  STIs  remains  a  cause  for concern. Neisseria  gonorrhoeae strains  have  developed  resistance  to nearly all antibiotics, leaving the third-generation cephalosporins the only effective antibiotics (WHO, 2016). Dual therapy consisting of cef­ triaxone and azithromycin is recommended to minimise further resis­ tance  development  (WHO,  2016).  The  increasing  burden  of  highly resistant Neisseria gonorrhoeae strains to almost all antimicrobial agents globally  highlights  the  looming  threat  of  untreatable Neisseria  gonor­ rhoeae infections (WHO, 2020).

The current antimicrobial agent against trichomoniasis is Metroni­ dazole, a 5-nitroimidazole. While other 5-nitroimidazoles (tinidazole, secnidazole and ornidazole) demonstrated antimicrobial activity against Trichomonas vaginalis , they are not widely available or FDA-approved. Of particular concern is the similar mechanism of action for all 5-nitro­ imidazoles,  posing  the  constant  fear  of  emerging  resistance  to  all possible  5-nitroimidazoles.  Further,  while  Metronidazole  is  generally effective  for  treating Trichomonas vaginalis infection, increasing resis­ tance has been widely reported (Schwebke and Barrientes, 2006; Ghosh et al., 2018). Of particular concern is the unavailability of non-nitroimidazole  antimicrobial  agents.  Refractory  cases  are,  there­ fore, treated with increased metronidazole doses or tinidazole, which is not widely available due to cost implications (Cudmore et al., 2004). Further research is still warranted to identify alternative non-nitroimidazole treatment therapies before all nitroimidazoles are rendered  inactive. Chlamydia  trachomatis is  currently  treated  with macrolides (azithromycin) or tetracycline (doxycycline) globally. Clin­ ical resistance of Chlamydia trachomatis to macrolides or tetracyclines is rare, and these antimicrobial agents are still widely used (P ´ aez-Canro et al., 2019). Given the increasing resistance of some STIs to antimi­ crobial or protozoal agents, the development of new therapies and rapid resistance tests that can detect antimicrobial resistance remains crucial to improve treatment outcomes.

STIs are associated with epithelial microabrasions and the recruit­ ment of HIV target cells to the FGT (Fox and Fidler, 2010). However, it is yet  unclear  whether  STI  treatment  may  sufficiently  limit  genital inflammation and so reduce HIV risk. Women treated for Chlamydia trachomatis with the antimicrobial azithromycin demonstrated decreased levels of cervical IL-8, IL-6, IL-1 β , IP-10, TNFα , IFNγ , and IL-1 α cytokines relative to untreated women (Srivastava et al., 2009; Sperling et al., 2013), implying that appropriate management of curable STIs can reduce FGT inflammation. Furthermore, gonococcal cervicitis treatment or resolution restored plasma CD4, and CD8 T cell counts in women suggesting  that  gonococcal  treatment  can  lead  to  a  restored immune response (Anzala et al., 2000). Since STIs are associated with inflammation and an influx of immune cells, enhanced management and prevention could improve reproductive health outcomes and reduce the risk of HIV infection.

## 3.2. BV treatment

Although BV can resolve naturally without treatment, the Centers for Disease  Control  and  Prevention  (CDC)  recommend  treatment  with Metronidazole (CDC, 2015). Metronidazole can reduce the BV burden in the short-term, but recurrence is common (Ahrens et al., 2020). Swid­ sinski et al. reported the association of BV recurrence with the presence of a Gardnerella vaginalis and Atopobium vaginae in patients treated with oral Metronidazole (Swidsinski et al., 2008). Prolonged metronidazole treatment  for  14  days  can  temporarily  improve  treatment  outcome; however,  the  effect  cannot  be  sustained  due  to  high  BV  recurrence (Schwebke and Desmond, 2007). Biofilm formation mainly caused by Gardnerella vaginalis and other anaerobes has been hypothesised to drive high recurrence rates, but this has not been confirmed in clinical studies (Verwijs et al., 2020; Zwittink et al., 2020). This implies the need for an improved regimen which will cater for biofilm-causing microorganisms that might facilitate recurrence rates.

Because Lactobacilli species are dominant in women without bacte­ rial  vaginosis  (BV),  several  studies  have  investigated  the  use  of  oral probiotic Lactobacillus strains  to  sustain  the  optimal  vaginal  environ­ ment (van de Wijgert et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019; Arooj et al., 2017).

Probiotics may restore the microbial ecosystem and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines in the genital tract when administered in adequate amounts (Mastromarino et al., 2009; Hemalatha et al., 2012). Several  clinical  trials  assessed  the  effectiveness  of Lactobacillus con­ taining  oral  or  intravaginal  tablets  in  the  treatment  of  BV,  restoring optimal commensal flora, and reducing BV recurrence (Mastromarino et al., 2009; Ling et al., 2013; Bradshaw et al., 2012; Hemalatha et al., 2012).  Although  mixed  results  were  observed  in  clinical  trials,  few studies  showed  promising  efficacy  of  probiotics  in  restoring  optimal vaginal flora, implying the alternate use for BV therapy (Mastromarino et al., 2009; Hemalatha et al., 2012; Bradshaw et al., 2012). Even so, probiotics were not successful in reducing recurrence BV and were less effective in eradicating pathogenic microorganisms compared to stan­ dard BV regimen (Bradshaw et al., 2012; Larsson et al., 2008; Ling et al., 2013). These studies highlight the need for novel and ideal probiotic strains that are functional and recently isolated to optimise women ' s health. Furthermore, probiotics are not likely to have side effects or the potential to cause antimicrobial resistance, making them the alternative to current BV treatment therapies (Darmayanti et al., 2019). Alterna­ tively, characterising and understanding the various groups of different bacterial  types  (morphotypes)  using  advanced  molecular  platforms might provide better insight into tailored treatments, hence improving women ' s genital health.

## 3.3. HSV and HPV treatment

While bacterial STIs can be successfully treated, the same cannot be said for common viral STIs. HSV symptoms can be managed with any of the  three  FDA  approved  antivirals  (acyclovir,  valacyclovir,  or  famci­ clovir). While these antivirals can effectively treat the first episode of infection, their effect on latent infections is suboptimal. This is evident by the failure of suppressive anti-herpes treatment in reducing inflam­ mation or HIV acquisition risk in women (Andersen-Nissen et al., 2016; Yi et al., 2014). HPV infection is often capable of clearing spontaneously. However, HPV-associated genital  warts  can  be  treated  with  immune response modifiers like imiquimod, which promotes a TLR-7-mediated local inflammatory response characterised by increased concentrations of IFNα , IFNβ , IFNγ , and TNFα (Tyring et al., 1998). HPV-associated neoplasias can be treated by cryotherapy, surgery, or chemical com­ pounds such as trichloroacetic acid and podophyllin (Hathaway, 2012). HPV vaccines are also available to prevent infection by several onco­ genic  and  wart-associated  HPV  genotypes  (Bergman  et  al.,  2019). Considering  the  relationship  between  HPV  infection,  genital  inflam­ mation and HIV risk (Liebenberg et al., 2019), it is possible that HPV vaccination  may  impact  both  genital  inflammation  and  HIV  risk,  in addition to reducing cervical cancer risk in women. Improved treatment regimens are required to reduce long-term morbidities associated with common viral STIs. However, common viral STIs such as HPV and HSV may be asymptomatic but still contribute to the inflammatory state of the FGT (Mlisana et al., 2012; Masson et al., 2014) and this contribution should be considered when managing BV and bacterial and protozoal STIs.

## 4. Conclusion

STIs and BV are co-factors in facilitating genital inflammation and HIV acquisition risk. Management of STIs and BV have shown the po­ tential to modify the pro-inflammatory environment in the genital tract, emphasising  the  need  for  better  control  of  the  female  genital  tract pathogens  to  reverse  the  immunological  consequences  of  STI-agents. Patient-tailored regimens with adequate support to ensure compliance could improve reproductive health outcomes. Furthermore, longitudinal studies are required to fully elucidate the impact of treatment on the mucosal immune responses associated with STIs and/or BV. These have to account for asymptomatic STIs and the dynamic nature of vaginal microbiota.  Additionally,  reviewing  current  STI  management  care remains crucial to optimise treatment accuracy and prevent the trans­ mission of asymptomatic untreated cases that can facilitate preventable reproductive comorbidities. It should, in part, integrate laboratory tests to address diagnostic accuracy.
