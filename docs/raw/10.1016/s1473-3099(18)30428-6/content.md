## Tenofovir 1% vaginal gel for prevention of HIV-1 infection in women in South Africa (FACTS-001): a phase 3, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial

Sinead Delany-Moretlwe, Carl Lombard, Deborah Baron, Linda-Gail Bekker, Busi Nkala, Khatija Ahmed, Modulakgotla Sebe, William Brumskine, Maposhane Nchabeleng, Thesla Palanee-Philips, Julius Ntshangase, Sidney Sibiya, Emilee Smith, Ravindre Panchia, Landon Myer, Jill L Schwartz, Mark Marzinke, Lynn Morris, Elizabeth R Brown, Gustavo F Doncel, Glenda Gray, Helen Rees

## Summary

Background Young women in southern Africa have substantial risk of HIV acquisition. Female-controlled biomedical interventions are needed to mitigate this risk. We aimed to assess the safety and efficacy of a pericoitally applied tenofovir 1% gel.

Methods We did a phase 3, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial at nine community-based clinical trial sites in South Africa to evaluate the safety and efficacy of tenofovir 1% gel. Sexually active women who were HIV negative and aged 18-30 years were enrolled. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) using sequential participant numbers to either tenofovir 1% gel or a placebo gel (one dose within 12 h before sex and one dose within 12 h after sex [BAT-24 regimen]), using dynamic permuted block sizes of 8 and 16 within each site. Women received monthly HIV-1 testing, risk reduction support, physical examinations, and product dispensing for up to 27 months. The primary efficacy outcome was incident HIV infection and the primary safety outcome was occurrence of grade 2-4 adverse events,  both  analysed  in  the  modified  intention-to-treat  population.  To  assess  the  efficacy  of  tenofovir  gel,  the cumulative probability of HIV infection was calculated for each treatment using the Kaplan-Meier method. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, number NCT01386294.

Findings From Oct 11, 2011, to Aug 29, 2014, 3844 women were screened, 2059 enrolled, and 2029 included in the primary analysis (1032 in the tenofovir group and 1027 in the placebo group); 39 (4%) in the tenofovir group and 36  (4%)  in  the  placebo  group  were  lost  to  follow-up.  123  HIV-1  infections  occurred  over  3036  woman-years  of observation; 61 in the tenofovir group (HIV incidence 4·0 per 100 woman-years, 95% CI 3·1-5·2) and 62 in the placebo  group  (4·0  per  100  woman-years,  3·1-5·2;  incidence  rate  ratio  [IRR]  0·98,  95%  CI  0·7-1·4).  A  higher incidence of grade 2 adverse events was observed in the tenofovir group than in the placebo group (IRR 1·09, 95% CI 1·0-1·2; p=0·02). The most common grade 2 or higher product-related adverse events were hypophosphataemia (n=22  for  tenofovir vs n=22  for  placebo),  genital  symptoms  (n=6  for  tenofovir vs n=2  for  placebo),  or  elevated transaminases (n=2 for tenofovir vs n=2 for placebo). No product-related serious adverse events were reported, and no differences in product-related adverse events (p=0·78), grade 3 events (p=0·64), or grade 4 events (p=0·74) were observed between treatment groups.

Interpretation Overall, pericoital tenofovir gel did not prevent HIV-1 acquisition in this population of young women at risk of HIV infection in South Africa. Alternate safe and effective products that are less user dependent than this product or do not require high adherence are needed.

## Introduction

Although the annual incidence of new HIV infections has declined or stabilised in many populations, women younger than 25 years in Africa account for a fifth of new  HIV  infections  globally. 1 Young  women  in  the region are up to eight times more likely to be infected with  HIV  than  their  same-age  male  counterparts. 2 In the absence of widely available pre-exposure prophylaxis,  HIV  prevention  options  for  women  have been restricted to partner reduction and condom use, strategies  that  are  seldom  under  a  woman's  direct control.

Microbicides, including topical, coitally dependent gels, are a potentially important female-controlled method for HIV  prevention. ## Research in context

## Evidence before this study

A systematic review by Obiero and coworkers of trials of vaginal microbicides for HIV prevention (n=13) found one trial that showed significant protective efficacy of tenofovir 1% gel use when applied pericoitally (risk ratio 0·63, 95% CI 0·43-0·93). Effectiveness data was obtained from a phase 3 trial by Marrazzo and coworkers of daily tenofovir (MTN-003) being done at multiple sites in three countries in sub-Saharan Africa. Two additional studies provided data on safety of vaginally applied tenofovir 1% gel in humans.

The Vaginal and Oral Interventions to  Control  the  Epidemic  trial  (MTN  003), which assessed a daily rather than pericoital regimen of tenofovir  1%  gel,  did  not  show  a  reduction  in  HIV infection  in  the  daily  tenofovir  gel  group. 8 This  finding was attributed to the very low adherence to daily gel use.

Given the attractiveness of a pericoital intervention and the preliminary efficacy data from the CAPRISA-004 trial, the Follow on African Consortium for Tenofovir Studies (FACTS)-001 trial was designed as a phase 3 study of the safety and efficacy of a pericoitally applied tenofovir 1% gel.

## Methods

## Study design and participants

The  study  was  designed  as  a  phase  3,  randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial. Women  were recruited at nine community-based clinical trial sites in South  Africa  and  were  included  in  the  study  if  they tested  negative  on  two  HIV-1  rapid  third-generation antibody tests, were aged 18-30 years, and were sexually active  (defined  as  reporting  at  least  two  episodes  of vaginal intercourse in the previous 30 days). Participants were enrolled if they were not pregnant (breastfeeding women were not excluded), agreed to use an effective modern method of contraception as well as condoms, had no clinical or laboratory evidence of renal, hepatic, or  bone  disease,  and  showed  adequate  understand­ ing prevent HIV-1 acquisition in this population of at-risk women in South Africa. This large phase 3 trial did not support licensure of tenofovir 1% gel for HIV prevention in women. This study highlighted the challenge of products that require near-perfect use to achieve HIV protection.

## Randomisation and masking

Randomisation  of  participants  was  done  in  a  1:1  ratio using dynamic permuted block sizes of 8 and 16 within each  site.  Site  randomisation  lists  were  prepared  by  a statistician who was independent from the trial. The study product was prepackaged by the study funder in one of eight  bins,  and  site  pharmacists  masked  to  allocation dispensed  the  product  to  participants  on  the  basis  of  a sequential participant number and bin allocation provided in a sealed tamper-proof opaque envelope.

Randomisation was to either tenofovir 1% gel or an inert hydroxyethyl  cellulose-based  universal  placebo  gel  that were in identical 4-mL, prefilled, single-use applicators. 9 A minimum  of  ten  applicators  (or  more  depending  on reported  sexual  activity  in  the  previous  month)  were dispensed each month.

All staff and participants were blinded to study group allocation.  Treatment codes were held by an unblinded statistician and study sponsor staff who were responsible for providing instructions for  product  packing  and labelling.  Both  the  statistician  and  sponsor  staff  were based in the USA.

## Procedures

Participants  were  counselled  to  insert  one  dose  of  gel within 12 h before sex and one dose of gel within 12 h after sex, but not more than two doses in a 24 h period (BAT-24  regimen).  Participants  were  then  required  to return all used and unused applicators at each monthly visit  for  adherence  assessment.  Returned  applicators were counted and reconciled against the number dispensed. Self-reported data on sexual frequency in the past 30 days together with gel use on the day of their most recent sex act was collected in a brief questionnaire given face-to-face by a trained interviewer who was not involved in clinical procedures, study counselling, or consent.

At  each  monthly  visit,  participants  were  counselled  and tested for HIV and pregnancy before gel dispensing. Urine and blood samples collected at these visits were assessed for renal, hepatic, and haematological abnormalities. In addition to adherence support, participants were also provided with a comprehensive package of HIV prevention services including  HIV  risk  reduction  counselling  and  testing, provision of condoms and contraception, and treatment for symptomatic sexually transmitted infections. At enrolment and quarterly visits, irrespective of symptoms, participants had a pelvic examination and a cervicovaginal lavage using 10 mL normal saline, which was collected for quantitative drug  assessments.  Vaginal  aspirates  were  collected  at  a single timepoint during one of these visits. Participants were followed up for a maximum of 30 months-ie, 27 months while  taking  the  study  product  and  a  final  study  visit 3 months after product discontinuation.

Study product was immediately withheld if  HIV seroconversion was suspected by HIV rapid testing, and discontinued if HIV-1 infection was confirmed. Tenofovir resistance testing was done by population-based Sanger sequencing of the pol gene on paired samples collected at the  seroconversion  visit  and  3  months  after  serocon­ version. 10

Clinical and laboratory adverse events were evaluated using the Division of AIDS Table for Grading the Severity of  Adult  and  Pediatric  Adverse  Events  (version  1.0, December,  2004;  clarification  August,  2009),  serious adverse events were recorded on the basis of International Council for Harmonisation clinical safety data management definitions, 11 and relationship to study product was recorded by study clinicians. In participants with grade 3 and 4 adverse events judged by the study clinician to be related to study product, the product was temporarily withheld, and resumed when improvement was documented. Participants with a positive pregnancy test also had the product withheld, but could reinitiate product use following an elective or spontaneous termination of pregnancy before 20 weeks of gestation, if  they had two negative pregnancy tests, taken on two different urine samples.

Given  the  importance  of  high  product  adherence  to show efficacy, a comprehensive adherence support programme  was  developed  on  the  basis  of  previous trials. 12-14 Individual comprehension and coping strategies were  addressed  through  participant-centred  counselling using  motivational  interviewing  methods, 14 motivational one-way mobile phone text messages, and simple teaching aids  for  communicating  BAT-24.  These  measures  were augmented  by  a  poster  campaign  and  adherence  clubs based on HIV treatment support clubs introduced during the trial to foster positive trial norms and to emphasise the importance of gel adherence. 15 Methods  to  improve identification  and  counselling  of  participants  with  low adherence  included  visual  inspection  of  returned  used applicators for more objective assessment of gel use, 13 a checklist that focused on participants who missed visits or were in known unstable partnerships, as well as counsellor calculators  and  graphs  to  monitor  individual  adherence trends.  Participants  with  low  adherence  and  those  who reported  barriers  to  adherence  received  more  intensive counselling and were linked to supportive interventions, on the basis  of  the  barriers  identified  (eg,  gender-based violence or mental health services). 16 These interventions were tailored to individual needs and varied according to the locally available resources.

We also did a post-randomisation analysis of cervicovaginal lavage tenofovir drug concentrations using a case-cohort  design,  in  which  a  simple  random sample (randomised using computer-generated random numbers) was drawn from the tenofovir 1% gel group by the unmasked statistician (EB), and was then supplemented by the remaining HIV-positive cases from this study group. Calculation of the random sample size required  for  the  case-cohort  design  was  based  on  the assumption  that  half  of  the  total  endpoints  available would be in the tenofovir group. This number was the total  number  of  assumed  cases.  A  case-cohort  ratio  of 1:3 was used to maximise power and efficiency. Tenofovir concentrations  in  cervicovaginal  lavage  were  assessed using  a  previously  described  ultraperformance  liquid chromatographic-tandem mass-spectrometric method with  a  lower  limit  of  quantification  of  5  ng/mL. 17 A threshold of protection equal to 30 ng/mL (equivalent to 1000 ng/mL in cervicovaginal aspirates) 18 was derived by calculating the median ratio of tenofovir concentrations in  aspirates  and  cervicovaginal  lavages.  Modelling  of cervicovaginal  fluid  concentrations  after  pericoital  use predicts  detectable  concentrations  of  tenofovir  up  to 4  weeks  after  the  last  dose. 19 62  paired  samples  of cervicovaginal fluid and aspirates from FACTS-001 participants  were  evaluated  to  estimate  the  correlation between  these  two  types  of  specimen  sources [ (median ratio of aspirate value:cervicovaginal fluid value equals 33) Only  cervicovaginal  lavage  samples  were  used  in  the assessment of drug concentration and HIV protection, as aspirates were not collected at the onset of the trial.

## Outcomes

The primary efficacy endpoint was incident HIV infection established  by  detection  of  HIV  antibodies  using  two rapid third-generation HIV tests and confirmed by HIV-1 western  blotting  on  two  samples  taken  at  least  1  week apart. Participants who seroconverted in the first 3 months of  follow-up had their enrolment samples evaluated for the presence of HIV-1 RNA. Participants with HIV-1 RNA detected  by  PCR  at  enrolment  were  excluded  from  the primary analysis. Participants who  seroconverted to HIV-1 were followed up for 12 weeks after seroconversion, and subsequently referred to local HIV treatment services in accordance with a standard operating procedure required  by  the  local  regulatory  authorities.  All  HIV endpoints  were  centrally  assessed  and  reviewed  by  an independent  HIV-1  endpoint  adjudication  committee masked to study group assignment. The primary safety endpoints were grades 2, 3, and 4 clinical and laboratory adverse events.

Secondary outcomes of the trial include HSV-2 incidence,  pregnancy  incidence  and  outcomes,  gel  and condom use, HIV-1 incidence following product discontinuation. Among the women who seroconverted, tenofovir resistance, CD4 T-cell count, and HIV viral-load setpoint were secondary outcomes of interest. The effect of tenofovir 1% gel withdrawal on progression of hepatitis B infection was also assessed. Changes in sexual behaviour were assessed as an exploratory outcome. This manuscript describes the primary outcomes of the study and provides brief information on the secondary outcomes relating to pregnancy incidence, as part of safety assessments, and gel use; other secondary and exploratory outcomes will be reported elsewhere.

## Statistical analysis

118  HIV-1  events  were  required  for  the  study  to  have 90% power for a log-rank test to detect a HR of 0·55 for incident  HIV  infections  of  vaginal  tenofovir  1%  gel compared  with  placebo.  The  required  sample  size  was estimated on the basis of the assumption of an annual HIV-1  incidence  of  3·5%  in  the  placebo  group,  equal allocation to the group, a one-sided significance level of 0·025, two planned interim analyses equally spaced with a Lan-Demets α spending function and O'Brien-Fleming stopping boundaries, a loss to follow-up of 13% (including for pregnancies), an enrolment period of 11 months, and follow-up of 27 months. To observe the 118 events required under these assumptions, a cohort of 2600 women was needed.

Analyses were done using STATA version 13. Missing data were considered as missing at random. The primary intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was modified a priori to exclude participants identified as ineligible for enrolment on  the  basis  of  criteria  present  before  randomisation, such as confirmed HIV-1 infection and participants with no HIV testing after randomisation. To assess the efficacy of  tenofovir  gel,  the  cumulative  probability  of  HIV infection  was  calculated  for  each  treatment  using  the Kaplan-Meier method. The difference in incidence curves  was  evaluated  with  a  log-rank  test  stratified  by site. Participants with no HIV infection before their last study  visit  were  censored  on  the  day  of  their  last  HIV test.  Incidence  rate  ratios  (IRRs)  and  95%  CIs  were estimated using a Poisson model with study group and site as the main effects and time on study as the period of risk for HIV. Predefined baseline subgroups were examined to assess whether any effects of tenofovir gel on HIV  incidence were dependent on age group (≥25 years or &lt;25 years), condom use, reported frequency of  anal  sex,  and  HSV-2  serostatus.  For  these  subgroup analyses, formal tests for interaction were done. Baseline factors  associated  with  HIV  incidence  and  adherence were calculated using Cox regression adjusted for baseline covariates. The incidence of adverse events was calculated using the total person-time calculated for the modified ITT population for all comparisons.

Gel adherence at each study visit was calculated for each woman by dividing half the number of returned empty applicators by the number of self-reported sex acts for that month, and was expressed as the percentage of sex acts covered by two doses of gel (assuming one sex act per 24 h period); during analysis, this percentage was categorised as more than 80% (high), 50-80% (intermediate), and less than 50% (low). 7   HIV incidence was compared between the tenofovir and placebo groups within these predefined strata  of  the  proportion  of  sex  acts  covered  by  gel  after randomisation. To investigate the gel adherence trend in the  study,  a  logistic  regression  generalised  estimation equation model was used with the monthly reporting of at least 50% of sex acts covered by gel use modelled on time (month  of  visit).  This  approach  was  used  to  take  into account the repeated measures of coverage within participants.  We  also  assessed  the  association  between baseline characteristics and average gel use in a least eight of ten sex acts using logistic regression.

The  study  was  overseen  by  the  Data  Monitoring Committee. This  trial  is  registered  with  ClinicalTrials. gov, number NCT01386294.

A time-varying Cox regression model with adherence (detectable tenofovir concentration in cervicovaginal lavage at the quarterly visit) as a time-varying exposure and  seroconversion  as  the  outcome,  with  censoring  at the time of last negative HIV test for those who did not seroconvert, was specified as the primary analysis of this case-cohort study. The model was weighted for the casecontrol  sampling  approach  and  adjusted  for  known confounders  including  age,  education,  marital  status, living with parent, baseline HSV-2 status, and stratified by site. Adherence at missed quarterly visits was imputed using the last value was carried forward.

## Results

Between  Oct  11,  2011,  and  Aug  29,  2014,  we  assessed 3844 women  for  eligibility and randomly assigned

2059  eligible  participants  to  either  tenofovir  1%  gel (n=1032)  or  placebo  (n=1027;  figure  1).  The  primary reason for screening failure was HIV infection (401 [22%] of 1785 excluded participants). Of the 30 participants who were randomised but excluded from the final analysis, 25  did  not  return  for  a  visit  after  randomisation  and five had acute HIV  infection at enrolment. Thus, 2029  participants  who  contributed  3036  woman-years (1515 woman-years in the tenofovir group and 1521 woman-years in the placebo group) were included in  the  modified  intention-to-treat  population.  Overall, 1870 (91%) participants (935 [92%] in the tenofovir group and 943 [93%] in the placebo group) completed a study exit visit.

The mean age of participants was 23 years (SD 3) and 1437  (71%)  of  2029  were  younger  than  25  years.  At enrolment,  participant  characteristics  did  not  vary  by study group (table 1).

123 HIV infections met the study endpoint definition and  were  included  in  the  primary  analysis;  61  events occurred in the tenofovir group (HIV incidence 4·0 per 100 woman-years, 95% CI 3·1-5·2) and 62 in the placebo group (4·0 per 100 person-years, 3·1-5·2). No significant difference  in  HIV  incidence  by  treatment  group  was observed (IRR 0·98, 95% CI 0·7-1·4; figure 2). Ten  participants  with  a  complete  set  of  blood  results seroconverted after product discontinu­ ation (six [0·6%] of 1015 in the tenofovir group vs four [0·4%] of 1014 in the placebo  group)  and  relative  incidence  did  not  differ  by treatment group when these participants were included (IRR 1·02, 95% CI 0·7-1·4; table 2).

The results of prespecified baseline subgroup analyses show that there was no significant difference in treatment effect by condom  use,  anal  sex, or  baseline  HSV-2 serostatus (table 2). We did, however, observe an interaction between treatment group and age. In women aged 25 years or younger, tenofovir use (based on assessment of gel use returns)  was  associated  with  lower  HIV  incidence  than placebo  (IRR  0·73,  95%  CI  0·5-1·1),  whereas  in  those older than 25 years, tenofovir use was conversely associated with higher HIV incidence than placebo (IRR 2·23, 95 CI 1·0-5·1;  p interaction =0·01). This finding can be further interpreted  in  the  context  of  significant  differences  in baseline  characteristics  by  age  group,  including  HSV-2 seroprevalence,  sexual  activity  (and  therefore  drug  and HIV exposure), and partner age (appendix).

## Discussion

The FACTS-001 trial of pericoital tenofovir 1% vaginal gel in  a  heterogeneous  population  of  young  South  African women found,  overall,  no  evidence  for  efficacy  in  HIV prevention. Trials of pre-exposure prophylaxis have repeatedly shown that showing effectiveness is depend­ ent on both product efficacy and adherence. 20,21 In this study, with  intensive  and  optimised  adherence  counsel­ ling, evidence  of  gel  use  was  observed  using  both  returned used applicators and tenofovir detection in cervicovaginal lavage  samples  as  markers  of  adherence,  but  overall product  use  was  too  inconsistent  and  insufficient  to achieve concentrations associated with HIV protection.

Among  women  with detectable tenofovir in the cervicovaginal compartment, there was some evidence of a protective effect of tenofovir gel, which was consistent across  several post-randomisation  statistical  analyses. The protective effect,  however,  was  of  lower  magnitude than  that  observed  in  some  of  the  oral  pre-exposure prophylaxis trials  of  tenofovir-containing  tablets. 22 This lower  magnitude  could  be  due  to  the  greater  difficulty in  assessing  adherence  to  event-driven  regimens,  un­ accounted differential confounding effects in the subgroup analyses, heterogeneous coverage of the cervicovaginal  mucosa  by  tenofovir  1%  gel,  or  local environmental factors tempering the anti-HIV activity of the  gel. Some  authors  have  also  suggested  that  microbicides targeting viral components, especially gp120, are much less  effective  in  the  presence  of  semen,  which  could possibly explain the discrepancy between in-vitro studies and  the  outcomes  of  HIV  microbicide  clinical  trials, although this negative effect of semen on efficacy has not been  shown  for  tenofovir. 25,26 Studies  comparing  tissue concentrations before and after sexual intercourse have shown  that  vaginal  tenofovir  bioavailability  decreases during sex, but tenofovir concentrations in tissue appear unaffected  by  sex  if  applied  in  a  BAT-24  regimen. 27 In FACTS-001,  missing  doses  or  using  only  the  precoital dose could have reduced tenofovir diphosphate in tissue, even when detection of tenofovir in cervicovaginal lavage for adherence measurement remained unaltered.

In a prespecified subgroup analysis, participants younger  than  25  years,  who  represent  almost  threequarters  of  the  cohort,  had  a  small  but  significant protective effect from tenofovir gel. However, this protective  effect  was  balanced  by  an  increased  risk  of HIV infection in the tenofovir gel group in those older than 25 years. In the dapivirine ring trials, efficacy in the youngest age groups (&lt;21 years) was not observed. 28,29 The reasons for this interaction of age and treatment effect are unclear, and could be due to chance, or unaccounted factors  unrelated  to  tenofovir  gel.  Participants  in  these two age groups did vary with respect to several baseline behavioural  and  biological  characteristics,  which  could have  altered  their  risk  for  HIV  in  the  presence  of tenofovir gel. HSV-2 baseline seroprevalence in particular differed  between  these  two  groups,  with  women  aged 25  years  and  older  having  a  higher  seroprevalence  of HSV-2 (56%) than those younger than 25 years (35%). HSV-2 can cause immune activation in the genital tract and recruit activated CD4 cells to the site of infection. 30,31 Some researchers  have  observed  that  tenofovir  gel  can promote hyper-responsiveness to ex­ ternal inflammatory stimuli. 32 Tenofovir gel might have amplified the inflammatory  effects  of  HSV-2,  thus  diminishing  its antiviral protective effects; however, this hypothesis requires further testing. Other biological factors associated with differential mucosal susceptibility to HIV

are cervicovaginal in- flammation and the vaginal microbiome. 33,34 Both have been postulated to be modifiers of vaginal tenofovir concentrations, and might have influenced tenofovir gel efficacy despite inconsistent use  in  some  populations  of  women. 35 We  hypothesise that  tenofovir  gel  might  have  been  associated  with increased genital irritation, as evidenced  by higher incidence of intermenstrual bleeding and product-related genital  events,  than  in  women  in  the  placebo  group, although the number of events were small.

Promoting high adherence to biomedical HIV prevent­ ion  interventions,  and  having  the  ability  to  measure product  adherence,  are  challenges  in  HIV  prevention science and reflect the limitations of this trial. Although we  enrolled  a  representative  at-risk  population,  such populations often also have barriers to adherence. Adherence  assessed  by  product  returns  was  lower  in unmarried women, women who lived with their parents, and  women  who  had  more  than  primary  education. Being unmarried and living with parents might influence the  circumstances  under  which  women  have  sex,  and could  have  influenced  the  ability  of  women  to  use  a pericoital product consistently, particularly if sex occurred frequently.  Although  very  few  women  reported  using fewer than two gels as prescribed, insertion of the second dose could have been more difficult for women who did not live with their partners. 36 Notably, consistent gel use per sex act were also associated with lower HIV incidence, irrespective of study group, suggesting that participants with better adherence had a lower risk of acquiring HIV than  did  participants  who  were  less  likely  to  adhere. More generally, even if gel coverage before and after sex was high, we were not able to measure exposure to HIV at  the  time  of  gel  use,  a  limitation  common  to  HIV prevention  trials.  Pericoital  topical  products  might  be suitable for some women-eg, those who have infrequent sex  or  who  do  not  wish  to  use  systemic  products-but consistent use with every sex act remains a key challenge. However, women who can achieve consistent and high use might gain some protection, according to evidence from subcohort analyses in this study and other studies. 37

In  summary,  this  trial  did  not  provide  evidence  to support  the  licensure  of  tenofovir  1%  gel  for  pericoital prevention  of  male-to-female  sexual  HIV  transmission, because  we  were  unable  to  replicate  the  results  of  the proof-of-concept CAPRISA-004 study. 7 Instead, we showed the requirement for high and consistent use, the challenge in  achieving  this  consistency,  and  the  inability  of  this regimen to provide HIV protection in women if implemented inconsistently. Given the higher than expected HIV  incidence in this population, despite intensive HIV prevention counselling and support, there remains  a pressing need to evaluate new  products and alternate delivery systems that are safe and effective, and that reduce user dependence or else facilitate consistent  use, if  HIV  prevention  in  young  women  in sub-Saharan Africa is to be adequately addressed.
