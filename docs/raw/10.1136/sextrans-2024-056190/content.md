## Abstract

Objectives: Migration is associated with increased risk of HIV infection in Africa, but evidence about non-HIV sexually transmitted infection (STI) burden among African migrants is limited.

Methods: We used data from the Sexually Transmitted Infection Prevalence Study (STIPS), a cross-sectional population-based study of chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomonas, syphilis, and herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2) prevalence in southern Uganda, to compare STI prevalence between adults 18-49 with and without a recent history of migration. Migration status was determined using household census data, with a recent migration history defined as having moved into one's community of current residence within the last ~18 months. Unadjusted and adjusted modified Poisson regression models were used to compare individual STI prevalence risk by recent migration status with associations reported as adjusted prevalence risk ratios (adjPRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). Adjusted models included participants' sex, age, and community of residence.

Results: Among 1,825 participants, 358 (19.6%) had a recent migration history. Overall, migrants exhibited a significantly higher combined prevalence of curable STIs (gonorrhea, chlamydia, high-titer syphilis [rapid plasma reagin ≥ 1:8], and trichomonas) as compared to long-term residents (34.4% vs. 24.2%; adjPRR=1.23; 95%CI: 1.03-1.47). Significant differences in curable STI prevalence by migration status were concentrated among persons living with HIV (49.4% vs. 32.6%; adjPRR=1.42; 95%CI: 1.10-1.85) and among women (38.8% prevalence in migrants vs. 27.8% in long-term residents; adjPRR=1.26; 95%CI: 1.01-1.58. Conclusions: The prevalence of non-HIV STIs is higher among migrants. Tailored outreach and service delivery approaches that address the needs of mobile populations are crucial for integrated HIV and STI epidemic control in Uganda to optimize resources and reduce transmission risks.

## INTRODUCTION

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) are a major public health concern associated with adverse reproductive health outcomes, neonatal morbidity and mortality, and decreased quality of life [1,2]. They place direct and indirect economic burdens on households and health systems [2]. Like HIV, age-standardized incidence of combined syphilis, chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomonas, and genital herpes infections in southern and eastern Africa remain the highest worldwide at an estimated 17,000 to 20,000 infections per 100,000 person-years [1]. Barriers to reducing the non-HIV STI burden include inaccessibility of testing and treatment [3], low rates of health-seeking for symptoms [4,5], and fragmented healthcare services [3]. While there is extensive research about drivers of HIV risk in subSaharan Africa, including migration [6-8], factors underpinning STI transmission remain understudied.

Data from the Rakai Community Cohort Study (RCCS) has previously shown higher HIV incidence among recent migrants relative to long-term residents in Ugandan communities [9]. Given the high rates of HIV-STI coinfections [10,11], migration status may similarly influence the prevalence of non-HIV STIs in Africa [12-14]. Migrants report increased engagement in risky sexual behaviors [6,15], and their vulnerability may be compounded by disrupted sexual networks [8,9] and reduced access to or engagement with health services [7]. Additionally, people living with HIV relocate at higher rates than their peers, maybe to seek better treatment access or avoid stigma [8,16]. Evidence about migration status and non-HIV STI risk in Africa is limited, and it is unclear how curable STI transmission dynamics may differ from those of HIV.

## METHODS

## Sexually Transmitted Infection Prevalence Study and STI testing

This study was a secondary analysis of the Sexually Transmitted Infection Prevalence Study (STIPS) in Uganda [17]. STIPS was a cross-sectional study of chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomonas, syphilis, and HSV-2 prevalence in southern Uganda between May 2019 and October 2019 [17]. Nested within the population-based Rakai Community Cohort Study (RCCS), STIPS enrolled consenting participants aged 18-49 years from two communities: a semi-urban trading center and surrounding rural villages, herein referred to as 'inland community;' and a Lake Victoria fishing community known to have a highly migratory population [7,18]. These communities were selected based on their representativeness, geographical diversity, population size, and enrollment timelines within the RCCS survey.

The parent RCCS study is an open population-based census and HIV cohort study conducted by the Rakai Health Sciences Program in southern Uganda. The RCCS is a sociodemographic, behavioral, and health information survey which includes voluntary HIV testing and counseling.

STIPS was approved by the Uganda Virus Research Institute Research Ethics Committee (GC/127/19/03/709) and the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB00204691) and was also registered with the Ugandan National Council for Science and Technology (HS 364 ES). Participants provided additional written informed consent specifically for STIPS.

Primary study outcomes in STIPS included prevalence of chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomonas, syphilis, high-titer syphilis, HSV-2, and HIV. Genital swabs were collected from consenting men and women for chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomonas testing. HIV, HSV-2 and syphilis antibody testing were conducted using serum samples from peripheral blood. HIV testing employed a validated three-test rapid testing algorithm with ELISA and PCR confirmation for first-time diagnoses. HSV-2 testing utilized the Kalon ELISA with an optical density cut-off of 1.5 (Kalon Biological, Guilford UK). Syphilis antibody status was determined using the SD Bioline Syphilis 3.0 (SD Biostandard Diagnostics Private, Gurgaon, India) immunochromatographic assay and syphilis titers were determined using the rapid plasma regain (RPR) test (Cypress Diagnostics, Hulshout, Belgium) for all participants with positive antibody results. High-titer syphilis was defined as RPR titer ≥ 1:8. Penile meatal and vaginal swabs were tested for chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomonas using the Abbott m2000 RealTime CT/NG assay (Abbott Molecular) and the OSOM Trichomonas Rapid Test (Sekisui [formerly Genzyme Diagnostics], Burlington, MA, USA), respectively.

Positive HIV, syphilis, and trichomonas rapid-test results were immediately disclosed to participants by on-site counselors and free treatment was offered according to Uganda Clinical Guidelines [19]. Participants with positive chlamydia or gonorrhea tests were recontacted after the survey and similarly provided counseling and free treatment. Participants who self-reported genital ulcers or vaginal or penile discharge but were not diagnosed with an STI were also offered treatment based on World Health Organization (WHO) syndromic management guidelines [20].

## Statistical analysis

We defined STIPS participants living in the same community as during the prior RCCS census round approximately eighteen months ago as 'long-term residents.' We defined participants who moved into the community since the prior census round as 'migrants.' We compared participants' demographics, sexual behaviors, and HIV service utilization by migration status and reported frequency counts and percentages, using Pearson's chisquare tests to assess statistical significance. Prevalence of each STI was estimated among migrants and long-term residents. Univariable and multivariable modified Poisson regression models [21] with robust standard errors were used to estimate prevalence risk ratios (PRRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95%CI), comparing migrants to long-term residents. In multivariable models, we adjusted for sex, age by years, community type (i.e., inland or fishing), education, occupation, and marital status based on prior work indicating these variables have a relationship with migration and HIV serostatus [7,9]. We did not adjust for sexual behaviors because (1) they likely act as mediators and confounders, and (2) we were primarily interested in identifying whether individuals with a recent migration history are an important demographic sub-group to prioritize for STI services (after adjusting for other demographic confounders). In subgroup analyses, we stratified by participants' sex and HIV status for each STI. In a post-hoc analysis, we stratified by age group for high-titer syphilis based on the observed high rates of syphilis among older males observed during analysis in the parent STIPS study. Statistical analysis of this study was carried out by Stata 15.1 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX). All p-values were two-sided, and a p-value of &lt;0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## RESULTS

## Study population

A total of 1,467 long-term residents and 358 migrants (19.6%) in the inland and fishing communities consented to participate in STIPS. Within these study communities, 73% of census-eligible individuals participated in RCCS, and 97% of those RCCS participants consented to STIPS. Participation was significantly lower among men than women (64.1% versus 77.7%, P&lt;0.0001) and among younger individuals (64.4% among ages 15-29 versus 76.3% among ages 30-49, P&lt;0.0001). Table 1 summarizes the study population's demographics, sexual behaviors, and HIV service utilization. Relative to long-term residents, surveyed migrants were significantly more likely to be younger (P&lt;0.001) and have a higher education level (P&lt;0.001). Migrants more frequently worked in the fishing sector (20.7% migrants versus 14.7% long-term residents; P&lt;0.001), while long-term residents more commonly worked in agriculture (30.4% long-term residents versus 19.6% migrants;

P&lt;0.001). Migrants also reported a greater number of sexual partners in the past year (39.9% migrants report more than one partner versus 30.5% long-term residents; P&lt;0.001).

## Migration status and STI prevalence

Compared to long-term residents, migrants had a significantly higher prevalence of most STIs (Table 2). For example, high-titer syphilis prevalence was 8.7% among migrants and 4.6% among long-term residents (unadjusted PRR=1.90; 95%CI: 01.26-2.86; adjusted PRR=1.68; 95%CI: 1.10-2.55). Overall, the prevalence of curable STIs - gonorrhea, chlamydia, high-titer syphilis, and trichomonas - was 34.4% among migrants compared to 24.2% among long-term residents (unadjusted PRR=1.42; 95%CI: 1.20-1.68; adjusted PRR=1.23; 95%CI: 1.03-1.47). HSV-2 infection was the only non-HIV STI with lower prevalence among migrants (47.6%) compared to long-term residents (55.1%), but this difference was not significant in the adjusted model (adjusted PRR=0.95; 95%CI: 0.851.06). HIV prevalence was also lower among migrants, but this difference was not statistically significant.

## Influence of gender and HIV status

Female migrants and long-term residents exhibited a higher prevalence of most STIs compared to their male counterparts (Supplemental Table). Controlling for age, the prevalence of curable STIs among female migrants was 1.38 times the prevalence among male migrants (38.8% vs 28.3%; 95%CI: 1.01-1.89; P=0.04). In a notable exception to that gender difference, the prevalence of high-titer syphilis among male migrants was especially elevated at 11.2% relative to 6.8% in female migrants (female to male migrants PRR=0.72; 95%CI: 0.37-1.41; P=0.34).

Disaggregation by HIV serostatus showed migrants and long-term residents living with HIV had higher rates of most STIs relative to individuals without HIV (Table 3). The prevalence of curable STIs was substantially higher among migrants living with HIV (49.4%) compared to migrants without HIV (29.7%). Similarly, migrants living with HIV had elevated rates of high-titer syphilis (20.0%) compared to those without HIV (5.2%).

## High-titer syphilis and age

In stratified analysis of high-titer syphilis disaggregated by age groups, we observed a significantly higher burden of syphilis in older age groups (Figure 1). The prevalence of high-titer syphilis ranged from 3.6% to 6.7% across different age groups of longterm residents and from 4.2% to 16.3% among migrants. Migrants aged 40 and above had a substantially higher prevalence of active syphilis compared to their long-term resident counterparts (16.3% vs. 3.6%; unadjusted PRR=4.48; 95%CI: 1.89-10.63; adjusted PRR=3.48; 95%CI: 1.52-7.95).

## DISCUSSION

Our analysis of cross-sectional population-based STI prevalence data from southern Uganda shows that migrants have a significantly higher combined burden of curable STIs (gonorrhea, chlamydia, syphilis, and trichomonas) compared to long-term residents. STI

prevalence was particularly elevated among female migrants and migrants living with HIV, highlighting the need for targeted interventions. Moreover, our analysis revealed especially elevated rates of high-titer syphilis among male migrants and older migrants, indicating an urgent need for syphilis control efforts in these groups. The clear association between STI prevalence and migration status can better inform targeting of high-risk subpopulations for integrated HIV and STI prevention, screening, and treatment interventions.

Our findings contribute to the limited but growing body of evidence that non-HIV STI prevalence is higher among migrants compared to non-migrants in sub-Saharan Africa [1214]. Zuma et al. reported South African male circular labor migrants had a 33.9% prevalence (95%CI: 27.3-40.9%) of at least one STI (active syphilis, gonorrhea, chlamydia, or genital sores with genital discharge) compared to a 20.2% prevalence (95%CI: 13.7-27.9) among male non-migrants [14]. These prevalence levels closely align with the prevalence of curable STIs among male migrants and male long-term residents in our analysis (28.3% and 20.3%, respectively), though the circular migrants in the South African study were potentially more mobile than the migrants in our analysis. In a study of Tanzanian agricultural plantation residents, Norris et al. (2013) reported male migrants had higher prevalence of any STI (HIV, syphilis, or HSV-2) compared to male non-migrants (PR=1.82; 95%CI: 1.32-2.50). Unlike our analysis, the study did not find an association between migration status and STIs among females [12]. In a systematic review on female sex workers globally, Platt et al. reported that migrant female sex workers in five countries had higher prevalence of acute STIs compared to non-migrants; however, this difference was not consistent across studies [13]. The present analysis thus expands on our understanding of the higher levels of STIs among migrants in Africa on a population-level, particularly emphasizing the heightened prevalence among female migrants and migrants living with HIV.

Women in our study communities were disproportionately affected by STIs, possibly reflecting heightened physiological and societal vulnerability or higher likelihood of asymptomatic and thus untreated infection relative to men [1,11]. Women in eastern Africa often relocate due to marriage or childbirth [7,9,16], underscoring the importance of linking women to healthcare services at these key milestones to interrupt downstream effects of STIs on infants. Notably, high-titer syphilis was an exception to this gender pattern, with women in our analysis exhibiting lower prevalence than men. Given the serious health consequences of congenital syphilis and increased risk of intrauterine HIV transmission in mothers with syphilis and HIV co-infection, uptake and coverage of antenatal syphilis testing in Uganda has increased in the past decade which may explain this gender difference [22,23].

The prevalence of high-titer syphilis among male migrants was 11.2% in the study communities, more than five times the estimated 2.0% prevalence of active syphilis among Ugandan men countrywide [24]. Our finding aligns closely with Norris et al., which reported 12% of Tanzanian male migrant agricultural plantation residents had active or recent syphilis infections [12]. Despite Uganda's Ministry of Health supporting a partner notification program - wherein pregnant women testing positive for syphilis receive notification slips to encourage partner testing - only a minority of partners pursue follow-up. The 'Syphilis Treatment of Partners (STOP) Trial' in Kampala found only 18% of notified male partners attended a clinic for follow-up [25]. An earlier study in Entebbe reported that while 82% of pregnant women accepted syphilis testing, only 1% of male partners accepted testing [26]. Low uptake of male partner follow-up as well as the concentration of infection in mobile, hard-to-reach men shown in our analysis may contribute to the lack of syphilis control in the study communities. Syphilis screening and treatment initiatives in Uganda should be broadened to encompass male migrants as a key, high-risk population.

We previously reported on the higher STI burden among people living with HIV in this population [17], underscoring the need to integrate STI prevention, testing, and treatment into existing HIV services [11,27]. Innovative strategies already employed by HIV service providers to reach and retain migrants - such as linking health records across locations and utilizing mobile phone (mHealth) technologies - could be adapted to encompass nonHIV STI services [8]. An integrated approach also provides a platform for addressing the syndemic relationship between HIV and STIs whereby reducing non-HIV STI burden can lower HIV transmission risk [11,28].

The high STI burden among migrants presents unique challenges for epidemic control. Community-level measures, like periodic presumptive mass treatment events which distribute one-time antibiotics irrespective of symptoms [2], would need to be implemented frequently to continuously capture new migrants. Additional concerns about the potential for mass treatment events to increase antimicrobial resistance further complicate this approach [29]. More tailored programs might include encouraging community health workers to identify new migrants at key locations such as fishing shorelines and sex worker venues to distribute condoms or link people to health services [8]. Technological developments in STI home-tests or self-tests can empower individuals to take control of their sexual health, overcome some concerns about STI stigma and confidentiality, and facilitate cost-effective implementation of such outreach programs [30].

Because our study was cross-sectional, a key limitation in our analysis is the inability to distinguish between STIs acquired prior to versus after migration. Evidence from subSaharan Africa has found people living with HIV relocate at higher rates than their peers, maybe to seek better treatment access, avoid stigma, or move after a marriage dissolution [8,16]. Additionally, previous work in Rakai showed the first two years after migration are associated with increased HIV acquisition risk [9]. This heightened risk after migration may be due to disruptions in sexual networks, disconnections from preventive healthcare, and higher levels of sexual risk behaviors [8,9,15]. A systematic review reported male and female migrants in South Africa engaged in more risky sexual behaviors compared to non-migrant counterparts, including multiple partnering, lower condom usage, and higher participation in sex work [6]. Lake Victoria fishing communities such as the one included in the present analysis have many male migrant fishermen with disposable income that they spend at sex work venues [31]. If other STIs follow these same transmission patterns, then highly mobile people need to be engaged in care both immediately after and within the first few years of migrating. Further research about causal mechanisms between migration and STI prevalence is crucial for understanding transmission dynamics and the role of imported infections.

The STIPS study had several additional limitations [17]. Our findings were derived from two communities in southern Uganda with high HIV burden, which may limit the generalizability to settings with lower HIV burden and different STI risk factors. We did not observe a statistically significant relationship between migration and HIV status (neither overall nor disaggregated by gender) unlike larger studies in this population [7,9], which may reflect our comparatively smaller sample size in the present analysis. Our analysis did not account for migration distance or short-term mobility such as temporary relocations for work or school. There was selective participation in the STIPS study, with men and younger individuals participating at lower rates, which potentially introduced bias in the estimates [17]. We did not make any statistical adjustments to account for the number of sub-analyses. Measurement error may have been introduced by swab contamination, particularly for women who self-collected their own samples, and by the OSOM rapid test used for detecting trichomonas which was not validated for male penile meatal swabs. Lastly, we were unable to differentiate between active, latent, and resolved syphilis infections among those with positive serology. This information would be necessary to understand the clinical implications of syphilis prevalence in the population.

In conclusion, our population-based study establishes a higher STI burden among migrants in two Ugandan communities characterized by high HIV burden. This data augments the STI and migration literature by highlighting the vulnerability of female migrants and migrants living with HIV. Of particular concern is the notably elevated high-titer syphilis prevalence among male migrants and older migrants in our study communities. These findings underscore the urgency and importance of integrating HIV and STI care in subSaharan Africa and tailoring these services to a mobile population.

## KEY MESSAGES

## What is already known on this topic:

There is a robust evidence base demonstrating a higher HIV prevalence among migrant populations compared to non-migrants in Africa. Prior research suggests people living with HIV are more likely to migrate, and migrants also exhibit increased risky sexual behaviors following relocation. However, there is limited research on non-HIV STI prevalence among this same population.

## What this study adds:

Our population-based study shows migrants in southern Uganda exhibited a significantly higher combined prevalence of curable STIs (gonorrhea, chlamydia, syphilis, and trichomonas) compared to long-term residents (34.4% versus 24.2%), interestingly in the absence of a significant difference in HIV prevalence. STI prevalence was particularly high among female migrants; because women often relocate due to marriage or childbirth, this finding underscores the importance of linking women to healthcare services at these key milestones to interrupt downstream effects of STIs on infants. We observed a notably high prevalence of high-titer syphilis among male migrants (11.2%), which suggests that a low uptake of male partner follow-up testing and the concentration of infection in mobile, hard-to-reach men may contribute to the lack of syphilis control in the region.

## How this study might affect research, practice, or policy:

The high STI burden among migrants in our study demonstrates a clear case for developing programs and policies that integrate HIV and STI prevention and care for mobile populations. Further research into the causal mechanisms between migration and STI transmission can further refine the development and implementation of tailored integrated HIV and STI interventions.
