## ABSTRACT

Objective Venues where people socialize, such as bars, play a significant role in HIV transmission risk. However, little research has been done that identifies characteristics of high-­ risk venues, to inform HIV prevention efforts. This study identified clusters of social venues in Uganda and their relationship with HIV vulnerability of young venue workers (15-24 years). We also assessed availability of HIV prevention services at the venues.

Design and setting This was a secondary analysis of data from a larger cross-­ sectional survey of social venues in 25 districts of Uganda.

Participants A total of 480 venues that employed young workers were included in this study.

Analysis Hierarchical cluster and mixed-­ effects regression analyses were conducted.

Results Four clusters were adopted: cluster 1 represented 127 venues (mainly alcohol-­ serving sex work venues with a provision of on-­ site sex); cluster 2 represented 80 venues (mainly alcohol-­ serving sex work venues without a provision of on-­ site sex); cluster 3 represented 113 venues (mainly alcohol-­ serving venues without sex work or on-­ site sex); and cluster 4 represented 160 venues (non-­ alcohol-­ serving venues). We found a positive correlation between venue clusters and HIV vulnerability. Workers who belonged to cluster 1 (alcohol-­ serving sex work venues with a provision of on-­ site sex) had a 0.15 increase in HIV vulnerability compared with those in alcohol-­ serving venues without sex work or a provision of on-­ site sex (cluster 3) (coefficient: 0.15, 95% CI: 0.030 to 0.287) after adjusting for age, sex and marital status. Thirty-­ seven per cent of cluster 1 venues reported any HIV prevention services in the past 12 months.

Conclusion Alcohol-­ serving sex work venues with a provision of on-­ site sex (cluster 1) appeared to reinforce vulnerability to HIV in this study and should be targeted with HIV prevention interventions. Such venues can amplify HIV transmission by facilitating sexual partner coupling and on-­ site sex.

These venues are common hotspots for populations at a higher risk of HIV exposure. 4 An increasing  body  of  literature  suggests  that venues for social interactions influence HIV transmission or acquisition in two ways. First, these venues may influence the probability of meeting partners with certain risk characteristics. 5 Second, the situational conditions found at different venues for social interaction may influence  the  social acceptability of risky behaviours such as excess alcohol use, having sex  with  a  new  or  casual  partner,  condom use and anal sex, hence increasing HIV or sexually transmitted  infection  (STI)  transmission. 1  6  7 A  number  of studies across different countries have found sexual risk-­ taking among workers and customers at venues for social interaction. 8-11 No wonder, venues for social interactions have been highlighted as potential 'hotspots' for HIV or STI intervention as they have been shown to be highly associated with the incidence of HIV or STIs and related risky behaviours. 8 10 12

Recent  studies,  including  a  study  in  Uganda,  found that HIV prevalence was highest among workers in social venues, especially the female workers (11%), 8 13 compared with  the  general  adult  population  of  Uganda  (6.2%). 14 For several reasons, workers in venues for social interaction are believed to have an elevated risk of HIV infection. First, these venues employ mainly young people. 15 16 These  young  workers  are  at  a  stage  transitioning  from childhood to adulthood, a time for exploring and navigating  peer  relationships,  gender  norms,  sexuality  and economic responsibility yet with less support from families and communities. 17 This transition to working and living independently can lead to early sexual onset. 18 Research indicates  that  young  people  who  begin  sexual  activity early  are  at  great  risk  of  engaging  in  risky  behaviours (having multiple partners, engaging in unprotected and intergenerational sexual activity, and experimenting sex with alcohol and other drugs), thereby increasing their chances  of  contracting  STIs  including  HIV/AIDS. 19-21 Second, the social context of the work setting of socialising  venues  puts  workers  at  increased  risk  of  exposure to  various  risky  behaviours  such  as  alcohol  consumption and sexual activities including sex work. 8 22 23 This, coupled with the poorly paid jobs, may in turn motivate young people who work in venues for social interaction to engage in selling sex to their customers or embark in age-­ disparate  and  transactional  relationships. 24  25 This highlights this subgroup as a bridge population for HIV transmission, since their customers, in turn, transmit HIV to their sexual partners, and finally, HIV rates may escalate in the general population.

Previous  research,  both  qualitative  and  quantitative, has given considerable attention to venues where people meet sexual partners. Studies, including some in Africa, have mapped and characterised these venues, 4 26-29 while others have examined sexual behaviours and HIV seroprevalence of workers and customers at venues for social interaction. 2 6 8 11 12 Relatedly, other studies have assessed availability of HIV preventive interventions at venues for social  interaction  and  provided  a  case  for  support  for venue-­ based HIV programmes. 2 30-32 However, no previous studies  identified  clusters  of  high-­ risk  venues  despite evidence showing varying prevalence of risky behaviours and availability of HIV prevention programmes in these venues. 33 Notably, certain aspects of the physical environment of these venues attract different types of customers and workers. This is so because the working environment in these venues is multifaceted and serves multiple functions,  including  being  a  place  for  recreation,  fostering socially  sanctioned  drinking,  facilitating  social  relationships and providing opportunities to meet sex partners. Moreover,  physical  characteristics  or  features  of  these venues  such  as  dim  lighting,  dark  corners  and  unisex bathrooms may be conducive for sex on-­ site. 1 12 34 Therefore, clustering venues for social interaction by their characteristics  or  features  and  linking  those  clusters  to  HIV vulnerability of workers may be a worthwhile endeavour for generating insights for HIV risk reduction interventions.  A  better  understanding  of  venue  clusters  could provide  useful  information  for  allocation  of  HIV/AIDS resources  to  high-­ risk  venues  to  reduce  new  infections. This study aimed to identify clusters of social venues in Uganda by their characteristics, and to examine the relationship  between  venue  clusters  and  HIV  vulnerability among the young people 15-24 working in such venues. We also assessed availability of HIV prevention services at the venues.

## METHODS Design

This  secondary  data  analysis  was  done  using  data  from a larger population-­ based survey that used Priorities for Local  AIDS Control Efforts (PLACE) methodology 35 to identify, map and characterise social venues in 25 districts in  Uganda.  The  survey  also  included  interviewing  and testing  (for  HIV)  individuals  who  were  socialising  or working at the venues during a busy time. The primary study  was  conducted  in  2018  and  it  employed  a  cross-­ sectional study design. The present study aimed to: (1) identify clusters of social venues in Uganda, (2) describe their  relationship  with  HIV  vulnerability  among  the young people (15-24 years) working in social venues and (3) assess availability of HIV prevention services at social venues.

## Overview of the primary study (PLACE 2018)

The  primary  study  was  conducted  in  25  districts  of Uganda, namely Mbale, Busia, Tororo, Namayingo and Mayuge  in  Eastern  Uganda;  Mukono,  and  Luwero  in Central  Uganda; Omoro, Agago, Kole, Apac and Moyo in Northern Uganda; Bunyangabu, Kakumiro, Kyegegwa, and Kyenjojo in Western Uganda; Bukomasimbi, Sembabule, Lwengo, and Kiryandongo in South Western Uganda;  Rakia,  Isingiro,  Kiruhura,  and  Ntungamo  in Southern Uganda; and Kotido in North Eastern Uganda. The  study  districts  were  selected  purposively  based  on their quarterly HIV/AIDS  programme  performance regarding  the  attainment  of  the  Joint  United  Nations Programme on HIV and AIDS (UNAIDS) 90,90,90 target. Performance for the quarter that preceded the study was the  one  considered.  The  survey  employed  the  PLACE methodology,  which  consists  of  five  steps  within  each district.  District  launch  meetings  in  which  district  HIV leaders,  stakeholders  and  implementing  partners  identify  subdistrict  areas  dubbed  'priority  prevention  areas' (PPAs) where contextual factors, including presence of fishing villages, transportation hubs, border crossings or trading  centres,  provide  evidence  of  contextual  factors likely  to  be  associated  with  elevated  HIV  risk.  In  each district, 8-12 PPAs were identified and described.

1.  Within each PPA in each district, systematic interviews with community informants (400) were conducted to generate a comprehensive list of public venues where people go to meet new sexual partners.
2.  Venues meeting specific criteria (sex work on-­ site, sex occurring  on-­ site,  locations  known  as  places  where men  who  have  sex  with  men  or  people  who  inject drugs socialise, and venues identified by more than 20 community informants) were defined as high priority; others were lower priority. A stratified random probability sample of 100 venues was selected for a venue visit to map and profile the venues, with oversampling of higher priority venues. A semistructured questionnaire was used to collect data from a venue informant, and it included questions about type, activities on-­ site, staff characteristics,  presence  of  key  populations  of  HIV and HIV prevention activities on-­ site, among others.
3.  Venue-­ based  face-­ to-­ face  interviews  and  HIV  testing were conducted in each district among approximately 600 people aged 15 years and older randomly recruited from a second probability sample of venues 30 selected from among the venues that were visited and found operational. All venue workers were interviewed and tested as well as a random sample of male and female venue patrons with the number of interviews per venue proportional to the size of the venue. A semistructured questionnaire was used to collect data and it included questions about sociodemographic information, sexual behaviour, health-­ seeking behaviour, and exposure to HIV prevention, care and treatment programmes, including antiretroviral therapy.
4.  District profile reports including two HIV prevention cascades were prepared for each district and used to facilitate HIV prevention planning.

## Sample, recruitment and data collection

In this study, data from 480 socialising venues and 1007 young venue workers (15-24 years) were drawn from the primary PLACE 2018 dataset and analysed. The inclusion criteria for this secondary analysis included data from only venues  that  employed  young  people  aged  15-24  years. In addition, participants with a positive HIV status were excluded from the analysis because the focus of this study was on vulnerability to primary HIV infections. According to WHO/UNAIDS, HIV risk is defined as the probability that a person may become infected, reinfected or superinfected with HIV due to their individual behaviours; and HIV vulnerability results from a range of factors outside the  control  of  individuals  that  reduce  their  ability  to avoid the risk (WHO/UNAIDS, 2008). In this paper, HIV vulnerability applies to individuals who are HIV negative. Subsequently, only 1007 out of 1071 young venue workers (15-24 years) in the primary survey (PLACE 2018) were included in this study.

## Variables

## Dependent variable

The dependent variable for this study was 'HIV vulnerability' and was defined using self-­ reported individual social demographic  and  risky  sexual  behaviours/characteristics.  Vulnerability  to  HIV  is  multifaceted  and  therefore shaped by a range of proximal biological and behavioural factors, as well as more distal social and structural factors, for example, gender inequality and poverty. 35 Other HIV vulnerability  indicators  mentioned  in  literature  include STIs, 36 37 and behavioural factors like engaging in transactional  sex,  having  multiple  partnerships,  engaging  in substance abuse and limited condom use. 38

Based  on  earlier  validated  HIV  vulnerability  indices such as the girls' HIV vulnerability index, 39 40 this  study selected a set of five indicators from the primary dataset and  created  a  composite  variable.  These  five  were  the only known indicators of HIV vulnerability in the primary dataset. The selected indicators included condom use in the past 6 months, number of sexual partners in the past 12 months, history of ulcerative STI symptoms in the past 12 months, education background and alcohol consumption in the past 4 weeks (table 1).

First,  the  selected  variables  were  transformed  into binary  variables  with  1  representing  high-­ risk  category and 0 low risk. Then, HIV vulnerability was determined by  generating  a  summary  score  from  0  to  1  through calculating the proportion of responses of increased risk among individual sexual activity/characteristic variables. The summary score was later classified into three categories:  1-low  ( ≤ 0.3);  2-moderate  (&gt;0.3  and ≤ 0.6);  and 3-high (&gt;0.6).

## Independent variables Venue clusters

We conducted a cluster analysis with 18 venue characteristics (variables) to obtain the venue clusters in this study. The 18 venue characteristics included those relating to type of activities at the venue and type of populations at the  venue  (table  2).  These  characteristics  were  chosen because of their importance in describing venue features. The clusters were constructed to reduce the number of venue-­ level characteristics into categories that represent the predominant groups that exist within. Cluster analysis forms groupings in such a way that units within a group (or cluster) have a higher measure of similarity than units in any other cluster. 41 42

The agglomerative hierarchical clustering method was used  to  explore  and  determine  the  number  of  venue clusters in this study. The hierarchical clustering method builds clusters based on hierarchy. We used a 'bottom-­ up' algorithm  (agglomerative  clustering  approach),  which treats  the  observations  separately  as  singleton  clusters, and then computes the distance of each pair and merges the most similar clusters until the final optimal clusters are  formed.  The  Ward's  method  was  used  to  combine clusters in the agglomerative clustering approach. Unlike the other methods of combining clusters in agglomerative approach, the Ward's method analyses the variance of  clusters  instead  of  measuring  the  distance  directly. We assessed the number of the clusters to be selected via dendrogram and scatterplot. The final number of clusters adopted was determined by the relationship observed between branches of connection in the data. The final cluster  solution  consisted  of  four  venue  clusters.  We added the identified clusters as separate variables to the dataset, representing  different venue  categorisations. The  four  clusters  were  each  given  a  meaningful  name based on those attributes with the highest mean values in the respective clusters.

To validate the cluster solution, the dataset was divided into two samples and a cluster solution ran on part of the dataset and repeated on another. Both analyses revealed a similar clustering.

Other  independent  variables  for  this  study  included: (1)  workers'  characteristics  and  (2)  HIV  prevention services  on-­ site  (HIV/AIDS/condom  posters,  condoms visible or for sale at the venue).

Furthermore, sociodemographic characteristics of young workers at the venues including age measured in complete years, sex and marital status were also among the independent variables.

## Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA V.15 (College Station, Texas, USA). Venue and individual-­ level data  were  weighted  based  on  the  multistage  sampling design and probability of selection in the primary study. Descriptive and  inferential statistics of  the  sampled venues and workers were computed using survey weight estimates.

## Descriptive analysis

Descriptive statistics  including  frequency  distribution and proportions were used to describe the characteristics of workers stratified by venue clusters and availability of HIV prevention services. Χ 2 test was used to examine the difference between categorical variables including venue characteristics and availability of HIV prevention services, sociodemographic characteristics and venue clusters.

## Regression analysis

To investigate the relationship between venue clusters and HIV  vulnerability,  a  multilevel  mixed-­ effects  regression model  was  used  with  random  effects  for  venue  cluster. The multilevel regression analysis approach was chosen because of the data's nested nature; venue workers are nested in the venues and the venues are nested in venue clusters.  Second,  our  dependent  variable,  HIV  vulnerability, is measured at a lower unit (venue workers), which also  calls  for  multilevel  modelling.  We  used  a  mixed-­ effects model (including a cluster variable as a fixed variable and random intercept) because it allowed to estimate the possible effect of the cluster variable on HIV vulnerability as well as adjust for variability or decomposition of the variance into two components, that is, variance due to  high-­ level  units  (venues)  and  lower  units  (workers). Model  specification  tests  were  conducted  comparing the  multilevel  mixed-­ effects  models  with  the  conventional  linear  regression  models.  Log-­ likelihood,  Akaike Information Criteria and Bayesian Information Criteria were used to select the best models. This association was adjusted  for  sociodemographic  variables  including  age, sex and marital status (known factors that influence HIV vulnerability). 40 Statistical significance was p&lt;0.05.

## Patient and public involvement

In this study, data were drawn from the primary (PLACE 2018) dataset and analysed. There was no direct patient and public involvement done. However, the primary study used local research teams to collect data and conducted district-­ level stakeholder engagements for research uptake.  Several  HIV/AIDS  stakeholders  both  at  subnational  and  national  level  were  engaged  in  the  primary study.

## RESULTS Description of social venues

Table 3 shows a general description of social venues by their features and availability of HIV prevention services at the venue in the past 12 months. A total of 480 socialising venues that had young workers below 25 years were included  in  this  study;  however,  for  the  description  of venues by availability of HIV prevention services, only 447 were analysed. The 33 venues excluded had no response on availability of HIV prevention in the past 12 months. Overall, 78.9% of the total (480) venues were entertainment establishments such as bars, disco halls, video halls, etc. The most common type of venues was alcohol establishments.  The  most  common  types  of  activities  at  the venues were buying and eating food, alcohol consumption, and music and dancing. Thirty-­ six per cent of the venues reported presence of key populations, that is, sex workers, transgender women and men who inject drugs, while 33.5% reported having female staff who meet sex partners on-­ site. Majority (54.3%) of the venues reported not having had any HIV prevention services in the past 12 months. The prevention services in question included HIV posters, condom distribution, HIV counselling and testing outreach programmes and HIV prevention education on-­ site.

## Cluster characteristics

A total of 480 venues were included in the cluster analysis. The analysis grouped venues into homogeneous clusters using selected venue characteristics. The analysis resulted in a four-­ cluster solution. Table 4 shows the proportion of venues with a certain characteristic within a given cluster.

About 24% (127 of 480) of the social venues were categorised  into  cluster  1  (alcohol-­ serving  sex  work  venues with a provision of on-­ site sex). These alcohol-­ serving sex work venues with a provision of on-­ site sex had multiple activities, in addition to having sex brokers. Workers and customers of alcohol sex work venues with a provision of on-­ site sex reported to find sexual partners on-­ site. About 16.6% (80) of the venues were categorised into cluster 2  (mainly  alcohol-­ serving  sex  work  venues  without  a provision of on-­ site sex). One hundred thirteen venues (23.5%) were categorised into cluster 3 (alcohol-­ serving venues without sex work and no provision of on-­ site sex)

and 33.3% (160) were categorised into cluster 4 (mainly non-­ alcohol-­ serving  social  venues).  Drinking  and  soliciting  sexual  partners  were  common  in  alcohol-­ serving venues (clusters 1, 2 and 3).

Regarding availability of HIV prevention services at the venue, only 37.6% of the alcohol-­ serving sex work venues with a provision of on-­ site sex (cluster 1) reported having had any HIV prevention services in the past 12 months. In addition, fewer venues, about 20% in clusters 2, 3 and 4, respectively, reported having had any HIV prevention services in the past 12 months.

## Sociodemographic characteristics of workers (aged 15-24 years) within the venue clusters

Table 5 shows sociodemographic and behavioural characteristics of young workers (15-24 years) within the venue clusters. A total of 1007 young people working in socialising  venues  were  included  in  this  study:  69.2%  (697) were females and 30.7% (310) were males. These young workers were nested in the 480 venues included in this study.

Alcohol-­ serving sex work venues with a provision of sex on-­ site (cluster 1) constituted 27% (n=273) of all young workers,  with  the  majority  (over  70%)  aged  between 20  and  24  years  old.  There  were  relatively  more  young female workers in such venues compared with the others (76.6%).  About  72%  of  the  young  workers  in  alcohol-­ serving sex work venues with a provision of on-­ site sex had no or some primary education, and over 80% were not married.  Alcohol-­ serving  sex  work  venues  with  a  provision  of  on-­ site  sex  also  had  a  notable  percentage  (6.6) of young workers with no education compared with the venues in other clusters.

Alcohol-­ serving  sex  work  venues  without  a  provision of  on-­ site  sex  (cluster  2)  had  17.1%  (n=173)  of  young workers, with the majority (over 70%) aged between 20 and 24 years old. Venues in this cluster had more young female  workers  than  male  workers  (71.1%  vs  28.9%); 71.6% had no or some education, and 68.2% of the young workers were not married.

Alcohol-­ serving venues without sex work or on-­ site sex (cluster 3) had 24.3% (n=245) of young workers, with the majority (over 60%) aged between 20 and 24 years old. This  cluster  had  more  young  female  workers  (73.1%); 70.6%  of  its  workers  had  no  or  some  education,  while over 70% of the young workers in this cluster were not married.

Non-­ alcohol-­ serving  venues  (cluster  4)  had  31.3% (n=316) of young workers, with the majority (over 70%) aged between 20 and 24 years old. This cluster had relatively more young male workers compared with the other clusters (41.1%). About 72% of all young workers in this cluster had either no or some education, and over 80% were not married.

## Effects of venue clusters on vulnerability to HIV

Background  characteristics  of  venue  workers  by  HIV vulnerability can be found in online supplemental table A1. The 1007 young venue workers (15-24) included in the  analysis  were  nested  within  480  socialising  venues. Overall, the burden of HIV vulnerability was very high at 92%, with 43.3% of the young venue workers found to have high HIV vulnerability, and 48.4% moderate vulnerability. For insights on HIV positivity rates within venue clusters, see online supplemental figure A1. An overview of the results of the multilevel linear mixed-­ effects regression  analysis  of  the  association  between  venue  clusters and HIV vulnerability of young venue workers is provided in table 6. A positive and significant association between venue clusters and HIV vulnerability was found at both univariate and multivariate analyses. Age, sex and marital status  were  not  statistically  important  at  both  levels  of analysis.  The  p  values  show  that  workers  who  belonged in cluster 1 venues (alcohol-­ serving sex work venues with a  provision  of  on-­ site  sex)  had  a  0.15  increase  in  HIV vulnerability  compared  with  those  in  cluster  3  venues (alcohol-­ serving venues without sex work or a provision of on-­ site sex) (coefficient: 0.15, 95% CI: 0.030 to 0.287) after adjusting for age, sex and marital status. The estimate  of  the  between-­ venue  cluster  variance  was  0.267, and the residual variation in HIV vulnerability of venue workers  after  adjusting  for  three  individual  factors  was 0.156. This implies that 26.7% of the total variance in HIV vulnerability of venue workers can be attributed to venue cluster membership.  Individual venue  characteristics were also regressed against HIV vulnerability but did not show any significant positive association with HIV vulnerability.  For  more  results  from  regression  analyses  (both cluster and individual venue characteristics models), see online supplemental tables A2-A5.

## DISCUSSION

The findings of this study tend to be new to the literature on structural factors affecting HIV risk and vulnerability. As  mentioned  above,  clusters  of  high-­ risk  venues  have never been identified in Uganda, nor have their association with HIV vulnerability done before. Rather, social venues have been uniformly referred to by their venue types and individual features. We found four clusters of socialising venues.

In  terms  of  HIV  vulnerability,  working  at  alcohol-­ serving  sex  work  venues  with  a  provision  of  on-­ site sex  (cluster  1)  was  found  to  be  associated  with  HIV vulnerability  among  young  people  working  in  socialising  places  compared  with  any  other  clusters  (2  and 3-alcohol-­ serving sex work venues without a provision of  on-­ site  sex  and  alcohol-­ serving  venues  without  sex work or on-­ site sex, respectively), even after controlling for  sociodemographic characteristics (ie, age, sex and marital  status).  Relatedly,  evidence  from  our  supplemental  analysis  showed  big  numbers  of  HIV  positive cases among workers at alcohol-­ serving sex work venues with a provision of on-­ site sex (cluster 1) and workers at alcohol-­ serving venues with sex work, no music and no provision of sex on-­ site (cluster 2), indicative of a high HIV  burden  in  these  clusters.  Comparably,  a  similar burden of HIV (estimated at 10%-26% HIV prevalence) among workers at venues with similar characteristics as cluster 1 venues has been documented in a similar setting (Uganda and Tanzania). 8  43 This  high  burden  of  HIV in cluster 1 could be partly because the alcohol-­ serving sex  work  venues  with  a  provision  of  on-­ site  sex  had  a combination of risk-­ shaping features such as drinking, presence of sex brokers and sex solicitation. The presence of provisions of on-­ site sex elevates chances of sex activity on-­ site and a likelihood of a worker engaging in sex at the venue. Earlier studies in Zimbabwe and South Africa  also  noted  that  alcohol-­ serving  establishments amplified HIV transmission risks by providing a place where sex encounters occur. 44 45 Our findings were also consistent with recent studies that have highlighted the negative  health  impact  of  alcohol-­ serving  venues  with sex work and on-­ site sex in Argentina and Brazil. 30 33 46 A study by Sanchez et al highlighted that the presence of  reserved  areas  for  sex  at  the  venue  increased  the chance of sexual assault at the venue. Morojele et  al 45 also  noted  that  alcohol  establishments  are  often  sex venues, where back rooms, back corners, and adjacent buildings or shacks offer locations for sex. 45 Similarly, a study conducted by Graham et  al in  drinking venues also showed that sexual assault was common in drinking venues during dancing. 47 Given these findings, venues for  social  interaction  with  a  mixture  of  features  like cluster  1  venues  (ie,  alcohol  establishments  with  sex work,  sex  brokers  and  a  provision  of  on-­ site  sex)  are high-­ risk venues and should be prioritised for targeted HIV  preventive  interventions  if  maximum  reduction in new HIV infections is to be realised. These targeted interventions should address the underlying conditions that facilitate risky behaviours at the venues, and those that  create  obstacles  for  the  venue  workers  who  may wish to protect themselves against HIV.

In this study, workers and  customers of alcohol-­ serving  sex  work  venues  with  a  provision  of  on-­ site  sex were reported to find sexual partners on-­ site. A study in Zimbabwe reported that meeting sex partners in drinking places increased the likelihood of sex after drinking and reduced  condom  use. 48 Relatedly,  these  alcohol-­ serving sex work venues with a provision of on-­ site sex attracted mostly young female workers with no or minimal education, and who were often not married. These individual characteristics  are  known  indicators  of  HIV  vulnerability. 39 40

Alcohol-­ serving  venues with no sex work or on-­ site sex (cluster 3), and non-­ alcohol-­ serving venues (cluster 4) had fewer activities compared with the two clusters with a sex work feature (clusters 1 and 2). In contrast to the alcohol-­ serving  sex  work  venues  with  a  provision  of  on-­ site  sex  (cluster 1), alcohol-­ serving venues with no sex work or on-­ site sex (cluster 3), and non-­ alcohol-­ serving venues (cluster 4) were mostly drinking and eating establishments like restaurants, with  minimal  sexual  interactions.  The  absence  of  high-­ risk features at venues in clusters 3 and 4 offers a plausible explanation  for  the  lower  risk  status  of  alcohol-­ serving venues  with  no  sex  work  or  on-­ site  sex  (cluster  3)  and non-­ alcohol-­ serving  venues  (cluster  4).  On  the  contrary, a  study  by  Duke et  al noted  that  workers  of  restaurants had high rates of drinking problem 49 ; moreover, previous studies  have  suggested  relationships  between  employee drinking patterns and high-­ risk sexual behaviour. 2 12 46 Likewise, workers who are employed in restaurants where no alcohol is served (eg, fast food restaurants) or in establishments with strict policies against drinking on the job (eg, corporate-­ owned restaurants and franchises) reported high rates of alcohol use. 50-52 In terms of workers, non-­ alcohol-­ serving venues employed more young male workers than female workers. A study by Babirye et al noted similarities and  some  differences  in  the  sexual  behaviours  and  HIV burden of male and female workers of socialising places in Uganda. Male workers in socialising places were found to have a lesser burden of HIV compared with their female counterparts. 8

## Strength and limitations

We used national-­ level data to identify clusters of socialising  places  in  Uganda  and  also  explored  associations between  Ugandan  socialising  venue  clusters  and  HIV

vulnerability. Moreover, previous research has not identified  clusters  of  high-­ risk  venues,  neither  has  it  linked venue clusters to HIV vulnerability. In this study, we also discussed the combined role of venue features in shaping a risk environment at the venue by stimulating and amplifying risky sexual behaviours.

The strength of the cluster analysis used in this study is that the approach forms groupings in such a way that units within a group (or cluster) have a higher measure of similarity than units in any other cluster. However, the results and interpretation of any cluster analysis depend on several researchers' choices and assumptions, such as the actual groups that exist, the choice of the variables on which the elements in the groups should be similar, the distance measured, and the clustering procedure and the number of clusters determined. 53

The  presented  clusters  describe  the  study  sample population (only venues that had young workers (15-24 years)), obtained from a larger population-­ based survey, and therefore, cannot be extrapolated to the entire population  of  venues  for  social  interaction.  However,  given that venues for social interactions mostly employ young people, these findings remain critical.

There  are  several  limitations  in  the  study.  First,  HIV vulnerability  indicators  are  broadly  defined  to  cover a  wide  range  of  measures;  this  study  included  only  five measures that were in the secondary dataset. Relatedly, this paper only assessed HIV vulnerability among venue workers who tested HIV negative in the original survey. Second, one dataset was used for cluster analysis in this study;  thus,  no  alternative  dataset  was  used  to  validate the cluster solutions generated. This was however limited by  dividing  the  current  dataset  into  two  and  using  the other  half  to  validate  the  cluster  solutions.  Third,  the cross-­ sectional design of the primary study does not allow conclusions  on  causality  of  the  associations  identified. Fourth, the representativeness of the sample is unknown, which could impact the generalisability of the findings. Lastly,  due  to  the  intimate  and  sensitive  nature  of  the study questionnaire and the stigma associated with risky sexual behaviour and HIV/STI positive serostatus, under-­ reporting in the primary study was expected.

## CONCLUSION

In this study, we identified clusters of social venues in Uganda  and  found  that  the  cluster  that  had  alcohol-­ serving  sex  work  venues  with  a  provision  of  on-­ site sex  was  associated  with  vulnerability  to  HIV  among young  people  working  in  socialising  places.  Similarly, evidence  from  supplemental  analyses  indicated  high HIV  positivity  in  the  same  cluster.  We  have  showed that the collective risk features of venues in this high-­ risk  cluster  negatively  influenced  vulnerability  to  HIV among young workers. Our findings are also in line with previous studies that demonstrated the negative health impact of the same venue features as venues in the high-­ risk  cluster  in  this  study, 8  9  18  24  47-49 hence  supporting targeted HIV prevention efforts. Therefore, future HIV prevention efforts should target venues for social interaction with a combination of features as venues in the high-­ risk cluster in this study because despite the notable similarities with the other clusters such as drinking and music, these particularly have sex work, sex brokers and a provision of on-­ site sex, which amplify HIV transmission risks by facilitating coupling and instant sex on-­ site.

Finally,  the  authors  recommend  future  research  on the  relationship  between  venue  clusters  and  HIV  positivity, and also research looking at the social norms within venues in high-­ risk clusters to facilitate understanding of what shapes HIV risk and vulnerability within venues in high-­ risk clusters.
