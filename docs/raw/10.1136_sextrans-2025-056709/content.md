## **ABSTRACT** 

**Objective** Partner- level sexually transmitted infection (STI) data from eastern Africa are rare, despite high STI burden. To address this gap, we examined STI prevalence and clustering among cohabiting couples in two high HIV- burden Ugandan communities, representing the largest population- based, couple- level STI study in the region. 

**Methods** We analysed data from the Sexually Transmitted Infection Prevalence Study (STIPS), a crosssectional, population- based study in southern Uganda which tested participants for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomonas, syphilis, high- titre syphilis and herpes simplex virus- 2 (HSV- 2). We restricted the present analysis to STIPS participants in a cohabiting sexual relationship. Poisson regression with robust standard errors was used to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of all STIs by partner’s infection status. 

**Results** Among 423 cohabiting heterosexual couples, at least one partner tested positive for a curable STI (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomonas or high- titre syphilis) in 37% of couples. STIs were strongly clustered within partner dyads. For example, female participants with male partners with gonorrhoea had increased prevalence of not only gonorrhoea (PR 10.4, 95% CI 6.4 to 16.8) but also HIV (PR 2.2, 95% CI 1.5 to 3.2), chlamydia (PR 2.5, 95% CI 1.1 to 5.7), trichomonas (PR 2.4, 95% CI 1.2 to 4.7) and HSV- 2 (PR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1 

to 1.7). 

**Conclusion** Partners of individuals with an STI are more likely to have a curable STI, which may be the same or may be a different pathogen; broad screening of partners could be essential to curbing transmission and preventing reinfection. Comprehensive couple- based approaches, including partner notification, treatment and counselling strategies, are critical for reducing STI disease burden.

## **METHODS** 

We conducted a secondary analysis of the Sexually Transmitted Infection Prevalence Study (STIPS), a cross- sectional, populationbased study conducted in a fishing community and an inland community with high HIV burdens in southern Uganda between May 2019 and October 2019.<sup>7</sup> Nested within the populationbased Rakai Community Cohort Study (RCCS), STIPS tested consenting participants aged 18–49 years for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomonas, syphilis treponemal antibodies, high- titre syphilis (rapid plasma reagin titre ≥1:8) and herpes simplex virus- 2 (HSV- 2). STI testing, disclosure and treatment protocols are detailed elsewhere.<sup>7</sup> 

We restricted the present analysis to STIPS participants in a cohabiting sexual relationship based on RCCS census and survey data from the study period. A relationship was characterised as cohabiting if the sexual partners lived in the same household at the time of the census. Polygamous relationships were treated as multiple dyads in our analysis, with each unique male–female pair counted as a separate couple. An additional sensitivity analysis was performed excluding these polygamous relationships. Poisson regression with robust standard errors was used to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of all STIs by partner’s infection status. Statistical analysis was conducted in Stata V.15.1 (Stata Corp, College Station, Texas, USA) and R 4.2.1 (R Core Team, 2022). All p values were two- sided, and p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. 

## **RESULTS** 

Among 1825 STIPS participants, there were 828 individuals in 423 cohabiting relationships. Seventeen males had more than one partner and thus were included in the primary analysis in multiple couples (sixteen males had two female partners, and one male had three female partners). Demographic characteristics and STI transmission- related characteristics for all participants are in online supplemental table S1. Male partners were older than females (median age 36 vs 29 years) and reported a higher number of lifetime sexual partners (86% vs 32% reported four or more). Prevalence of curable STIs in this couples’ cohort was similar to prevalence in STIPS overall (25.4% vs 26.7%). 

At least one partner tested positive for a curable STI (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomonas or high- titre syphilis) in 37% (157/423) of couples. Online supplemental table S2 shows a tabulation of the number of different curable STIs among males and females within partner dyads. Among the 126 females with at least one curable STI, 21% (n=27) tested positive for more than one curable STI. Additionally, 45% (n=57) of their male partners had at least one curable STI, and 8% (n=10) had multiple curable STIs. In contrast, among the 297 females with no curable STIs, 10% (n=31) of their male partners had at least one curable STI, and only 1% (n=3) had multiple. 

Online supplemental table S3 shows the prevalence of STI concordance patterns. Among couples where at least one partner tested positive, HIV and HSV- 2 were more frequently seroconcordant than serodiscordant. Curable STIs were more often serodiscordant, with the female partner testing positive and the 

male partner testing negative. PRs of each STI based on partner’s infection status are shown in figure 1; associated prevalences are in online supplemental table S4a and 4b. Except for high- titre syphilis, the prevalence of a given STI was significantly higher among participants whose partner tested positive for that same STI. In the most notable example, gonorrhoea prevalence among male partners of females with gonorrhoea was 38% vs 2% among male partners of females without gonorrhoea (PR 17.8, 95% CI 8.05 to 39.4). The smallest increase in prevalence was seen with HSV- 2, where females with male partners with HSV- 2 were 2.3- fold more likely to have HSV- 2 (PR 2.3, 95% CI 1.9 to 2.8). Multiple STIs clustered within partner dyads. For example, male participants with female partners living with HIV had significantly increased prevalence of not only HIV (PR 6.6, 95% CI 4.7 to 9.4) but also gonorrhoea (PR 3.7, 95% CI 1.7 to 8.4), syphilis (PR 3.7, 95% CI 2.4 to 5.8), high- titre syphilis (PR 5.4, 95% CI 2.4 to 12.2) and HSV- 2 (PR 1.6, 95% CI 1.4 to 1.9). In another example, female participants with male partners with gonorrhoea had increased prevalence of not only gonorrhoea (PR 10.4, 95% CI 6.4 to 16.8) but also HIV (PR 2.2, 95% CI 1.5 to 3.2), chlamydia (PR 2.5, 95% CI 1.1 to 5.7), trichomonas (PR 2.4, 95% CI 1.2 to 4.7) and HSV- 2 (PR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.7). Chlamydia was generally an exception to this trend; in most cases, participants were not more likely to have a different STI when their partner tested positive for chlamydia. Online supplemental table S5a and 5b show the PRs from a sensitivity analysis excluding polygamous couples; none of the PRs differed meaningfully between our primary analysis shown in figure 1 and this sensitivity analysis. **DISCUSSION** This secondary analysis of data from the population- based STIPS in Uganda demonstrated that curable STI prevalence among cohabiting heterosexual partnerships was high at 37%. Prevalence for a given STI was substantially increased among participants with partners with the same STI, with PRs ranging from 2.3 for HSV- 2 to 17.8 for gonorrhoea. A prior study of STIs in HIV- 1- serodiscordant couples in Kenya reported a similar magnitude result: individuals with a partner with a treatable STI were 5.9- fold more likely to also have a treatable STI (OR 5.9, 95% CI 2.3 to 14.4)<sup>6</sup> ; our analysis builds on those findings with more recent, population- based data. Furthermore, multiple STIs tended to co- occur within couples. The biological and epidemiological links between HIV and STIs are well established,<sup>7</sup> but the patterns of clustering among non- HIV STIs remain less well understood. It is unclear why, for example, in our analysis, both male and female partners of individuals testing positive for high- titre syphilis more commonly had an STI other than syphilis. This may be due to the unique clinical course of syphilis, which is thought to be transmitted only during certain stages of infection, as well as differences in testing methods, as syphilis RPR testing has lower sensitivity in primary or asymptomatic infections.<sup>8</sup> In another exception to clustering trends, participants were not generally at increased risk of testing positive for chlamydia when a partner had a different pathogen, possibly because chlamydia confers some degree of protective immunity against reinfection.<sup>9</sup> In the larger STIPS cohort, including participants not in relationships, chlamydia was most often seen as a solitary infection with especially high prevalence in younger adults, while the other STIs were most often seen as co- infections and more evenly spread across age groups.<sup>7</sup> Overall, our findings underscore the need for improved partner notification

While interventions such as partner- delivered medication and post- treatment follow- up interactions are feasible, evidence from Uganda and similar contexts reveals suboptimal rates of willingness to notify a partner of an STI and even lower rates of successful partner treatment.<sup>2 4</sup> Studies of pregnant women with syphilis in Uganda report male partner treatment rates of 18%–35%.<sup>4 5</sup> While partner notification in cohabiting partnerships is typically higher than in casual partnerships, unique dynamics such as fear of violence, divorce and disclosing extramarital sex complicate disclosure in cohabiting relationships.<sup>1 2</sup> Additionally, research from South Africa has shown women in cohabiting relationships experience elevated STI risk relative to women without a cohabiting partner due to the belief they are protected from STIs, desire to become pregnant and inability to safely insist on condom use or refuse sex.<sup>1</sup> Further research is needed to identify safer and more effective couples’ STI 

testing and counselling strategies. Lessons can be drawn from HIV research on strategies such as notifying partners without revealing the identity of the index patient, providing patients with self- test kits to bring home to partners, offering financial incentives and diversifying testing venues beyond healthcare facilities.<sup>3</sup> 

Our findings can help inform clinical decision- making for individuals within cohabiting partnerships. The standard of STI care in sub- Saharan Africa involves syndromic management rather than laboratory testing. We previously published our findings that nearly half of STIPS participants reporting STI- related symptoms in the last 6 months did not seek any treatment, and one- third of those who did seek treatment still tested positive for a curable STI at the time of survey.<sup>10</sup> These findings, as well as the broadly elevated PRs across STIs observed in our study, suggest clinicians consider empirically treating both a symptomatic patient and their partner for multiple STIs, as curable STIs are often asymptomatic. While our data cannot differentiate between primary infections and reinfections, the high PRs may also suggest that even after treatment, individuals face an ongoing risk of reinfection by untreated cohabiting partners. This is especially relevant in antenatal settings, as Uganda’s antenatal care guidelines recommend STI testing only during the first trimester. However, reinfection remains a possibility without partner treatment. For example, the Syphilis Treatment of Partners trial of pregnant women in Kampala, Uganda, found that male partner non- treatment was independently associated with adverse birth outcomes (OR 2.75, 95% CI 2.36 to 3.21).<sup>4</sup> 

The generalisability of our results is limited by our study setting of two Ugandan communities and lower participation among men and younger individuals in STIPS. The cross- sectional nature of our study does not allow us to draw conclusions about the risk of reinfection after treatment within couples. Future research could explore the role of bacterial vaginosis in cohabiting couples, as STI coinfection is common, and we have previously reported on the high prevalence of BV in this study population.<sup>11 12</sup> 

## **CONCLUSION** 

This population- based secondary analysis shows high prevalence and co- occurrence of STIs among cohabiting couples, with substantially higher prevalence of STIs among individuals with an infected partner. Expanding the breadth of effective partner notification and treatment interventions is critical to addressing the high STI burden in this population. Further research is needed to better inform clinical decision- making about empiric treatment for co- occurring asymptomatic infections and rescreening individuals in stable partnerships after treatment, as well as how to most effectively target these efforts to optimise cost and resources.
