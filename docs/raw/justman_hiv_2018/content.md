## HIV Population Surveys - Bringing Precision to the Global Response

Jessica E. Justman, M.D., Owen Mugurungi, M.D., and Wafaa M. El-Sadr, M.D., M.P.H .

T he past two decades have seen extraordinary advances in our understanding  of  human  immunodeficiency virus (HIV). The global  scale-up  of  access  to  antiretroviral therapy (ART) for people living  with  HIV  is  perhaps  the greatest of these advances. As of the  end  of  2017,  more  than  20 million of the world's 37 million people with HIV were taking ART, often in the form of a single pill a  day.  Despite  this  major  public health achievement, almost 2 million  new  infections  continue  to occur  each  year, 1 leaving  many countries, primarily in sub-Saharan Africa, with a daunting epidemic.

In order to make further progress,  countries  need  to  answer some fundamental questions: How well are they faring in responding to their national epidemics? Where have  they  succeeded  and  where have they fallen short? What would be the best focus for future resources and efforts?

Until now, the information needed to answer these questions has come largely from health facility data and mathematical modeling. Each of these sources has its limits. Health facility HIV data vary in quality and include information only from people who have already  tested  HIV-positive.  Key groups are missing: those who have not had an HIV test and are unaware of their HIV-positive status, those who may be aware of their HIV-positive  status  but  have  yet to present at health facilities and engage in care, and those who've been lost to follow-up. Projections from  mathematical  models  are useful in understanding the trajectory of an epidemic but often depend on health facility data and on a series of assumptions.

These limitations highlight the need for population-based, directly measured estimates. Such data provide a more complete picture of an epidemic, including the gaps in the response, and point the way to  focused  approaches  and  efficient use of resources. Some population measures of the HIV epidemic have been available through the Demographic and Health Surveys  and  AIDS  Indicator  Surveys, but these surveys have not provided  direct  estimates  of  critically important measures, such as HIV incidence,  population-level  viral load suppression (defined as HIV RNA &lt;1000 copies per milliliter) as well as other essential biomarkers needed to measure progress.

To  address  the  need  for  detailed population HIV measures, the  Population-based  HIV  Impact Assessment  (PHIA)  Project  was launched in 2014 (http://phia .­ icap .­ columbia .­ edu/ ­ )  to  conduct  HIVfocused, household-based, nationally representative surveys of adults and  children  in  14  high-burden countries. These surveys, funded by the President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief and conducted by ICAP  at  Columbia  University  in partnership with  ministries of health and the Centers for Disease Control  and  Prevention,  go  beyond measuring HIV prevalence.

VLS is defined as an HIV RNA level below 1000 copies per milliliter and is an indicator of effective treatment in the individual and decreased risk of transmission of HIV to others. The highest VLS category, 73 to 100%, corresponds to full achievement of the UNAIDS target for VLS.

Each  survey  provides  a  national HIV report card, with direct measures of HIV incidence; pediatric prevalence;  primary  drug  resistance; and among all people with HIV, awareness of HIV infection, ART use, and overall prevalence of viral load suppression.

Initial findings from the PHIA surveys are already available from seven  countries  in  sub-Saharan Africa  (Zimbabwe,  Malawi,  Zambia,  Swaziland  [recently  renamed eSwatini], Lesotho, Tanzania, and Uganda), where approximately 200,000  individuals  from  more than 85,000 households have been surveyed.  The  findings  reveal  exceptional successes. For example, in the three contiguous southern African  countries  of  Zimbabwe, Malawi,  and  Zambia,  PHIA  surveys conducted in 2016 2 revealed that the combined prevalence of viral load suppression among all adults living with HIV, irrespective of  their  reported  knowledge  of their HIV-positive status or use of ART,  was  62%.  This  value,  the first  true  measure  of  national, population-level  viral  load  suppression across multiple countries, is  close  to  the  Joint  United  Nations Program on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS)  target of  73%  and higher than the 2014 estimate of 49%  in  the  United  States. 3 This high level of viral load suppression is  all  the  more  remarkable  given the  very  high  HIV  prevalence  of 12.2% among adults in the three countries combined.

Digging  deeper  into  the  data reveals common challenges faced by all seven countries. For example, awareness of HIV-positive status is, surprisingly, a much bigger challenge than achieving high uptake of ART and viral load suppression once people are aware of their  HIV  infection.  The  consistently  low  proportions  of  men, adolescents, and young adults who are aware of their HIV status 4 tells us that HIV-testing programs that focus on men and young people are essential. These insights would not  be  possible  without  population-based data.

Beyond such national data, the PHIA  Project  findings  also  provide  an  unprecedented  level  of population-based, subnational detail about each country's epidemic and offer a blueprint to guide its national response. The maps show the  geographic  variation  within the first seven surveyed countries in  the  prevalence  of  both  HIV and viral load suppression. These snapshots  of  the  HIV  epidemic show which areas are doing well and where discrepancies between HIV prevalence and viral load suppression  indicate  a  compelling need to direct resources.

Population-based data are even more valuable when repeated surveys reveal trends over time. For example, Swaziland, a small country  in  southern  Africa  with  the highest  HIV  prevalence  in  the world,  has  had  two  consecutive population-based,  national  HIV surveys, first in 2011 and again in 2016. During that 5-year interval, there was a 44% reduction in HIV incidence along with a doubling of population viral load suppression, from 35% to 71% among HIV-positive people 18 to 49 years of age, irrespective of reported awareness of  HIV  infection  or  ART  status. 5 Swaziland's successful initial response to its epidemic is a testimony to its government's commitment to scaling up HIV treatment and prevention.

Planning and implementing population surveys requires concerted efforts. These include using a two-stage cluster random-sampling scheme to ensure national representativeness and establishing close collaborations with incountry  partners  to  achieve  high response rates, both key elements in  population  surveys.  Carefully planned community-sensitization campaigns  have  to  be  launched weeks in advance of each survey. Field survey workers require rigorous  training  in  order  to  conduct high-quality household-based HIV counseling and testing, return the results, and refer HIV-positive survey participants for care. Similarly, highly trained laboratory staff need  to  process  and  store  blood specimens at designated laboratories  in  a  timely  manner.  Robust data-management systems are required  to  enable  the  transfer  of electronic questionnaire data collected by field workers to centralized  data  servers,  allowing  for real-time  monitoring  of  survey progress and data quality, all with the  goal  of  making  the  findings available  as  soon  as  possible. Though  these  elements  may  be viewed  as  complex  and  costly, the information gained can prevent the misdirection of resources  and  enhance  the  impact  of interventions.

Looking ahead, there may be a need for frequent, focused surveys in areas and among subpopulations  where  the  HIV  burden and need for care are greatest and new infection rates are highest. In such settings, data from population  surveys  that  use  innovative assays for HIV incidence, such as those conducted through the PHIA Project,  may  be  combined  with health facility data to improve geospatial  modeling  of  subnational HIV trends. To address the HIV epidemic and other health threats effectively, it is critical to have accurate and current population-level estimates in order to measure how we are doing and to guide future actions with precision.
