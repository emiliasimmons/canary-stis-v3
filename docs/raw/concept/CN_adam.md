**Beyond treatment: modeling the strategic value of STI diagnostics for
HIV prevention**

Adam Akullian

TBD co-authors / collaborators: Robyn Stewart, Michelle O'Brien, Jeff
Imai-Eaton, M. Kate Grabowski

Curable STIs are highly prevalent among women across sub-Saharan Africa
and are associated with increased risk of HIV acquisition. Because STI's
and HIV are transmitted on the same sexual network, the presence of an
STI may be a marker of both behavioral and biological factors that
increase HIV risk. Randomized trials of STI treatment, however, have
shown inconsistent effects on HIV incidence, highlighting a gap between
individual-level mechanisms and population-level impact. STIs may thus
serve less as direct drivers of HIV persistence in a population and more
as observable markers of HIV, offering opportunities to identify
priority populations for HIV prevention and provide insights into
underlying sexual network structure.

Recent advances in point-of-care STI diagnostics create an opportunity
to leverage STI management platforms for the efficient delivery of HIV
prevention. The INSTEP individual randomized trial, embedded within a
long-standing community cohort study in rural Uganda, will evaluate
whether integrating STI diagnostics into HIV prevention services can
improve identification of women at elevated risk and increase PrEP
uptake and persistence. We propose a complementary modeling study to
quantify the broader population-level impact of STI diagnostics across
multiple pathways to HIV incidence reduction, including: (i) direct
treatment of STIs and reduced biological susceptibility to HIV; (ii)
prevention of onward STI transmission; (iii) identification of
individuals at elevated HIV risk for delivery of HIV prevention (iv)
identification of high-transmission sexual networks where interventions
may have disproportionate impact.

This modeling framework will explicitly quantify the relative
contribution of each pathway to overall HIV incidence reduction and
assess how these contributions vary by setting, including differences in
HIV incidence, prevalence, sexual network structure, and stage of the
HIV epidemic. By decomposing these pathways, the analysis will provide
insight into when, where, and among whom STI diagnostics can generate
the greatest impact.

Key questions include:

1.  How much of the impact of STI diagnostics arises from direct
    treatment of infections versus improved targeting of HIV prevention
    interventions?

2.  How much more efficient is targeting HIV prevention to individuals
    with an STI compared to other risk indicators?

3.  To what extent do partner notification and testing amplify the
    impact of STI diagnostics?

4.  To what extent does STI positivity identify individuals connected to
    high-transmission sexual networks where HIV risk is concentrated?

5.  How does the effectiveness and efficiency of STI diagnostics vary by
    epidemiologic context, including HIV and STI prevalence and epidemic
    phase?

To address these questions, we will develop a transmission modeling
framework that integrates HIV and STI dynamics using the Institute for
Disease Modeling's HIVsim model embedded with the Starsim modeling
platform, leveraging the key feature that multiple STIs and HIV transmit
over shared underlying sexual networks. The model will be calibrated to
empirical cohort data from the Rakai Community Cohort Study setting and
used to simulate alternative diagnostic deployment strategies. We will
explore the sensitivity of results to assumptions about sexual network
structure, including the role of core groups and highly connected
individuals, as well as health-seeking behavior and care cascades, which
may act as critical bottlenecks to impact.

This work will generate decision-relevant evidence on how STI
diagnostics can be used as a strategic platform for precision HIV
prevention, informing where, among whom, and how these tools can be
deployed to achieve the greatest impact in high-burden settings.
