## Using Biomarkers to Assess the Validity of Sexual Behavior Reporting across Interview Modes among Young Women in Kampala, Uganda

Christine A. Kelly, Paul C. Hewett, Barbara S. Mensch, Johanna C. Rankin, Samuel L. Nsobya, Samuel Kalibala, and Pamela N. Kakande

Understanding the transmission dynamics of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections is critically dependent on accurate behavioral data. This study investigates the effect of the mode of questionnaire delivery on the quality of sexual behavior reporting in a 2010 survey conducted in Kampala, Uganda, among 18-24-year-old women. We compare the reported prevalence of five sexual outcomes across three interview modes: traditional face-to-face interviewing (FTFI) in which question rewording was permitted, FTFI administered via computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) in which questions were read as written, and audio computer-assisted self-interviewing (ACASI) in which participants listened to prerecorded questions and entered responses using a computer touchscreen. We then assess the validity of the data by evaluating the reporting of sexual experience against three biological markers. Results suggest that ACASI elicits higher reporting of some key indicators than FTFI does, but self-reports from all interview modes were subject to validity concerns when compared with biomarker data. The study highlights the important role that biomarkers can play in sexual behavior r  esearch. (Studies in Family Planning 2014; 45[1]: 43-58)

U nderstanding the transmission dynamics of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) at the population level and evaluating programmatic interventions designed to contain their spread are critically dependent on accurate data regarding sexual behavior. Given that sexual activity cannot be directly observed, research must rely on self-reports, which are subject to problems of recall, question misinterpretation, social desirability bias, and normative reporting. Indeed, notable oddities in behavioral data-including STIs among women claiming to be virgins (Buvé et al. 2001; Glynn et al. 2001; Cowan et al. 2002), lower than expected HIV incidence among men reporting multiple nonmarital partners (Nnko et al. 2004), and discrepancies between information reported during individual interviews and that obtained from medical examination (Rassjö, Mirembe, and Darj 2011)-suggest that under- or over-reporting of sexual activity distorts assessments of HIV risk.

Biases in reporting may be reduced or exacerbated depending on the mode of questionnaire delivery. Much of the survey data from developing countries-most notably from Demographic and Health Surveys (DHSs)-are collected using traditional face-to-face interviewing (FTFI). Although this method has logistical and efficiency advantages over other approaches, reporting via FTFI may be compromised by interviewers' inaccurate rewording of questions, directive probes, nonverbal cues, and inappropriate feedback (Fowler and Mangione 1990). Researchers leading studies in Kenya and Malawi that found high levels of consistent reporting of sexual behaviors in FTFI, compared with audio computer-assisted self-interviewing (ACASI), speculated that, despite instructions to the contrary, interviewers sometimes skipped questions to which they assumed they already knew the answers, or reconciled discrepant responses on their own (Mensch et al. 2008a). Turner and colleagues (2009) found that participants in a clinical trial acknowledged deliberately misleading interviewers in FTFI in an attempt to be polite to researchers or because they feared criticism or embarrassment.

Increasing attention has been paid to technology-based interview methods, including ACASI, as potential alternatives to FTFI (Phillips et al. 2010). One of the primary advantages of ACASI's interview technique is the private nature of its data collection and thus the potential to reduce social desirability bias in the reporting of sensitive or stigmatized behaviors. With ACASI, the participant listens to prerecorded questions through headphones and enters responses using a touchscreen or answer keypad. This technique affords a greater standardization of questionnaire administration by providing an identical script to all respondents and, therefore, minimizes interviewer effects. ACASI is a technique that can be implemented in low-development settings and has been shown-for example, in India (Potdar and Koenig 2005), Kenya (Hewett, Mensch, and Erulkar 2004), Malawi (Mensch et al. 2008a), Vietnam (Le et al. 2006), and Zimbabwe (Langhaug et al. 2011)-to elicit higher reporting of some sensitive behaviors relative to other interview modes, although results vary by setting and sample. A recent systematic review of questionnaire delivery modes in developing countries found that, overall, ACASI produced greater reporting of sensitive behaviors than did FTFI (Langhaug, Sherr, and Cowan 2010), whereas a second review and meta-analysis of quantitative interviewing tools in low- and middle-income countries concluded that results depended on the sensitivity of the outcome and the population under study (Phillips et al. 2010).

In instances in which levels or types of reporting statistically differ among interview modes, greater reporting of sexual activity and other risk behaviors typically is assumed to reflect more accurate reporting, at least among women, because of the stigma associated with acknowledging such activities (Hewett et al. 2008). Formally assessing the truthfulness of reporting in different interview modes is vital, however, for determining the relative validity of various modes of data collection regarding sensitive behaviors. Including an external validation measure against which self-reported behavior can be compared is informative. STI biomarkers can provide such an objective standard, although relatively few studies have combined randomized interview mode experimentation with STI outcomes as indicators of risky behavior (Langhaug, Sherr, and Cowan 2010; Phillips et al. 2010; Siegfried and Mathews 2010). Exceptions include Hewett and colleagues' experiment among women in São Paulo, Brazil (2008), which evaluated sexual behavior reporting in ACASI and FTFI against STI biomarkers and found both that reporting of STI risk behavior was significantly greater in the computerized interview, and that associations between risk behavior and STI infection were stronger in ACASI relative to FTFI. In South Africa, Mensch and colleagues (2011) also observed an advantage of ACASI over FTFI in a placebo gel microbicide study that tested participants for recent semen exposure. In contrast, Minnis and colleagues (2009) found in Zimbabwe that self-reported sexual activity and condom use were equally problematic in FTFI and ACASI when validated against an objective biomarker of recent unprotected sex.

This study builds on existing literature by combining a randomized interview mode experiment with three different biological outcomes. Unlike most previous experiments, the study also explicitly examines the role of interviewer effects in FTFI by comparing two interview delivery styles.

## METHODS

The study was conducted among women aged 18-24 years in Kampala, Uganda. Using the sampling frame from the 2002 census, 40 enumeration areas in Kampala were selected using simple random sampling. Within each sampled enumeration area, 30 households were selected, also using simple random sampling. All women aged 18-24 who were either permanent residents of selected households or visitors to the household the night preceding the interview were eligible to participate in the study.

Study participants were randomized to one of three interview modes:

-  Group 1: FTFI using conventional paper and pencil, with question rewording permitted.
-  Group 2: FTFI using computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI), 1  with questions read as written.
-  Group 3: Traditional FTFI for nonsensitive questions and ACASI for sensitive ones.

The questionnaire used was a subset of questions from the women's instrument of the 2006 Uganda DHS. The survey covered a number of sensitive topics, including sexual initiation, recent sexual activity, transactional sex, forced sex, condom use, and HIV/AIDS awareness. As is standard practice in the DHS (ICF Macro 2011), interviewers in Group 1 were permitted, when necessary, to reword questions to improve respondents' understanding. 2  For Group 2 (CAPI-questions read as written), interviewers were trained only to read questions from the computer screen verbatim, regardless of participants' comprehension. The response option 'Respondent does not understand question' was added to each question, and treated as missing in analyses. For Group 3 (ACASI), nonsensitive questions were administered via paper-and-pencil FTFI, following the same protocol as Group 1. Sensitive questions were administered using handheld computers; participants listened through headphones and recorded their answers by pressing colored buttons on the screen. In all interview modes, questionnaires were offered in either English or Luganda.

1 CAPI interviewers used handheld computers to read questions and enter participants' answers, eliminating the need for paper questionnaires and data entry.

2 The following instructions were included in the interviewers' training manual for paper-and-pencil FTFI: 'If the respondent has not understood the question, you should repeat the question slowly and clearly. If there is still a problem, you may reword the question, being careful not to alter the meaning of the original question. '

Block randomization was used to assign respondents to one of the three interview modes. Respondents were also randomized to an interviewer to control for the potential effect of interviewer characteristics. Interviewers were selected from a cadre of trained personnel having previous experience administering the Uganda DHS. Because of interviewers' familiarity with DHS protocol, Group 1 and Group 3 interviews were undertaken first, after which interviewers were retrained to administer Group 2 interviews. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants.

Upon completion of the survey and administration of additional informed consent procedures, a blood sample was obtained via finger prick for HIV and herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2) testing. HIV testing was conducted at households, consistent with the Uganda Ministry of Health Guidelines (Uganda Ministry of Health 2003). Specimens for the HIV test were evaluated using Determine. ™  Positive samples were then retested using Uni-Gold ™  Recombigen ® . Both Determine ™  and Uni-Gold ™  Recombigen ®  have a sensitivity of nearly 100 percent and a specificity greater than 99 percent. A third rapid test, Clearview ®  HIV 1/2 STAT-PAK ® (99.7 percent sensitivity, 99.9 percent specificity), served as a tiebreaker in the event of discordant results. After completion of HIV testing, respondents were provided with their results, post-test counseling, and, if applicable, referrals for follow-up testing and care.

HSV-2 testing was conducted at the Molecular Research Laboratory (MOLAB) in Kampala, which is part of the Makerere University and University of California, San Francisco (MU-UCSF) Research Collaboration. The HSV-2 testing used dried blood spots (collected at the household) instead of whole blood because of the difficulties associated with storing whole blood and with transporting it to the lab. The HSV-2 tests were based on the Kalon ELISA antibody test on dried blood spots. Of the initial 277 samples tested using the Kalon assay with a 1:4 dilution, however, only 5 tested positive, a prevalence well below expectations based on prior sero-prevalence surveys. Thus, the conclusion was drawn that validation of dried blood spots for the Kalon HSV-2 test, which theretofore had not been conducted, needed to be undertaken. The validation, conducted at MOLAB, tested dilutions of 1:2, 1:3, and 1:4 on 220 stored serum-tested positive and negative specimens from the 2004-05 Uganda HIV/AIDS Sero-Behavioural Survey, and found that the 1:2 ratio of buffer and eluate produced the best results with dried blood spots. The sensitivity and specificity of the test were relatively low, however: 85 percent and 74 percent, respectively (Marandet et al. 2013). The HSV-2 results reported here should therefore be interpreted with caution, although any misclassifications should be independent of interview mode. HSV-2 results were provided to 67 respondents before dissemination was discontinued after concerns regarding the sensitivity and specificity of the dried blood spots testing arose.

In addition to providing a blood sample, participants were asked to perform a self-administered vaginal swab for a Rapid Stain Identification of Human Semen (RSID ™ -Semen) test, a third study biomarker. The RSID test, which indicates whether a woman has engaged in unprotected sex during the previous 48 hours, has the sensitivity to detect the presence of as little as 1 µl of semenogelin, a major component of seminal fluid (Mauck 2009). RSID specimens were self-swabbed by respondents at the household, placed in a dry tube, and transported to MOLAB for testing. Women were told that the RSID test identified the presence of semenogelin in vaginal fluids, but not that their sexual-activity reporting would be compared with the test result.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Population Council Institutional Review Board, the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology, and the Makerere University Higher Degrees Research and Ethics Committee.

## Analysis

The analysis, which combines interview and biomarker data, focuses primarily on two evaluations of the reporting of sexual behavior:

1. A comparison of the reporting between Groups 1 (FTFI) and 2 (CAPI) to examine the effects of interviewer assistance and question rewording on reporting.
2. A comparison of the reporting between Groups 2 (CAPI) and 3 (ACASI) to assess whether the strict reading of questions in the CAPI mode reproduces the standardization of the ACASI interview, albeit retaining potential other interviewer effects on reporting.

For completeness, we also compare Groups 1 and 3, which is the interview mode comparison that has been conducted in prior experimental research (Mensch, Hewett, and Erulkar 2003; Mensch et al. 2008a).

Statistical analysis was conducted in Stata version 12.1. Using pairwise z tests, we begin by comparing the proportions of respondents interviewed via FTFI, CAPI, and ACASI who report five key sexual outcomes: ever had vaginal sexual intercourse, ever forced to have sex, transactional sex in the past 12 months, more than one lifetime sexual partner, and unprotected sex in the past two days. We categorize a woman as having had sex if she provided an age at first intercourse. We also include 11 women in the CAPI group who responded 'do not know' to the question regarding age at first sex but reported sexual activity elsewhere in the survey. Respondents are considered to have experienced forced sex if they reported any of the following: forced sexual initiation, forced sex with current or most recent husband/partner, forced sex with a nonmarital partner in the past 12 months, or forced sex with any person at any other time. Women who reported having transactional sex answered affirmatively to the question: 'In the past 12 months, did you ever give or receive money, gifts or favours in exchange for sex?' Respondents were also asked how many different individuals they had sexual intercourse with in their lifetime. We identify those having more than one lifetime sexual partner, excluding those who answered 'do not know' unless elsewhere in the survey they reported having more than one partner in the past year or having been married more than once. The indicator of unprotected sex in the past two days combines reporting of (1) sexual activity on the day of the interview, the previous day, and two days prior, and (2) condom use on each day of reported sex.

We examine the association between interview mode and the reporting of the five selected outcomes in a stepwise fashion, comparing odds ratios from unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models. In the multivariate models, we control both for characteristics of questionnaire delivery and for selected sociodemographic variables. Specifically included are the language of the questionnaire (English or Luganda) relative to the language of the interview (in Groups 1 and 2, interviewers could conduct the interview in a language other than that used for the questionnaire), and whether the interview was conducted in the respondent's native language. 3 We thereby aim to control for potential question misinterpretation by respondents and possible mistranslation by interviewers. We also control for age, highest level of schooling attended, marital status, religion, asset ownership, paid work in the past 12 months, and current contraceptive use.

Introducing the biological data, we then validate the reporting of sexual activity and condom use. We first calculate the number of respondents in each interview mode who did not report ever having sex and yet tested positive for HIV or HSV-2. For the RSID test, we examine reporting of unprotected sex in the past two days. Because of rapid clearance of semenogelin from vaginal fluid after exposure, and thus the likelihood that the positive RSID tests underestimate true prevalence of recent unprotected sex, we follow Minnis and colleagues (2009) by restricting our analysis of discrepant data to those respondents who tested positive for RSID. The analysis concludes with an examination of item nonresponse in each of the three interview modes, particularly that which derives from question incomprehension in the CAPI group.

## RESULTS

Figure 1 shows the number of completed interviews and completed biomarker tests in each questionnaire delivery mode. Overall, 82 percent of eligible women (1,058 ÷ 1,297) were successfully interviewed. The completion rate 4 in the CAPI group (342 ÷ 404 = 85 percent) was significantly higher than that in ACASI (351 ÷ 449 = 78 percent) (p = 0.016). No difference was found between the CAPI and FTFI (365 ÷ 444 = 82 percent) arms, or between FTFI and ACASI. Thirtytwo women refused to participate in the study, with a slightly-but not quite statistically significantly-lower proportion (1 percent) of eligible women in the CAPI group declining relative to the other two interview modes (3 percent in FTFI, p = 0.060; 3 percent in ACASI, p = 0.093). An additional 7 percent and 4 percent of the electronic data collected via CAPI and ACASI, respectively, were unrecovered because of technology or backup-protocol failure. The analytic sample consists of 1,020 women: 365 in Group 1; 317 in Group 2; and 338 in Group 3.

Table 2 compares the reported prevalence of the sexual experience measures across interview modes. Reporting of sexual experiences was highest in the ACASI mode in four of the five outcomes of interest, although not all of these differences were statistically significant. Ninetytwo percent of respondents using ACASI reported ever having sex, which was significantly greater than the 86 percent reported in the CAPI group and marginally significantly greater than the 88 percent reported in FTFI (p = 0.076). Prevalence of forced sex was also highest in the ACASI group, and the difference was statistically significant when compared with the FTFI group. Note that respondents were given the opportunity to report an episode of forced sex even if they had not provided an age at sexual debut; 17 women (1 in FTFI, 8 in CAPI, 8 in ACASI) did so. However, the questionnaire skip pattern restricted subsequent questions regarding sexual activity to those who had reported an age at first sex. Among these women, in the ACASI group a higher percentage acknowledged more than one lifetime sexual partner than did respondents in FTFI or CAPI, but the differences were not significant. Approximately one-fifth (19 percent) of sexually active women in the ACASI group reported engaging in transactional sex, which was a significantly greater proportion than the 12 percent in the FTFI group. Although similar proportions of women (26-30 percent) in each of the three interview modes reported having had sex during the previous two days, reporting of condom use was significantly higher in ACASI, compared with FTFI and CAPI. Given the presumed social desirability associated with condom use in a country having high HIV prevalence, the opposite pattern of responses was expected.

## Validation with Biomarkers

The number of women in each group who completed the biomarker tests is shown in Figure 1. Women in the CAPI arm were less likely to refuse one or more biomarker tests than were women in the FTFI (5.6 percent versus 12.6 percent, p = 0.001) or ACASI (5.6 percent versus 9.4 percent, p = 0.054) groups. Among those who consented to the biomarker component, 4.4 percent, 2.5 percent, and 2.2 percent of the results were incomplete in Groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively, as a result of insufficient or spoiled samples, failure to safely return specimens from the field, or data-entry error. A comparison of sociodemographic characteristics among women for whom one or more biomarker results are missing-because of refusals or for other reasons-shows that these women do not differ from their counterparts having complete biomarker data (not shown). Similarly, among women who undertook biomarker testing but for whom corresponding survey data are missing, the prevalence of positive HIV, HSV-2, and RSID tests does not statistically differ from the prevalence in the analytic sample.

Table 4 presents the number of women, among those for whom survey and biomarker data were available, who reported recent sexual activity and condom use, according to interview mode and the results from two of the three biomarker tests. Among women with complete data, 7.3 percent tested positive for HIV: 9.1 percent of those in the FTFI group, 7.3 percent in CAPI, and 5.4 percent in ACASI (not shown). The difference between FTFI and ACASI was marginally significant (p = 0.079). Because all participants who tested positive for HIV also reported sexual initiation, we do not present these results in Table 4.

The top panel of Table 4 enables us to calculate that 47 percent of women screened positive for HSV-2 antibodies, with prevalence varying slightly between interview modes: 44 percent FTFI, 51 percent CAPI, and 46 percent ACASI. The difference between CAPI and FTFI was marginally significant (p = 0.078). Unlike for HIV, the HSV-2 test results provide some evidence of discrepant reporting. Among those who tested positive, 5.6 percent in the FTFI group reported never having had sex, 4.5 percent did so in CAPI, and 4.9 percent in ACASI.

Some of the apparent discrepant reporting may stem from result misclassification, given the specificity of the test. According to the Kalon validation with dried blood spots, the positive predictive value-or the probability that a participant who tested positive is actually positive-is 0.75 (95 percent CI: 0.69-0.81). Hence, the probability of each self-reported virgin who tested positive being positive is 0.75. This will affect the magnitude of the estimates, but because the distribution of false positives should be independent of interview mode, the comparisons should not be influenced.

Table 4 also analyzes the association between RSID test results and the reporting of recent sex and condom use. Among women who tested RSID positive, 38 (49 percent) did not report unprotected sex in the previous two days in FTFI, and 49 (55 percent) did not report unprotected sex in CAPI. Fifty-one women, or 67 percent of those with a positive RSID result, did not report unprotected sex in the past two days in ACASI, a proportion that is significantly greater than in the FTFI group. Interestingly, although the vast majority of discrepant reports in the FTFI and CAPI groups stemmed from women reporting not having sex in the past two days, one-sixth of women in ACASI who tested positive for RSID reported using condoms during all recent sex acts. 6

## Item Nonresponse

Finally, we briefly examined the extent to which the sociodemographic characteristics and sexual activity indicators displayed in Tables 1 and 2 were affected by item nonresponse (not shown). In the FTFI and ACASI interviews, respondents were given the option of declining to answer a particular question or to answer 'do not know.' In the CAPI group, interviewers had the additional opportunity of indicating that participants did not understand a question by using a designated response option. For questions regarding sociodemographic characteristics, 7  no participants in any interview mode did not know the answer or declined to respond, but question incomprehension was noted by interviewers in the CAPI interview. Whereas questions regarding schooling and childbirth, for example, were minimally affected, 26 women-or 8 percent of participants having nonmissing data-did not understand at least one of the series of five questions regarding labor force participation. 8  Among the sexual behavior questions, reporting of transactional sex was most affected by question incomprehension: 9 women-or 3 percent of the subset of sexually active women-did not understand the question. In addition, CAPI participants were significantly more likely than FTFI respondents to answer 'do not know' to questions regarding age at first sex (3 percent versus 0 percent, p &lt; 0.001) or to the number of lifetime sexual partners (4 percent versus 1 percent, p = 0.031).

## DISCUSSION

We investigated whether the reported prevalence of five sexual outcomes differed by interview mode, and then assessed the validity of self-reported data against three biological outcomes. Considered alongside the biomarker data, our results indicate that self-reported sexual behavior data are problematic irrespective of questionnaire delivery mode. Although no self-­ reported virgins tested positive for HIV, approximately 5 percent of women who screened positive for HSV-2 denied sexual initiation, whereas between 49 and 67 percent of women who tested positive for RSID reported not engaging in unprotected sex in the past two days. These figures are consistent with findings from previous studies that have identified discrepant reporting using biomarkers (Gallo et al. 2013). 9 Although the self-reported data from all three delivery modes raise concerns, we did observe differences between the groups with regard to reporting of sensitive behaviors, data validity, and item nonresponse.

The first featured comparison, between variants of the face-to-face interview-paper and pencil (question rewording permitted) and CAPI (questions read as written)-did not reveal any significant differences in either the reported prevalence of sexual activity or in the magnitude of discrepant data when validated against biomarker results. This suggests that interviewer delivery had neither a strongly positive nor strongly negative effect on the reporting of our outcomes of interest. Importantly, because the interviewers had previous experience administering the Uganda DHS, in which question rewording is permitted, they may have found adhering to the Group 2 protocol difficult. Deviations from protocol may then explain the absence of differences in reporting between the two FTFI groups. Nevertheless, we did find that item nonresponse to questions related both to sociodemographic characteristics and to sexual behavior was greater in CAPI relative to FTFI, stemming partly from question incomprehension. Although the magnitude of nonresponse in Group 2 was generally very low, Group 1 interviewers likely played some role in clarifying queries and resolving inconsistencies. Alternatively, participants who were questioned by more 'active' interviewers may have felt pressured to provide responses even if they did not understand the question or know the correct response (Mensch et al. 2008b).

The second featured comparison explored whether ACASI elicited greater reporting of sensitive behavior than did FTFI administered using a standardized script from which interviewers were instructed not to deviate. Results show that reporting of four key measuressexual initiation, forced sex, multiple lifetime partners, and transactional sex-was greater in ACASI than in CAPI, but the differences were not statistically significant for the second and third of these measures and only marginally significant for the first and fourth. Compared with biological data, however, reporting in ACASI was equally or more inconsistent than in the other groups. The inflated reporting of condom use in ACASI is particularly puzzling given the presumed social desirability associated with using condoms.

Several possible explanations can be given for the discrepant data that do not necessarily imply deliberate misreporting. First, because more women in the ACASI arm reported forced or transactional sex, greater condom use among this group could reflect these riskier partnerships. However, we found no association between the reporting of recent condom use and risky sexual behavior. Alternatively, the survey instrument did not include questions concerning condom slippage or breakage, which may have caused some respondents who reported having protected sex nevertheless to be exposed to semenogelin. We would, though, expect condom failures to be independent of questionnaire delivery mode.

9 If participants had been informed before completing the interview that their responses would be compared against biological specimens, their accuracy may have been improved, but a recent study did not find a significant difference in reporting of unprotected sex between participants randomized to know and not know about subsequent biomarker validation (Thomsen et al. 2007).

Finally, although the wording and sequence of condom-use questions was the same in ACASI as in the other two modes, and reporting of condom use was not associated with low education or literacy levels that might signal question incomprehension, some participants may have misunderstood what was being asked, especially because reporting of condom use elsewhere in the survey did not differ across modes. A calendar question such as this, which involved respondents first answering a series of questions concerning sexual activity on each of the previous five days, and then a set of associated questions regarding condom use on those particular days, may not be suited to self-administered interview modes. More generally, although the proportion of respondents answering 'do not know' or declining to answer questions in ACASI was low, it is conceivable that, given the experience in the standardized CAPI interview, some participants may have registered responses to questions they did not understand. A key advantage of the automated, self-administered nature of ACASI is the elimination of interviewer effects, but such a design removes the opportunity for interviewers to probe respondents or clarify questions, which may consequently restrict the level of complexity of data that can be collected (Luke, Clark, and Zulu 2011).

Greater reporting of some of the sexual behavior indicators in ACASI relative to the other two modes could, on the contrary, reflect a greater ease with the privacy afforded by the computerized interview. In a South African clinical trial, Turner and colleagues (2009) found that female participants overwhelmingly preferred being interviewed via ACASI rather than FTFI (46 percent versus 8 percent; 45 percent expressed no preference). The majority of women also reported feeling that the computerized interview was either equally (33 percent) or more (61 percent) private than FTFI (7 percent). Beauclair and colleagues (2013) similarly found that ACASI was preferred to FTFI among urban South African respondents, but identified certain participants-those who were unemployed or aged 40-70 years old-who were less likely to find the method easy to use. Overall, the effectiveness of ACASI has been shown to vary by study visit, population, setting, and research objective (Gallo et al. 2013). Thus, in contexts where evidence is limited concerning the utility of a particular interview mode, acceptability pilot studies would be advised.

In view of the problems associated with self-reported data, biomarkers can play an important role in sexual behavior research by providing alternate, and presumably more accurate, measures of STI risk, and by validating self-reported behavioral data (Mauck 2009). Of course, incorporating biomarker testing into research studies increases the cost and logistical challenge of data collection. Kalon ELISA test kits are fairly inexpensive (US$3 per test) (Ng'ayo et el. 2010), whereas RSID-Semen kits cost nearly US$8 per test. 10 The use of either of these kits necessitates additional expenditure for sample collection, storage, and transport, and for laboratory analysis. In field-based research, securing the level of privacy required for sample collection may be difficult, as may obtaining the consent of participants unfamiliar with the procedures involved (Gallo et al. 2013), although these factors did not appear to have a big effect on our study. Finally, STI biomarkers may be of limited use as a means of validating selfreports if prevalence is low, demonstrated by the HIV results featured here.

In studies in which including biological data is not feasible, and in which outcomes such as coital frequency and exposure to anal sex cannot adequately be measured by biomarker tests, researchers must continue to rely on self-reports. Investigators should therefore continue to develop and evaluate innovative methods of collecting self-reported data that promote accurate reporting, and to supplement such methods with biomarker tests.
