# 35 **SUMMARY** 

- 36 _Background_ 

- 37 Novel HIV prevention interventions such as long-acting pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) could 

38 

- substantially reduce HIV transmission in Africa. However, efficient implementation in high- 

39 

prevalence settings where incidence has declined requires an understanding of the 

40 

contemporary dynamics driving new infections. 

41 

- 42 _Methods_ 

43 We identified incident HIV cases from a longitudinal, population-based cohort in Uganda. We 

44 

individually matched cases to HIV-negative controls; traced and enrolled reported sexual 

45 partners; and enrolled female sex workers (FSWs) from reported venues. Conditional logistic 46 regression, transmission modeling, and phylogenetics were used to characterize transmission 47 networks. 

48

# _Findings_ 

50 From 2021-2024, 38,899 HIV tests among 22,255 people identified 187 people with incident 51 infections (47.6% male); 164 (88%) were enrolled and matched to 164 HIV-negative controls. 

52

53

# 71 **INTRODUCTION** 

- 72 Since 2010, HIV incidence in eastern and southern Africa (ESA) has declined by nearly 60% (1). 

- 73 In 2024, 93% of people living with HIV in ESA were diagnosed, 91% of people diagnosed were 

- 74 on treatment, and 95% of people on treatment were virally suppressed (1). Despite these 

- 75 achievements, there were 490,000 new HIV infections, accounting for 37% of global incident 

76 

infections (1). In the context of declining incidence (2) and constrained international funding for 

77 HIV treatment and prevention (3), effectively reaching sexual networks where transmission 

78 persists is critical for achieving HIV epidemic control. 

79 

80 However, identifying these networks remains challenging in historically generalized epidemics 81 that are now characterized by low incidence. Some predict that as epidemics decline, incidence 82 will increasingly cluster in key populations at elevated HIV risk (e.g., female sex workers) (4,5). 83 However, data from across ESA suggest that a minority of new infections occur in such high-risk 84 populations (1,6,7), with no notable shifts over the past decade (6,8). Moreover, phylogenetic 

85 

studies depict a diffuse epidemic with few large transmission clusters (9–11), and conflict as to 

86 

whether high-HIV-prevalence groups and geographic regions are sources or sinks of 

87 transmission (12–14). Taken together, these findings suggest that transmission may remain 88 broadly distributed across populations. 

89 

90 Uncertainty about the structure of ongoing transmission in declining, low-incidence epidemics 

91 

partly reflects limitations of prior study designs. Most prior studies were conducted before 

- 92 widespread ART scale-up and incidence declines, and rarely integrated key populations with the 

- 93 broader community. Moreover, population-based cohorts often underrepresent individuals most 

- 94 likely to sustain transmission because they are harder to reach and less engaged in care (15–

The copyright holder for this preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted medRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. 

- 95 17). Although assisted partner notification (APN) programs aim to capture transmission 

- 96 networks of newly diagnosed people (18), they typically lack well-matched controls and are 

- 97 subject to selection bias, limiting inference about network-level risk factors and population-level 

- 98 transmission dynamics. As a result, the extent to which transmission remains generalized 

- 99 versus concentrated within specific networks remains unclear. 

- 100 

- 101 Here, we conducted a matched, case-control and contact-tracing study nested within a 

- 102 longitudinal, population-representative cohort in southern Uganda: a setting with high levels of 

- 103 viral suppression and declining HIV incidence (11,15,19), representative of many later-stage 

- 104 African epidemics (2). We enrolled sexual partners of population-representative incident HIV 

- 105 cases and HIV-negative controls, and characterized the prevalence of HIV, viremia, sex work, 

- 106 and intervention uptake across these networks to inform efficient implementation of 

- 107 interventions, like long-acting prevention.

# 109 **METHODS** 

- 110 **Parent Study Description** 

- 111 This matched case-control study was nested within rounds 20 (January 2021-March 2023) and 112 21 (April 2023-June 2025) of the Rakai Community Cohort Study (RCCS), a population-based 

- 113 cohort of all persons aged ≥15 years in 34 communities in southern Uganda, described in the 

- 114 Supplemental Methods and elsewhere (15,20). All participants were provided HIV services 

- 115 according to national guidelines and linked to follow-up care with the regional HIV control 

- 116 program (Supplemental Methods).

# **RCCS Laboratory Methods** 

- 119 HIV serostatus was assessed using a validated rapid testing algorithm with confirmation by 

- 120 ELISA (21). Viral load testing was performed using the Abbott M2000rt (Abbott Laboratories, 

- 121 Abbott Park, IL) and HIV serologic recency testing was done using the Asante HIV-1 Rapid 

- 122 Recency assay (Sedia Biosciences Corporation, Portland, OR). Participants with HIV were 

- 123 considered virally suppressed with <200 copies per mL and viremic with ≥200 copies.

- 131 with a self-reported last-negative HIV test result in the past year. HIV-negative controls, enrolled

# 135 **Partner and Venue Reporting** 

- 136 Sexual partners in the previous 24 months were reported by cases and controls, and enrollment 137 was attempted for all partners with sufficient contact information. Many FSW partners were 

- 138 untraceable, as participants often reported venue-based encounters without identifying details. 

- 139 In contrast to many assisted partner notification studies, which report tracing success among 

- 140 partners with contact information, our estimates include all reported partners. The study protocol 

- 141 was modified in May 2022 to include reporting of FSW venues in response to frequent reporting 

- 142 of untraceable FSW partners. Managers at reported venues provided a list of FSWs present on 

- 143 the same dates as the index participant. From that list, consenting FSWs were enrolled. All 

- 144 enrolled sexual partners and venue-based FSWs were counseled and tested for HIV and 

- 145 referred for appropriate HIV services, and venues were reported to the regional HIV program.

# **Statistical Analysis** 

- 194 We compared socio-demographic, mobility, and sexual behavioral characteristics of cases and 

- 195 controls and their enrolled sexual partners, stratified by sex. We assessed the characteristics of 

- 196 contact-tracing partner networks and estimated the prevalence of HIV, HIV viremia, and the

# 328 **DISCUSSION** 

- 329 In this population-representative study of incident HIV in a high-prevalence, low-incidence 

- 330 African epidemic (2,11,15,19), male incident cases had substantially larger sexual networks 

- 331 than female cases or controls of either sex. Compared to male controls, expanded male case 

- 332 networks were driven primarily by partnerships with FSWs, and having a FSW partner was the 

- 333 strongest overall predictor of incident infection, with more than four in ten male cases linked to 

- 334 FSWs. Most FSW partners were untraceable, as men were often only able to identify FSW 

- 335 venues. HIV-negative partners of cases, regardless of sex, were rarely using PrEP, indicating 

- 336 that individuals at high risk remain insufficiently reached by prevention services. Together, these 

- 337 findings suggest that ongoing transmission is disproportionately concentrated within large male 

- 338 client networks characterized by low uptake of prevention interventions.

- 340 Notably, this concentration of cases in FSW networks did not manifest as tightly clustered local 

- 341 outbreaks. Cases rarely shared reported partners or FSW venues, and genetic analyses 

- 342 identified few additional linkages, consistent with a locally fragmented transmission structure. A 

- 343 similar lack of clustering has been noted in phylogenetic studies throughout ESA (9,24–28). This 

- 344 consistent finding may reflect the dynamic and episodic nature of sex work, as well as 

- 345 geographic mobility that diffuses transmission across broader networks (29). Consistent with

- 347 existing key population program venue lists, suggesting gaps in routine outreach. Incorporating 

- 348 systematic venue reporting into assisted partner notification programs for newly diagnosed men, 

- 349 which is not currently standard of care, could support targeted prevention efforts in areas of

- 351 Prior studies support a prominent role for male clients of FSWs and men more generally in 

- 352 population-level HIV transmission in African settings. For example, the Key-Pop model (30) 

- 353 estimated that eliminating transmissions during commercial and non-commercial sexual 

- 354 partnerships involving male clients would have reduced overall HIV infections by 6.9% and

- 356 disproportionately and increasingly driving new HIV infections as overall HIV incidence declines, 357 contributing to a widening female to male HIV incidence ratio across Africa (10,11). These prior 358 findings are aligned with our results showing an outsized role of key populations and their 

- 359 bridging networks.

361 Despite their central role in epidemic control, key populations have experienced substantial and 362 disproportionate reductions in HIV prevention funding since 2025, in the context of wider global 363 health financing cuts (31,32). Notably, male clients of FSWs are not recognized as a formal key 364 population in UNAIDS or PEPFAR guidance (33,34) and remain understudied as candidates for 365 long-acting injectable PrEP and other targeted prevention strategies (35). This is particularly 366 relevant given that African men have lower HIV diagnosis and treatment coverage levels (1,36) 367 and are more likely to remain persistently viremic than women (11,37,38). Overall, our findings 

- 368 suggest continued investment in prevention programs for FSWs, alongside targeted 

369 engagement of male clients, will be critical to maintain epidemic control.

380 This study has limitations. Our data were limited to southern Uganda, but HIV epidemiological 381 trends in the RCCS, including declining incidence (2), fragmented transmission structures (9), 382 and disproportionate onward male transmission (11), are consistent with many other late-stage 383 African epidemics (2,10). We were also unable to locate a proportion of reported partners, 384 particularly FSW partners. However, enrollment exceeded 85% across traceable partners 385 regardless of sex and study arm, comparing favorably with APN programs in the region (42–47). 386 Viral suppression was common at the time of sampling, and thus genetic linkages between 387 partners could often not be assessed. However, when genetic linkages could be evaluated, 388 viremic partners were almost always linked to the index. Our transmission models used 389 imputation to account for untraceable partners and allowed virally suppressed partners to be 390 considered as potential sources of infection, recognizing that transmission could have occurred 391 before viral suppression. However, inferred transmission pathways involving untraceable and 392 suppressed individuals should be interpreted with caution. We cannot rule out the possibility that 393 recall bias contributed to the observed association between self-reported FSW partners and HIV 394 incidence. However, if recall bias were the primary driver, one would expect similarly strong 395 associations for other self-reported sexual risk behaviors, including the number of non-sex396 worker partners, but these variables displayed low to modest associations with HIV incidence. 397 Finally, sex work client status was not collected for male partners of female index cases, 

- 398 precluding identification of male client partnerships as a correlate of female incident infection.
