## ABSTRACT

Few studies have sought to untangle the influence of social determinants and  pregnancy  on  adolescent  marriage  declines.  Using  longitudinal  data from  15-  to  17-year-old  girls  in  the  Rakai  Community  Cohort  Survey,  we assessed  how  education,  socio-economic  status,  orphanhood  and  preg­ nancy contributed to trends in adolescent marriage. We examined descrip­ tive  trends  and  logistic  regressions  of  the  associations  between  social determinants  and  adolescent  marriage  and  conducted  causal  mediation analysis  to  assess  the  extent  to  which  pregnancy  mediated  the  effect  of education on marriage. Between 1999 and 2018, adolescent marriages and pregnancies  dropped  substantially  (24-6%  and  28-8%).  Girls'  secondary schooling was strongly associated with lower marriage risk (aOR marriage = 0.09;  95%  CI = 0.07-0.12),  accounting  for  time.  Lower  pregnancy  rates partially  explained the effect of secondary schooling on lower adolescent marriage (aOR indirect effect = 0.55; 95% CI = 0.421-0.721). Findings affirm the  importance  of  education  in  preventing  adolescent  marriages  but  call attention to the role of pregnancies in influencing adolescent marriages.

The terms 'child marriage' and 'adolescent marriage' are both used to describe marriage before 18 years of age; however, in this paper we opt for the language of 'adolescent marriage'. The term 'child  marriage'  is  commonly  used  to  underline  the  human  rights  violations  that  early  marital practices have for under-age girls and boys under international and regional treaties and conven­ tions (Nour, 2009). While the term 'child marriage' has mobilized attention and solidarity towards the issue, this terminology homogenizes the issue of marriage as a problem affecting the 'girl child' with little  to  no  agency  in  the  marriage  decision-making  process.  More  specific  to  Uganda,  this  'child marriage' terminology can be problematic where marriage more commonly occurs during middle to late  adolescence  (15-19 years)  and  when  adolescents  may  or  may  not  exert  varying  degrees  of agency and consent in the marital decision-making process. To reflect this adolescent life stage, we intentionally use the terminology 'adolescent marriage' (as marriage before age 18) in this paper.

Despite significant global declines, limited information exists on how longitudinal social changes might  be  affecting  changes  in  adolescent  marriage.  Substantial  evidence  exists  about  the  crosssectional associations of social determinants - such as education, socio-economic status (SES) and orphanhood, among others - and adolescent marriage in different contexts (Bhan et al., 2019; Jain &amp; Kurz,  2007;  Kohno  et  al.,  2020;  Raj  et  al.,  2014).  In  recent  years,  researchers,  practitioners,  and policymakers  have  identified  several  research  gaps  that  require  longitudinal  approaches.  First, there is limited understanding of how social and structural factors - such as economic and educa­ tional improvements at a societal level - might be driving large-scale adolescent marriage declines. Second,  there  are  scant  analyses  that  have  adequately  teased  out  the  bidirectional  relationship between teenage pregnancy and marriage (Plesons et al., 2021), including whether teenage preg­ nancies contribute to adolescent marriages or whether adolescent marriages perpetuate teenage pregnancies.  In  this  paper,  we  bridge  this  gap  by  combining  descriptive  and  causal  inference methods to assess the extent that education, SES, orphanhood, and teenage pregnancies contrib­ uted to marriage declines among 15- to 17-year-old girls over time.

## Conceptual framing: social determinants, adolescent marriage and pregnancy

The drivers of adolescent marriage are complex, inter-related and context-dependent, yet universally sustained by social and gender inequities (Raj, 2010). Risk factors for adolescent marriage include structural conditions such as poverty and inadequate access to schooling and economic opportu­ nities, adverse family circumstances such as orphanhood, and social and gender norms about ideal marriage  age  (Chae,  2013;  Jain  &amp;  Kurz,  2007;  Mathur  et  al.,  2003).  Although  adolescent  marriage affects both boys and girls, adolescent marriage is a gendered pattern that adversely affects girls and young women. Often, young women enter marriage early due to limited educational and economic opportunities - and because of teenage pregnancy.

Adolescent pregnancy and adolescent marriage are closely associated and driven by similar social determinants.  Girls'  education  is  a  key  protective  factor  against  both  adolescent  marriage  and adolescent  pregnancy  (Mathur  et  al.,  2003;  Patton  et  al.,  2016).  Specifically,  access  to  education and higher educational attainment are consistently associated with later age at marriage (Jain &amp; Kurz, 2007;  Wodon et al.,  2017)  and  lower  rates  of  teenage  pregnancy  (Patton  et  al.,  2016).  In  Uganda, research suggests that girls are at high risk of dropping out during upper primary school and end of primary school examinations, due to parents' inability to pay for school fees and supplies, unwanted pregnancies,  and/or  arranged  marriages  (Ahaibwe  et  al.,  2018;  Bantebya  et  al.,  2014).  As  such, keeping girls in school, and particularly secondary school, is considered one of the most effective strategies to delay age at marriage and pregnancy for young women.

Girls'  education  is  widely  credited  as  one  of  the  most  important  protective  factors  against adolescent marriage across global research (Bantebya et al., 2014; Jain &amp; Kurz, 2007; Petroni et al., 2017; Wodon et al., 2017). Yet, there are various mechanisms underlying the relationship between education and adolescent marriage. In contexts where parents have strong decision-making powers over the marriage process, parents may choose to prioritize their daughter's education and delay her marriage until school completion. Other studies suggest that the agency of girls increases the longer they are in school, allowing them greater choice in the timing of their marriage (Jain &amp; Kurz, 2007). Relatedly, education influences girls' life aspirations, while giving them needed negotiation skills to navigate  and  choose  their  life  goals,  including  future  educational,  professional  and  partnership choices.

<!-- image -->

The relationship between education, pregnancy and/or marriage is bidirectional and mutuallyreinforcing.  Education  is  not  only  a  predictor  of  marriage  and  pregnancy  outcomes  for  adoles­ cent  girls  but  education  (and  in  particular  school  drop-out)  is  an  outcome  of  marriage  and/or pregnancy  experiences.  In  Uganda,  an  early  and  unwanted  pregnancy  curtails  girls'  future  life aspirations, including their education goals (Bantebya et al., 2014; Stoebenau, 2015; Wodon et al., 2016).  As  of  2016,  research  showed  that  pregnancies  accounted  for  about  22%  of  drop-outs among girls 14-18 years old; at the same time, only about 8% of pregnant girls who dropped out of  school  were  given  a  chance  to  re-enrol  in  school  (Uganda  Ministry  of  Education  and  Sports MoES, 2016).  Although  government  guidelines  were  recently  developed  to  encourage  pregnant girls  to  stay  in  school  (MoES,  2020);  in  reality,  pregnant  girls  often  drop  out  of  school  due  to social  stigma  and  school-specific  policies  that  ban  pregnant  girls  from  returning  to  school (Spindler,  2022).

The  relationship  between  education  and  adolescent  marriage  may  also  be  confounded  or mediated by other social factors. Economic constraints, and in particular lack of economic opportu­ nities, may drive young girls out of school and into adolescent marriages. In Uganda, for example, girls from poorer households are more likely to drop out of school due to financial constraints. After leaving school, girls are then faced with the choice of either staying and working in their childhood home or enter a marital union. With the lack of viable work options, girls from poorer households are thus faced with an 'either/or' scenario between marriage or staying in their childhood home (Wodon et al.,  2017). In this situation, girls and their families may choose marriage as an avenue to ensure girls'  future  financial  and  social  security  (Lee-Rife  et  al.,  2012).  Lastly,  marriage  may  be  used  by families  as  a  social  protection  mechanism  -  and  in  particular,  to  shield  girls  from  social  sanctions arising from a non-marital pregnancy (Bantebya et al., 2014; Greene &amp; Stiefvater, 2019).

## Social determinants and adolescent marriages in Uganda

At  the  national  level,  Uganda  has  experienced  significant  economic  growth  and  implemented policies that have contributed to young people's improved educational, marital and life outcomes, particularly for girls. Uganda experienced significant poverty reduction, with the proportion of those living in poverty declining from 56% in 1992 to 21% in 2017 (UBOS &amp; Macro International Inc., 2018). Although Uganda's economy has significantly grown over the last two decades, the labour market remains insecure for both young men and women, who make up a large portion of the population. The youth unemployment rate among 15 to 24-year olds has remained high, ranging from 5.3% in 2000 to 4.5% in 2023, and higher than the total labour force (World Bank, 2024).

At the same time, marriage under 18 years among 20- to 24-year-old women dropped from 46% to 34% in just 10 years, from 2006 to 2016 (Uganda Bureau of Statistics (UBOS) &amp; Macro International Inc., 2007; 2018). In 1995, the legal age at marriage was raised to 18 years for men and women under the Ugandan constitution, although exceptions were allowed with parental consent. Under Uganda's Children Act Amendment, the minimum legal age of marriage was subsequently set to 18 years for both boys and girls with no exceptions or parental consent (2016).

In  1997,  the  Ugandan  government  implemented  a  universal  primary  education  (UPE)  policy, followed by universal secondary education (USE) in 2007. Multiple studies have demonstrated the positive  impact  of  the  UPE  policy  for  Ugandan  girls,  including  increased  years  of  education  and delayed age at marriage (Deininger, 2003; Kan &amp; Klasen, 2020; Koski et al., 2018; Moussa &amp; Omoeva,

<!-- image -->

2020;  Nishimura  et  al.,  2008).  Yet  there  have  also  been  inadvertent  consequences  from  UPE's compulsory educational requirements, including lowered education quality due to higher student enrolments (Deininger, 2003), and an increased economic burden for families due to the increasing schooling-related expenses and costs (Moore et al., 2023).

Sexuality  education  efforts  within  schools  in  Uganda  have  long  been  challenged  by  religious organizations and leaders, who simultaneously hold power and influence within policy circles and also own many of Uganda's private boarding schools. As adolescent marriage efforts advanced in 2018,  a  new  comprehensive  national  sexuality  education  framework  failed  in  parliament  due  to resistance of religious leaders, cultural leaders, and community parent groups (Moore et al., 2021; Spindler, 2022). Consistent with this, related research has shown that the lack of sexuality education in  Uganda  poses  barriers  to  'ending'  adolescent  marriages,  given  the  close  relationship  between adolescent sex, premarital pregnancies, and marriages in Uganda (Spindler, 2022).

Rakai,  in  southcentral  Uganda,  provides  a  unique  setting  to  explore  the  relationship  between improvements  in  social  determinants  and  pregnancy  and  marriage  declines.  Rakai  has  been  the centre of HIV research, treatment, and prevention efforts for the last 30 years, following the outbreak of the HIV epidemic in the region in the late 1980s. Over the last 30 years, the region experienced increased  foreign  donor  and  NGO  investments  in  response  to  the  HIV  epidemic,  infrastructure investment of schools and health centres, and diversified livelihoods and economic opportunities (Cummiskey, 2020; Smith et al., 2001).

Research in Rakai shows rising household asset wealth, increasing school enrolment, decreased orphanhood and improving adolescent sexual and reproductive health (SRH) outcomes, including significant declines in adolescent marriages and pregnancies (J. Santelli et al., 2019; J. S. Santelli et al., 2022).  At  the  national  level,  orphanhood  has  been  associated  with  increased  risk  of  adolescent marriage in Uganda (Chae, 2013); in Rakai, orphanhood has drastically declined primarily due to the rollout of HIV combination prevention interventions in the mid-2000s (Santelli et al., 2022). As well, emerging research from Rakai shows that girls' age at first sex increased drastically between 1997 and 2013; yet the time interval (in years) from girls' age at first sex to their first pregnancy decreased, as well as the interval from age at first sex to their first marriage (Hoffman et al., 2024).

In  this  paper,  we  untangle  the  influence  of  social  determinants  and  pregnancy  on  adolescent marriage declines in southcentral Uganda. We conducted two separate but complementary analyses. We first used repeat cross-sectional data from 15- to 17-year-old girls between 1999 and 2018 to assess the extent to which social determinants - including education, SES, and orphanhood - as well as  adolescent  pregnancy,  were  associated  with  adolescent  marriage.  This  repeat  cross-sectional dataset provided a population snapshot of time trends, as well as associations between our variables of  interest  and  adolescent  marriage.  From  the  repeat  cross-sectional  data,  we  then  constructed a  panel  dataset  of  girls  who  were  consistently  followed  over  time  to  conduct  a  causal  mediation analysis examining the relationships between education, adolescent pregnancy and the adolescent marriage outcome. This panel dataset provided an individual-level examination of girls' behaviours over time, enabling us to isolate and characterize the sequencing between education, pregnancy, and marriage events among girls who had at least two rounds of survey data. We hypothesized that (1)  At  the  population  level,  higher  education,  higher  SES,  and  lower  rates  of  orphanhood  were associated with lower risk of marriage accounting for changes over time and (2) At the individual level,  adolescent  pregnancies  partially  explained  the  protective  effect  of  girls'  higher  education levels on their lower risk of marriage while accounting for time.

## Methods

## Data source and population

The Rakai region is home to the Rakai Health Sciences Program (RHSP) and the RCCS, a populationbased, open-cohort survey that has collected demographic and SRH-related data from participating households since 1994. Since 1994, RCCS has conducted 18 survey rounds with ~14,000 women and men between 15 and 49 years,  every  12-18 months.  RCCS  includes  approximately  6,500  to  9,500 adolescents and young adults per study round. As an open cohort, newly age-eligible 15-year-olds and recent in-migrants are enrolled at each round. For this analysis, data were drawn from 13 surveys (1999-2018) of 30 continuously followed RCCS communities.

<!-- image -->

Each RCCS survey round includes a household census, with data provided by the head of house­ hold. Two to four weeks after the census, consenting residents are interviewed and asked to provide blood  for  HIV  and  STI  testing.  RCCS  participation  rates  are ~95%  of  those  present  at  the  time  of survey;  ~25%  of  censused  residents  are  absent  at  each  round  -  for  work,  school  or  other  travel (Grabowski et al., 2017). Eligible individuals (age 15-49 years) are interviewed about SRH and HIVrelated  topics  and  offered  HIV  counselling  and  testing  on  the  day  of  interview.  Data  collection  is monitored by field supervisors and quality control staff to ensure data integrity.

## Analytic sample for association analysis (using repeat cross-sectional data)

We included girls 15-17 years old who participated in any of the 13 survey rounds from 1999 to 2018 to examine trends and associations between our variables of interest. Across the 13 survey rounds, the  analytical  sample  included  6,998  observations  from  5,169  girls  aged  15-17 years  across  30 continuously followed communities.

## Analytic sample for causal mediation analysis (using panel data)

For  the  causal  mediation  analyses  examining  pregnancy  as  a  mediator  between  education  and marriage, we constructed a panel dataset from observations of 15- to 17-year-old girls who were consecutively  followed  across  multiple  survey  rounds.  The  use  of  panel  data  from  repeat  crosssectional datasets is a common analytic approach used to complement macro-level analyses with more 'micro' individual-level data (Harpham et al., 2003); for these causal mediation analysis, using panel data from girls followed over time allowed us to isolate the temporality between potential pregnancy and marriage events (whether a pregnancy and/or marriage occurred before or after one another). Due to RCCS' open cohort design, adolescents typically stay in the cohort between one and three survey rounds. Of the 5,169 girls who participated in RCCS between 1999 and 2018, approxi­ mately  77%  participated  in  one  survey  round  only,  21%  in  two  rounds,  2%  in  three  rounds.  To construct the panel sample, we included 1,104 girls who had two to three rounds of survey data between 1999 and 2018 (after removing observations with missing pregnancy data).

## Measures

## Marriage

Adolescent marriage was constructed from self-reports of ever or current marriage at each survey round. We used self-reports of marriage from 15-17-year-old girls, rather than self-reported age at first  marriage  from  20-24-year-old  women,  which  is  a  more  common  measure  of  child  marriage (UNICEF, 2024). Examining age at first marriage among 20- to 24-year-olds was not possible since age at first marriage was not consistently collected and measured at each survey round.

## Pregnancy

Adolescent  pregnancy  was  measured  from  self-reports  of  ever  or  current  pregnancy  among  girls between  15  and  17 years.  Ever  or  current  pregnancy  was  not  measured  during  survey  round  17 (2015-2016);  as  a  result,  this  survey  round  is  excluded  from  multivariable  models.  For  the  causal mediation analyses, we constructed a pregnancy mediator that excluded any pregnancy events that occurred  after  marriage  (rather  than  before  marriage).  This  pre-marriage  pregnancy  variable excluded any pregnancy events that occurred at the same survey round as marriage, a pregnancy

## Social determinants

Social  determinants  included  education  level,  a  household  assets-based  measure  of  SES  and orphanhood status (death of one or both biological parents). Girls' educational level is measured categorically  according  to  the  highest  schooling  level  reported  by  respondents  at  each  survey round  across  three  categories  of  schooling  level:  1.  Some  lower  primary  schooling  or  lower;  2. Some  upper  primary  schooling,  and  3.  Some  lower  secondary  schooling  or  higher.  SES  was measured  as  a  categorical  variable  (low,  low-medium,  high-medium,  high  quartiles),  using  an asset-based measure of nine household assets (e.g. radio) and modern construction (e.g. metal or tile  roof)  (J.  S.  Santelli  et  al.,  2021).  Orphanhood  status  was  reported  by  the  head  of  household and  measured  as  a  categorical  variable  (not  an  orphan,  maternal  orphan,  paternal  orphan,  and double orphan).

## Time

Time  was  measured  as  a  continuous  variable  corresponding  to  the  13  survey  rounds  (rounds 6-18).

## Other variables

In  all  analyses,  we  included  age  at  first  interview  and  type  of  community  as  confounders. Community type is  measured  as  either  a  rural  or  peri-urban  community.  Other  variables  exam­ ined in the descriptive analyses include religion (nominal variable), ever had sex (yes/no), and age at first sex. Age at first sex was constructed as a categorical variable from two variables: 1. Never/ ever  had  sex;  2.  Age  at  first  sex  (among  those  who  ever  had  sex)  and  included  the  following levels: never had sex, 12-14 years old at first sexual intercourse and 15-17 years old at first sexual intercourse.

## Trend analyses

Using  the  repeat  cross-sectional  dataset,  we  examined  the  prevalence  of  adolescent  marriage, adolescent pregnancy, social determinants and other characteristics among 15 to 17-year-old  girls at  each  survey  round.  To  assess  the  significance  of  observed  changes  over  time,  we  ran  separate models with generalized  estimating  equations  (GEE),  regressing  each  characteristic  of  interest  on time.

## Association analyses

Using  the  repeat  cross-sectional  dataset,  we  then  employed  both  cross-tabulations  and  logistic regressions  to  understand  the  associations  between  1.  Each  social  determinant  (education,  SES, and orphanhood) and the adolescent marriage outcome, 2. Adolescent pregnancy and adolescent marriage,  3.  Other  demographic  characteristics  (age,  type  of  community,  religion,  age  at  sexual debut) and adolescent marriage. We adjusted for age and time in each model to determine whether the  above  associations  were  still  present  accounting  for  changes  over  time  and  age  structure. Variables  that  were  either  theoretically  and/or  statistically  associated  with  adolescent  marriage below  the p = 0.20  threshold  in  these  models  were  then  included  in  a  final  multivariable  logistic model. This final model assessed the conditional relationship of the different social determinants to adolescent marriage, adjusting for adolescent age, community type and time (in one multivariable model).  All  models  were  estimated  through  GEE  with  exchangeable  correlations  to  account  for within-subject correlations.

<!-- image -->

Adolescent pregnancy was not included as a predictor in the final multivariable model given its strong association with adolescent marriage. However, we used conditional probability tabulations to assess the likelihood of adolescent marriage given a previous or current pregnancy at each survey round.

## Causal mediation analyses

Subsequently,  we  employed  a  causal  mediation  framework  (T.  VanderWeele,  2015),  to  assess  the underlying mechanism between education, pregnancy, and marriage using longitudinal data from 1,183 girls. Figure 1 shows the hypothesized relationship between our exposure of interest (educa­ tion),  mediator (pregnancy), and marriage (outcome), as well as potential confounders. The green arrow represents the direct effect of education on marriage. Above, the orange arrow represents the indirect  effect of  education  levels  on  marriage  that  works  through  lower  rates  of  adolescent pregnancies.  The total  effect of  higher  education  and  lower  pregnancy  rates  on  marriage  risk  is reflected by combining both the green arrow (direct effect) and orange arrows (indirect effect).

The causal mediation analysis was conducted through a two-stage process. In the first stage, we tested  our  assumptions  about  the  presence  of  associations  between  our  exposure,  mediator  and outcome by running four separate logistic regression models: 1. The total effect of the education exposure on the marriage outcome not adjusting for pregnancy; 2. The direct effect of education exposure on the marriage outcome adjusting for pregnancy; 3. the effect of education exposure on pregnancy  as  an  outcome,  and  4.  the  direct  effect  of  the  education  exposure  on  the  marriage outcome, allowing for  a  pregnancy  and  education  exposure  interaction.  All  four  of  these  models adjusted for confounders in Figure 1 (time, age, SES, and type of community).

In  the  second  stage,  we  isolated  the  indirect  effect  of  pregnancy  on  the  relationship  between education and marriage, decomposing the non-linear regression estimates into four separate effects: 1. the controlled direct effect of education on marriage, 2. the natural direct effect of education on marriage  ('natural'  effects  allow  for  education  and  pregnancy  interaction);  3.  the  natural  indirect effect of education on marriage that works through pregnancy; and 4. total effect of education on marriage (the sum of education and pregnancy on marriage). We used 'Paramed' package in Stata 17 (Emsley &amp; Liu, 2013). A more detailed explanation of causal mediation assumptions, processes, and analyses can be found in the Supplemental Appendix 2.

Baseline  values  from  the  education  exposure  and  confounder  variables  were  included  in  all models (that is, the first time measurement of education, SES, community, age, and survey round for each girl in the dataset). We used generalized linear modelling (GLM) for all models. In our results, we only present the natural direct, natural indirect, and total effects. ## Ethical procedures

Both  written  minor  assent  and  parental/guardian  permission  were  obtained  for  unemancipated minors (&lt;18 years) participating in the RCCS; 18+ year-olds and emancipated minors provide their own written informed consent. This study has been approved by the Uganda Virus Research Institute Research  and  Ethics  Committee  (UVRI-REC;  Federal  Wide  Assurance  [FWA]  No.00001354)  and  the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (UNCST), and Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the Columbia University Medical Center in the United States.

## Results

## Changes in social determinants, pregnancy, and marriage between 1999 and 2018

Table  1  details  the  changes  in  adolescent  marriage,  pregnancy,  social  determinants,  and  other sociodemographic  characteristics  using  cross-sectional  data.  Between  1999  and  2018,  marriage and  pregnancy  among  15-  to  17-year-old  girls  declined  in  a  parallel  trend  from  24%  to  6%,  and 28% to 8%, respectively. The proportion of girls who ever had sex also declined substantially from 62% in 1999 to just 23% in 2018, consistent with declining adolescent pregnancy rates. All changes in pregnancy, marriage, sex initiation and social determinants over time were statistically significant (p-value for trend &lt; 0.0001).

Figure 2 visualizes the striking social and adolescent marriage changes over this time period - household SES rose over time, as did secondary schooling levels, which rose from 35% in 1999 to 54% in 2018. Rates of orphanhood were high and unchanged (&gt;50%) before 2004, then declined steadily through 2018 (21%) following the rollout of HIV treatment in the region in 2004. Over the same time period, both adolescent marriage and pregnancy declined in a parallel downward trend.

To better understand the relationship between pregnancy and marriage over time, we assessed the conditional probability of marriage given a current or prior pregnancy among 15- to 17-year-old girls  at  each  survey  round.  As  shown  in  Figure  3,  the  probability  of  marriage  given  a  current  or previous pregnancy remained high (68-52%) and steady over time. These data suggest that while adolescent marriage may have declined over time, there is a high and consistent risk of a marriage if previously or currently pregnant, and this relationship has remained consistent over time.

## Cross-sectional associations between social determinants and adolescent marriage

Table 2 shows the independent associations between different socio-demographic predictors and the adolescent marriage outcome; these separate models were used to build our final multivariable model  (in  Table  3).  The  logistic  regression  models  in  Table  2  are  adjusted  for  time  and  age  to understand whether associations were still present accounting for changing age structure and time. The results confirm a strikingly close relationship between pregnancy and marriage, adjusting for age and time (aOR = 39.59; 95% CI: 31.12; 50.36). Education was strongly associated with adolescent marriage;  girls  with  lower  secondary  or  higher  education  had  91%  lower  odds  of  being  married relative to girls with lower primary level or no schooling, adjusting for age and time (aOR = 0.09; 95% CI: 0.07; 0.12). Girls from high SES households also had 41% lower odds of being married than girls from low SES households, adjusting for age and time (aOR = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.47; 0.75). The association between  orphanhood  and  marriage  varied;  girls  whose  parents  were  both  alive  had  marginally higher odds of being married compared to girls who lost both parents (aOR = 1.08; 95% CI: 0.83; 1.40) and compared to those who lost a father (aOR = 1.12; 95% CI: 0.84; 1.49), adjusting for age and time. However, girls whose mother was deceased had higher odds of being married relative to girls who had lost both parents, adjusting for age and time (aOR = 1.49; 95% CI: 1.05; 2.11).

## The causal relationship between education, pregnancy and marriage

In  the  first  stage  of  the  causal  mediation  analyses,  we  found  strong  associations  between education,  pregnancy,  and  marriage,  consistent  with  causal  mediation  assumptions  (see Supplemental  Appendix  4  for  first-stage  regression  results).  Table  4  presents  the  second-stage mediation results, which shows total and direct effects, as well as the indirect  effect of education on marriage that is mediated through pregnancy. To simplify interpretation, the controlled direct effect is not shown given that its effect size was almost identical as the effect size of the natural direct  effect  (suggesting  minimal  interaction  between  the  education  exposure  and  pregnancy mediator).  The  results  confirm  that  there  is  a  strong  and  significant  direct  relationship  between higher  education  levels  and  lower  odds  of  marriage  (direct  effect  aOR:  0.28;  95%  CI:  0.11-0.61), but  that  pregnancy  partially  mediates  this  relationship  (indirect  effect  aOR:  0.55;  95%  CI:  0.420.72).  Overall,  the  results  show  that  pregnancy  mediates  approximately  31.5%  of  the  effect  of higher  education  on  lower  risk  of  marriage  (using  the  risk  ratio  scale).  Sensitivity  analyses indicate that the mediation findings are robust to potential unmeasured confounding (see results in  Supplemental  Appendix  5).

## Discussion

Using  close  to  20 years  of  data,  this  study  provides  key  insights  into  the  drivers  of  adolescent marriage among girls in southcentral Uganda. The findings show that education is a strong deter­ minant of lower marriage risk, although the influence of more upstream determinants such as SES should not be discarded. Between 1999 and 2018 in Rakai, Uganda, expectations of marriage for young women following a premarital  pregnancy  remained  relatively  high  and  unchanged.  Using a causal mediation framework, we found that lower pregnancy rates partially explained the relation­ ship between higher education and lower risk of adolescent marriage, but that the direct effect of education on marriage also remained important. Taken together, these findings show that education continues to be a protective factor in preventing adolescent marriage. Yet, lower pregnancies rates may play an important role in explaining the protective effect of education on adolescent marriages, and in contributing to adolescent marriage trends over time.

First, we found sharp declines in adolescent marriage in southcentral Uganda, in line with global and regional downward declines in adolescent marriage. The trends from our study in Uganda are consistent with global evidence that structural drivers like educational and economic improvements may be driving declining trends (UNICEF, 2021). In particular, we found that higher education levels significantly  predicted  lower  marriage  rates,  even  when  accounting  for  time.  The  strong  effect  of education  on  marriage  corroborates  existing  evidence  that  continuing  girls'  education  -  and particularly  secondary  education  -  is  effective  in  delaying  marriage  globally  (Jain  &amp;  Kurz,  2007; Mathur et al., 2003; Patton et al., 2016) and in Uganda (Koski et al., 2018; Wodon et al., 2016). In the context of Uganda, the significant contribution of education is consistent with other national quasiexperimental  studies  showing  that  UPE  implementation  in  1997  -  and  to  a  lesser  extent  USE  in 2007 - boosted school enrolment (Deininger, 2003), reduced adolescent marriage (Koski et al., 2018), and fertility desires among young women (Behrman, 2015).

At  the  same  time,  our  findings  caution  against  relying  on  educational  investments  as  a  standalone  solution  to  'ending'  adolescent  marriages.  Specifically,  we  found  that  lower  adolescent pregnancies  played  an  important  role  in  explaining  the  relationship  between  higher  education levels and lower rates of marriage. These findings are in line with calls from researchers and activists at multiple regional levels - globally, in East Africa and Uganda - to increase efforts and resources towards the role of pregnancy and sexuality-related stigma in perpetuating adolescent marriages (Bantebya et al., 2014; CEFMU and Sexuality Working Group, 2022). Consistent with their calls, our findings emphasize the importance of framing adolescent marriage as not just a child protection issue, but one related to adolescent pregnancy and sexuality.

Our paper advances research and measurement of adolescent marriage in several ways. Of use for future studies, we provide an example of a causal mediation approach that unpacks the relationship between education, pregnancy and marriage, consistent with calls to tease out the complex relation­ ship between adolescent marriage and pregnancy (Plesons et al., 2021). Building from our analysis, future  research  could  replicate  this  approach  with  other  datasets  to  assess  whether  the  causal relationship between education, pregnancy and marriage holds across other settings. As well, future research could explore alternate causal pathways between education and marriage, and, in parti­ cular, the role of alternative employment and skills training opportunities following schooling that might help to delay age at marriage.

As  more  funding  and  resources  continue  to  be  invested  towards  adolescent  marriage  pro­ grammes  globally,  additional  national  and  sub-regional  analyses  are  needed  to  examine  and compare large-scale changes and drivers of adolescent marriage across different global and regional contexts. In bridging this gap, we provide a methodological approach to examine how social and structural  factors  -  such  as  economic  and  educational  improvements  -  are  driving  large-scale adolescent marriage behaviours, which could be adapted to other population-based cohorts and studies.

Of  use  for  activists  and  policymakers,  we  provide  longitudinal  evidence  that  can  be  used  to advocate  for,  and  invest  in  policies  and  programmes  that  focus  both  on  education  and pregnancy  to  prevent  adolescent  marriages,  particularly  in  the  context  of  Uganda.  On  the one  hand,  governments  and  funders  should  continue  investing  in  larger-scale  education  and socio-economic policies and programmes to delay girls' age at marriage. Yet to end adolescent marriage  in  Uganda  as  per  Sustainable  Development  Goal  (SDG)  Target  5.3,  stronger  invest­ ments  towards  adolescent  pregnancy  prevention  are  especially  warranted,  including  pro­ grammes  and  policies  that  address  norms  and  issues  related  to  sex,  contraception,  and sexuality  among  girls  and  boys.  The  role  of  sexuality  education  in  Uganda  has  long  been  at the  centre  of  controversy  (Moore  et  al.,  2021),  yet  our  findings  point  to  an  urgent  need  to better  integrate  sexuality  education  and  pregnancy  prevention  efforts  in  adolescent  marriage policies  and  programmes.

## Study strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is the use of data that span 19 years, enabling the examination of both changes and associations between social determinants, marriage, and pregnancy behaviours at population  and  individual  levels.  On  the  one  hand,  the  association  analyses  are  useful  in  under­ standing  general  trends  and  relationships  over  time  but  are  limited  for  making  causal  inferences from the data; on the other hand, the causal mediation analyses enable us to assess causal relation­ ships, but they also introduce bias as the analysis is restricted to individuals who were followed-up in the cohort and to those who had clear temporal pregnancy and marriage data. Specifically, potential loss  to  follow-up  might  have  affected  the  representativeness  of  adolescent  girls  who  were  in  our panel  sample  -  biasing  the  panel  sample  towards  adolescent  girls  who  may  have  different  char­ acteristics and behaviours. For example, there may be loss to follow-up due to out-migration, given that in- and out-migration is high in the region and the RCCS (Grabowski et al., 2020). In line with this, research in the region shows that some newly married women, particularly between the ages of 15 and 19 years old, move to a different community to form a new household with a marital partner (Schuyler et al., 2017). As a result, our panel data might have omitted adolescent girls who dropped out of the RCCS to marry in a different community.

These data also provide marriage and SRH information directly from 15- to 17-year-old girls, rather than  retrospectively  from  women  20  to  24 years  old  (as  typically  done  in  adolescent  marriage population  studies).  The  use  of  data  from  15-  to  17-year-old  girls  allows  us  to  directly  measure their  childhood  exposures  and  social  determinants,  rather  than  exposures  during  adulthood  as measured with 20- to 24-year-old women. Although 15- to 17-year-old girls are less prone to recall bias  than  20-  to  24-year-old  girls,  they  may  be  more  likely  to  under-report  a  marriage  given  the sexuality  education  bans  in  schools,  as  well  as  Uganda's  legal  reforms  around  the  age  of  sexual consent in 2007. The latter legal reforms criminalized sexual relations when one of the partners is a  minor,  generating  substantial  public  attention  to  the  issue  of  adolescent  sex,  pregnancy  and marriage  in  Uganda  (Parikh,  2012).  Thus,  the  increased  public  awareness  of  sexual  defilement laws  -  and  its  serious  repercussions  -  could  have  contributed  to  underreporting  of  underage marriages over time.

The adolescent marriage measure used in this analysis does not reflect the conventional method for measuring adolescent marriage prevalence in population surveys, limiting the comparability of these prevalence data to population studies on adolescent marriage. Specifically, data from 15- to 17-yearold  girls  are  right-censored  -  they  do  not  capture  girls  who  will  experience  an  adolescent  marriage event after their interview. For example, these data might include an observation for an unmarried girl who is interviewed once at 15 years old but does not capture whether that girl will marry at 16 years or 17 years  old  if  she  does  not  return  for  an  interview.  As  a  result,  these  data  likely  under-estimate adolescent marriage prevalence in the Rakai region. Lastly, we recognize that our analysis is limited to young  women  and  girls  but  that  pregnancy  and  marriage  is  a  gendered  issue  that  involves  both women  and  men.  Additional  quantitative  data  from  boys  and  men  would  have  strengthened  this paper, but we were limited by the lack of pregnancy-related data for partners of men in the dataset. Future quantitative and qualitative measurement approaches should be improved to include boys and men's experiences of pregnancy and marriage to bridge these gaps.

<!-- image -->

Using  close  to  20 years'  worth  of  data,  this  paper  provides  new  insights  into  the  role  of social  determinants  and  teenage  pregnancies  in  driving  changes  in  adolescent  marriage  in the  East  Africa  context.  These  findings  affirm  the  importance  of  education  in  preventing adolescent  marriage  in  Uganda  while  also  calling  attention  to  the  role  of  adolescent  preg­ nancies  in  contributing  to  adolescent  marriage  declines.  To  delay  adolescent  marriage altogether,  funding  efforts  need  to  shift  towards  adolescent  pregnancy  prevention,  as  well as  the  structural  and  social  drivers  of  adolescent  pregnancy  and  marriage  in  East  African contexts  like  Uganda.
