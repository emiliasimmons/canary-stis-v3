## Dissolution of transactional sex relationships during COVID-19: A qualitative study of Ugandan men's experiences during COVID-19 lockdowns

Holly M. Nishimura a,* , Neema Nakyanjo b , William Ddaaki b , Anne C. Kiyingi b , Emmanuel Mukwana b , Fred Nalugoda b , Charles Ssekyewa b , Julie Denison a , M. Kate Grabowski c , Joseph Kagaayi b , Caitlin E. Kennedy a

b Rakai Health Sciences Program, Kalisizo, Uganda

c Department of Pathology, Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine, Baltimore, MD, USA

## Abstract

The COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent mitigation measures led to social disruption and negative economic shocks for a large proportion of Uganda's population. The social and economic consequences of COVID-19 on Ugandan men's sexual behaviours, including transactional sex relationships, are unclear. We conducted in-depth interviews between November 2021-February 2022 with 26 men in a high HIV prevalence region of Uganda. Data were analysed thematically to understand how sexual relationships, including transactional sex, were impacted by COVID-19. We found that COVID-19 mitigation had far-reaching social and economic impacts on most respondents, particularly those employed in the informal economy. Men described experiencing job loss, food insecurity and restricted mobility, which limited opportunities to provide for and meet with transactional sex partners. Inability to provide financial resources meant that men could not form new transactional sex relationships and men who could no longer provide for their existing transactional sex partners consistently reported relationship dissolution. Men who reported stable employment during the pandemic described few changes in transactional sex relationships. Similarly, men in non-transactional relationships did not report relationship dissolution despite decreased financial provision. Further research should assess the potential short- and long-term impacts of COVID-19 mitigation measures on transactional sex relationships.

## Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic and associated mitigation measures have had far-reaching impacts on the health and economic well-being of populations globally (World Bank 2020). In response to the growing number of COVID-19 cases internationally, the Ugandan government began to proactively implement mitigation measures prior to the country's first documented COVID-19 case on March 22, 2020. These measures included the suspension of public gatherings and a 14-day mandatory quarantine for travellers entering the country. On March 30, 2020, the government implemented its most restrictive 'lockdown' policies including country-wide curfews, stay-at-home-orders, restricted local travel, and the closure of schools (Ministry of Health Uganda 2022).

A timeline showing Uganda's COVID-19 restrictions and key events is presented in Figure 1. Uganda's first lockdown ended in mid-May 2020 and a second lockdown was implemented in response to a surge of COVID-19 cases in June 2021. Both lockdowns resulted in a sharp contraction of economic activity which disproportionately impacted different economic sectors. Employment rates during the first lockdown were at 71% compared to 87% in March 2020 and work stoppages were concentrated in the commerce and personal services sectors (Atamanov et al. 2022). During the second lockdown, employment was at 81% with more than half of the work stoppages occurring in the agricultural sector. Following Uganda's second lockdown, food insecurity increased dramatically, with more than half of households nationally reporting moderate or severe food insecurity (Atamanov et al. 2022). Uganda also closed schools at all levels of education from February 2020 to January 2022, the longest school closure of any country in the world.

COVID-19 mitigation measures likely impacted sexual risk behaviours but this area has been underexplored. In particular, transactional sex relationships, or relationships dependent on the implicit exchange of favours, money, and/or material goods for sex, (Mojola 2014; Stoebenau et al. 2016) may have been affected. Transactional sex relationships are distinct from commercial sex work in that material exchange is not formally negotiated and transactional sex partners may not identify as sex workers or clients. These relationships encompass a spectrum of formality (e.g. one-night stand, casual partnership, girlfriend) but do not include marital relationships, which are part of a formally recognised social institution and subject to different social and economic expectations. The vast majority of research on transactional sex in sub-Saharan Africa has focused on relationships between men and women and the gendered power imbalances which play out in these relationships and which are associated gender-based violence, inconsistent condom use, alcohol use and HIV transmission, among other risks.

Transactional sex relationships vary greatly in terms of relationship length, emotional commitment, and the nature of exchange. A study of men in Rakai, Uganda reported that approximately 13% of men's sexual relationships could be classified as transactional sex relationships prior to the COVID-19 pandemic (Nishimura 2022). These transactional sex relationships tended be shorter in duration (e.g. less than 3 months) but varied in nature, from one-off sexual encounters to relationships lasting several years. Unmarried men were more likely than married men to engage in transactional sex. Fishermen and boda boda

(motorcycle taxi) drivers were more likely than men in other occupations to engage in transactional sex. Frequent mobility and greater access to sexual partners could explain some the higher engagement in transactional sex observed among fishing and boda boda drivers compared to other occupations. Research in Uganda, South Africa and Eswatini, suggests that men who engage in transactional sex are more likely than those who do not report intimate partner violence, multiple sexual partnerships, alcohol use before sex, and relationships with higher risk partners including women who work in bars (Nishimura 2022; Dunkle et al. 2007; Miller et al. 2021).

Though research on men's motivations for engaging in transactional sex relationships is sparse, one qualitative study of men in relationships with young women in Uganda and Eswatini reported that, in both settings, the provision of money or gifts was the primary way to establish and sustain sexual relationships, regardless of whether the relationships were short-term or long-term (Pulerwitz et al. 2021). Men in the study described provision of material support as normative behaviour and an expectation of potential partners. Some men perceived their younger partners as having agency in their ability to pursue transactional sex relationships to get desired material items and did not view themselves as exploiting their transactional sex partners. However, others reported that some men sought to exploit poor and vulnerable young women through transactional sex.

Negative economic shocks, such as those associated with Uganda's COVID-19 lockdowns, may have significantly impacted the formation and continuation of transactional sex relationships. Evidence from a 2021 systematic review suggests that sexual behaviours such as transactional sex, multiple partnerships and condomless sex are sensitive to persistent negative economic shocks such as illness, drought and civil disorder (Cust et al. 2021). However, studies included in the review took place prior to COVID-19, and the overall findings do not capture local nuance or variations in the formation of continuation of transactional sex relationships in populations experiencing negative economic shocks. There is reason to believe that the impact of COVID-19 may be different from other economic shocks, as COVID-19 was accompanied by significant social impacts due to policies which limited movement (strict travel restrictions, stay-at-home orders, curfews) and opportunities for social interaction (closure of schools, social distancing policies, and size limits for gatherings). These limitations on movement may have decreased sexual risk behaviours and HIV transmission if they reduced people's ability to form and sustain sexual relationships.

There has been limited research on how COVID-19 impacted sexual risk behaviours in subSaharan Africa, and there is conflicting evidence on how the formation and continuation of transactional sex relationships may have changed during the pandemic. Two Kenyan studies reported a stark decrease in the number of transactional sex partners reported by women during the pandemic compared to pre-pandemic levels (Kavanagh et al. 2021; Richterman et al. 2022). In contrast, a study of male and female adolescents from Botswana reported more favourable attitudes towards transactional sex and three times higher engagement in transactional sex, during the pandemic but these findings were not disaggregated by gender (Sun et al. 2022). Research is needed to explore potential mechanisms behind these changes in transactional sex relationships, particularly from the perspective of men. We conducted a qualitative study to understand how COVID-19 and associated mitigation measures impacted the formation and continuation of transactional sex relationships among men in rural Uganda.

## Method

## Study setting

Rakai District is located in south-central Uganda. The district is bordered to the south by Tanzania, to the north by Masaka District, and to the east by Lake Victoria. The region is mostly rural and characterised by three main types of community: agricultural communities (generally rural and more remote), trading centres (generally along main roads with more businesses), and fishing villages alongside Lake Victoria (with more in-migrants seeking temporary/seasonal jobs in fishing and related services). In addition to economic and infrastructure differences between these community types, HIV prevalence ranges widely, from 13.7% in agrarian and trading communities to 40.1% in fishing communities (Ratmann et al. 2020).

## Recruitment and sampling

We conducted qualitative semi-structured in-depth interviews (IDIs) by phone with 26 sexually active adult men ages 18-49, residing in Rakai District, Uganda from November 2021-February 2022. Men who had participated in the longitudinal Rakai Community Cohort Study (RCCS) (Chang et al. 2016) and who had previously agreed to be recontacted for future studies were eligible for recruitment. The RCCS is administered by the Rakai Health Sciences Program (RHSP) and is a population-based cohort study which samples individuals in 41 agrarian, trading and fishing communities in and around Rakai District, Uganda. The survey collects demographic data (e.g. age, education, occupation) and detailed sexual behaviour data for up to four past-year sexual partners (e.g. partner age, transactional sex, alcohol use before sex) (Grabowski et al. 2017). To gain insight into the experiences of men with and without a self-reported history of transactional sex, we sampled 21 men who reported engaging in transactional sex between 2016 and 2018 and five men who did not. Participants were purposively sampled for variation in geographical location across Rakai District (fishing, trading, and agrarian communities), age, occupation, HIV and viral suppression status (via measures included in the RCCS).

Men residing in agrarian and trading communities were contacted via mobile phone to assess their interest in participating in a study to understand non-marital sexual partnerships. Men residing in fishing villages, who were more challenging to recruit because of unpredictable fishing schedules with limited phone access, were contacted by RCCS staff who provided a phone and private location for recruitment conversations and interviews. Two RHSP staff working with the Department of Social and Behavioral Sciences, and with extensive experience of qualitative interviewing, conducted recruitment and consent procedures and in-depth interviews over the phone. Verbal consent was provided by all participants via telephone and documented.

## Data collection

In-depth interviews were conducted via mobile phone in Luganda, were audio-recorded, and then simultaneously transcribed and translated to English. Traditionally in this setting, RHSP staff conduct interviews in-person. To limit potential COVID-19 exposure for participants and RHSP staff, we opted for phone-based IDIs which had been successfully conducted in similar qualitative studies conducted by RHSP. In a prior study involving remote data collection in Rakai, investigators documented lessons learned related to rapport building, trust, privacy and confidentiality, and challenges with the use of technology (Renosa et al. 2021). We addressed these potential issues by convening debrief sessions with interviewers after each interview and devised strategies such as asking participants during recruitment to ensure they had a private place to take the interview to ensure confidentiality and plan to have their phones charged prior to the interview. If the participant was in a noisy location, or if the participant was in a location where his responses could be overheard by others, the interviewers stopped the interview and asked the participant to resume in a private location. Interviewers introduced themselves as RHSP staff to build rapport since RHSP is a highly regarded and well-known organisation in the area. We were also able to capitalise on some of the benefits of remote data collection including faster recruitment, lower costs associated with travel, and flexible scheduling.

Interview questions regarding the impact of COVID-19 on transactional sex relationships included:

- Since the COVID-19 lockdowns in March 2020, how have your sexual relationships changed among your non-marital partners?
- Have social distancing considerations changed how you engage in sexual partnerships?
- Has the way that you provide for your non-marital sexual partner changed?

Participants were compensated 10,000 Ugandan Shillings (approximately 3 USD), the standard rate for qualitative studies conducted by RHSP. Reimbursement was provided via mobile money transfer which allowed study staff to transmit the compensation to participants using their telephone number and did not require participants to have a bank account.

Our analytic approach was informed by Braun and Clarke's six phases of thematic analysis: 1) familiarisation with the data; 2) generating initial codes; 3) searching for themes; 4) reviewing themes; 5) defining and naming themes; and 6) producing the report (Braun and Clarke 2006). Phase 1 involved a close reading of the transcripts and analytic memo-ing on potential codes. In phase 2, we generated an initial codebook using a deductive process based on the questions in the interview guide and emergent codes identified through close reading of the transcripts. The codebook was refined in an iterative process over multiple discussions with the study team. Once the final codebook had been developed, all transcripts were coded by the first author (HN). In phase 3 the codes were grouped into themes and the relationships between codes and themes were noted in analytic memos. Themes were

## Results

Below, we present respondent characteristics and describe men's accounts of the social and economic impact of Uganda's COVID-19 mitigation measures, which included policies that restricted mobility and reduced income-generating opportunities. We then discuss how financial strain and reduced mobility impacted transactional sex relationships for most men during the height of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 and 2021.

Table 1 shows the characteristics of study participants. All study participants (N=26) were men residing in agrarian (n=9), fishing (n=9), or trading (n=8) communities in and around Rakai District, Uganda. The majority of respondents were married (n=13). Most respondents reported engaging in transactional sex in both the RCCS which was collected in 2016-2018 (n=20) and in their most recent non-commercial and non-marital sexual relationship. The most common occupation was fishing (n=8).

## Economic impact of COVID-19

Men described the significant negative economic impact of COVID-19 on their livelihoods. A majority experienced job loss, decreased income, and food insecurity during the pandemic. The few respondents employed in the formal sector such as those with positions with the military or government appeared to be more financially stable while men whose primary source of income lay in the informal economy were disproportionately impacted by job loss, decreased income, and food insecurity.

…people had no money. Money became scarce during that time and many people were just struggling to eat. (Age 25-34, married, fishing community, no transactional sex relationships reported)

Uganda's economy rebounded after the country's second lockdown, yet some men had not fully recovered from the economic impact of the pandemic, although interviews took place approximately a year and a half after Uganda's first lockdown.

…we are no longer working in the same way we used to get money so things are just normalising but still we have not started to work as we used to. My income is still down.

(Age 18-24, never married, trading community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

## Influence of financial strain on transactional sex relationships

Fewer economic opportunities changed the way most men reportedly provided for their transactional sex partners. Men described being unable to maintain the frequency and amount of financial support they gave their partners prior to the pandemic. A number of fishermen in particular described how a decrease in demand for fish during the pandemic affected their financial livelihood and ability to provide for their sexual partners and this pattern appeared to be consistent across age groups and marital status.

Interviewer: Has the way that you provide for your non-marital sexual partner changed?

Participant: Yes, we have been greatly affected financially, I can't provide for her the way I used to…Things are not going well on the lake…and the people who were our customers don't have money. (Age 25-34, married, fishing community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

For many, decreased provision led to conflict in their transactional sex relationships or dissolution of the relationship. Some men reported that they ended their transactional sex relationships themselves because they could no longer provide for their partner. One participant described the direct relationship between decreased provision and relationship dissolution he witnessed among his friends.

Those who had like 3 or 4 partners, had to end (their sexual relationships) because they could not afford to take care of them since their income levels had reduced. They could no longer take care of them. Most of my friends abandoned their non-marital sexual partners in the houses they had rented for them because they could no longer pay rent for their non-marital sexual partners and provide for them. Some of them had lost their jobs. (Age 35-44, married, trading community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

Some respondents suspected that their partners ended the relationship or began a relationship with another man because of their decreased ability to provide financial support. One respondent attributed the decrease in provision and fewer opportunities to meet his partner in person to her pursuing other relationships.

A lot changed during the lockdown because my earnings were reduced a lot and I was not able to provide the way I used to. I was not even able to meet her during that time but would communicate with her by phone. COVID changed my life a lot, especially my finances. I think that also contributed to her promiscuous ways. (Age 18-24, previously married, resides in agrarian community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

While some men were unable to provide for any of their transactional sex partners, others with multiple partners strategically ended selected transactional relationships and maintained one relationship they could support financially to adapt to job loss and lower incomes. This pattern of strategically ending transactional sex relationships appeared across age groups and community types.

… before I used to have more than one partner but because of the lockdown, I was affected financially, and I couldn't manage them, and I remained with one who I am able to support financially…Before (the pandemic), I managed to come from work with a gift for my partner but now you can go to work, and you fail to get money. (Age 25-34, married, agrarian community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

During the lockdown, I dropped some fake relationships and concentrated on one who understands me… all other activities had come to a standstill and I remained only with farming. I was affected and even the way I used to provide for my partners changed, I was no longer able to provide for them in time as I used to. Due to the financial hardships, I had to stick to one partner. (Age 45-49, previously married, trading community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

Two respondents from trading and agrarian communities reportedly noticed an increase in women in their communities interested in pursuing transactional sex relationships during the lockdown. However, these men were unable to pursue relationships with these women because of their own difficult economic situations.

## Influence of restricted movement on transactional sex relationships

At the beginning of lockdown, several men described being worried about COVID-19 transmission, but this worry subsided over time. Fear of COVID-19 transmission did not appear to be a significant reason for relationship conflict or dissolution during the lockdown and was not mentioned as a reason for restricting time spent with sexual partners.

It [fear of COVID-19] affected us a lot at the start of the lockdown, but now we are no longer being affected by it. [Before], even when you went out and you returned in the evening, your partner would not even need to come close to you because she didn't know where you had been and the people you had been associating with, but now everything has returned to normal. (Age 25-34, married, fishing community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

More than fear for COVID-19 transmission, stay-at-home policies, curfews, and social distancing requirements were cited as restricting men's ability to meet with their transactional sex partners and form new transactional sex relationships. These policies, particularly the curfew from 7:00pm to 6:30am, also changed how and where men reportedly spent time with partners.

It changed in that the time you would have spent with our partner in the evening hanging out, was curfew time - you have to be at your place of residence. (Age 18-24, never married, trading community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

Strict travel restrictions prevented men from visiting partners who lived far away. While the ability to provide for one's transactional sex partner varied by the respondent's occupation, travel restrictions and curfews affected all respondents.

The only thing that changed [during the pandemic] were the times I met my partner due to the lockdown. She was a bit far and therefore I struggled to reach her since all transport means had been stopped but I would find a way to visit her like once a month. (Before the pandemic), I used to meet her twice or once a week. (Age 35-44, married, trading community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

One young man described adapting to travel restrictions by increasing phone communication with his non-transactional sex partner, but this strategy was not described by men in transactional sex relationships.

Before the lockdown, we had a lot of time to meet each other which was not the case when they introduced the movement restrictions… Since we were not able to meet the way we used to, communication through phone calls increased, and another thing was that people were home so whenever you call, you were able to talk to the person you needed to talk to. (Age 18-24, previously married, agrarian community, no transactional sex relationship reported)

A small number of respondents were able to maintain their pre-lockdown transactional sex relationships, but the frequency and amount of material support decreased, creating challenges in the relationships and sometimes relationship dissolution. One man described that he was unable to meet his transactional sex partner in person for sex due to restrictions on mobility during the lockdown, so he decreased the amount of financial support to his partner.

It changed a bit because she used to tell me to send her money through mobile money and yet I wanted to give it to her physically… I would send her money occasionally, but I would send her less than she wanted. For example, if she asked me like for 50,000 Ugandan shillings, I would send her 10,000. During the lockdown, the love she had towards me reduced a bit…I would send less because I was not meeting her in person (for sex). (Age 35-44, married, fishing community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

Several men in transactional sex relationships with partners who lived far from their homes, established new transactional sex relationships in closer proximity, who they could visit without violating travel restrictions.

Before, the lockdown I had one (non-marital sexual partner) but when the lockdown began, we could no longer meet, then I got this current non-marital partner...I got another (partner) that did not need me to travel long distances. (Age 25-34, married, fishing community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

One respondent reported refocusing on his relationship with his wife while required to stay at home during the lockdown. In contrast, fishermen, who were often migrants to fishing communities, reported staying with their transactional sex partners and being unable to visit their wives who resided in other villages due to travel restrictions.

During COVID nothing changed because my non-marital partner stayed with me at the fishing site. It only changed with my wife in the village because I could not go to the village since there were no means of transport. (Age 35-44, married, fishing community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

One fisherman shared how HIV infections in fishing villages may have decreased following the lockdowns, as fewer women migrated to fishing villages, creating fewer opportunities for sexual partnerships.

What I have seen during this time is that there have been few people coming to the fishing site, which was not the case (before). Before we used to get many women coming from different parts, some from different fishing sites, others from Kampala. So, the infections were coming from different angles. Remember here we are on the border, so people come from different angles. So, the rate of HIV infections was going down and remember most of the HIV infections happen at night and we have night curfew so now people don't move at night. (Age 25-34, married, fishing community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

## Some relationships did not change

A small proportion of men in the study reported no change in their transactional sex relationships. Men with regular employment during the lockdown and whose partners lived in the same village were able to continue pre-pandemic provision for their partners which allowed them to maintain these relationships.

I was able to meet her (transactional sex partner) because she stays in the same area that I stay in with my wife. There's no big distance so I was able to meet up with her during the lockdown…when the lockdown was announced, I was working, and I had a number of sites that I was managing so I was able to continue providing for her. (Age 45-49, married, agrarian community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

Nothing changed (during the lockdown) because we were using the money she saved from the farm… I had to lie to my wife that I was at work and therefore I had no means of reaching home and she accepted…Before lockdown, I had made some money that I saved up and it also helped because I sent that to my wife. (Age 35-44, married, fishing community, recent transactional sex relationship reported)

Non-transactional sex relationships may have been less impacted by the lockdown. These relationships appeared to be better able to adapt to reduced financial provision and decreased opportunities to meet in person. One respondent described decreasing the amount of money he provided to his non-marital partner, but decreased provision did not influence the relationship.

Of course, (the way I provide for my partner) reduced a bit because I wasn't able to give her the amount of money, I used to give her, but she understood the situation. (Age 35-44, married, trading community, no transactional sex relationships reported)

Another respondent in a non-transactional relationship, in which provision was infrequent prior to COVID lockdowns, reported no change in his relationship.

To me and my partner [having more or less money] doesn't influence our [nontransactional] relationship. I don't change when I get more or less. I behave the same way…The lockdown did not affect me just as I told you before because I was not giving her money daily. Each time she needed money, I had it. (Age 18-24, previously married, agrarian community, no transactional sex relationships reported)

According to respondents' reports, COVID-19 restrictions significantly impacted almost all respondents' ability to earn money, particularly those working in the informal economy. Due to Uganda's strict travel restrictions and curfews, respondents were universally restricted in their ability to meet sexual partners in person, and this was particularly impactful on the relationship if the respondent's partner lived farther away. Since Uganda's first lockdown, most respondents who were in one or more transactional sex relationship reported the dissolution of some or all of those relationships due to restricted movement and decreased material provision. Restricted travel and curfews also affected men's ability to meet new partners. Non-transactional relationships appeared to be more adaptable to changes in material provision and restricted movement.

## Discussion

This study adds to the body of literature on transactional sex by illustrating a clear link between men's ability to provide material support and the formation and continuation of transactional sex relationships. During the COVID-19 pandemic, men in Rakai, Uganda faced significant economic challenges including job loss and food insecurity. In this context, men's ability to provide material goods for partners decreased and many transactional sex relationships dissolved. During the pandemic, a large proportion of men and women who lost their jobs in urban and peri-urban centres returned to villages for agricultural work (World Bank 2021). However, the formation of new relationships was limited because of men's economic insecurity and diminished capacity to act as providers. In this study, men who were financially stable during the pandemic reported few changes in their transactional sex relationships. Additionally, men who were not engaged in transactional sex relationships did not report changes in their sexual relationships. Some described that provision before the pandemic was infrequent and others explained that their relationships were unaffected by decreased provision.

Although the present study was restricted to men, our findings are consistent with the wider literature on the economic and social impact of COVID-19 on transactional sex relationships generally. Two studies conducted in Kenya similarly found a stark decrease in women's reported transactional sex relationships in the initial stages of the pandemic compared to pre-pandemic reports (Kavanagh et al. 2021; Richterman et al. 2022). In the context of Uganda's COVID-19 restrictions and subsequent economic contraction, transactional sex and therefore associated sexual risk behaviours may have substantially decreased according to men's accounts. These findings fill an important gap in the literature on how persistent negative economic shocks may impact men's engagement in transactional sex (Cust et al. 2021).

## Limitations

This study has its limitations. We purposefully recruited 21 men who previously reported transactional sex relationships, given our interest in this group. Therefore, the study may not have reached saturation in terms of how the COVID-19 pandemic has impacted nontransactional sex relationships (n=5) differently than transactional relationships. However, this analysis offers a comprehensive summary of how COVID-19 mitigation measures impacted men's ability to materially provide for their transactional sex partners and draws a direct connection between provision and ability to sustain transactional sex relationships in the Ugandan context.

## Conclusions

This study provides insight into how Uganda's COVID-19 mitigation measures impacted men's transactional sex relationships through changes in their economic standing and restricted mobility. The study also sheds light on a potential pathway through which COVID-19 may have impacted transactional sex relationships and risks associated with these relationships such as intimate partner violence, alcohol use, and HIV transmission. Further research is needed to determine the extent to which transactional sex relationships may have decreased during the pandemic, the impact on associated risk behaviours, and whether these changes persisted post-lockdown.
